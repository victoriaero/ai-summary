Introduction
Patient safety is a critical global public health concern, defined as the proactive measures taken to prevent harm to patients1. It is also a routine implementation of evidence-based standards, clinical protocols, effective communication, and risk-reduction strategies by healthcare providers to minimise preventable adverse events and ensure safe patient care2. Furthermore, patient safety is integral to the overall quality of healthcare. The World Health Organization recognizes patient safety as a fundamental human right and has implemented the Global Patient Safety Action Plan for 2021–2030, which seeks to enhance patient safety on a global scale1,3. The current patient safety practices focus on risk reduction through evidence-based guidelines, systems for reporting and learning from errors, patient and family engagement, and the promotion of a strong safety culture within healthcare facilities4.
Healthcare professionals must implement standard precaution principles during every patient interaction, treating all individuals, whether patients or staff, as potentially infectious or at risk of infection. This patient safety protocol is intended for everyone utilising healthcare services5. Quality and safety are essential components in creating and maintaining health systems that are accessible, effective, and responsive6,7. To enhance patient safety practices, it is vital to provide feedback on mistakes, ensure regular incident reporting, and facilitate the exchange of patient information8.
Poor patient safety is linked to significant complications for patients and is considered one of the leading causes of death and disability4,6. Patient safety risks encompass medication errors, healthcare-associated infections, unsafe surgery, improper injections, diagnostic inaccuracies, sepsis, unsafe transfusions, radiation errors, and insufficient mental health care9. Cultivating a robust patient safety culture is fundamental to advancing safety within healthcare organisations10.
Worldwide, it is estimated that around 10% of all hospital admissions lead to unintentional harm to patients, with as many as 75% of these incidents being avoidable11. Each year, approximately 134 million adverse events take place in hospitals across low- and middle-income countries, resulting in 2.6 million deaths due to unsafe healthcare practices4. The African Region faces significant challenges, including a lack of thorough assessments and comprehensive policies aimed at enhancing patient safety and health systems. In response, various strategies have been implemented to reduce unsafe practices, such as offering training for healthcare professionals, fostering effective communication skills, ensuring adequate staffing, providing leadership support, adopting a non-punitive approach to errors, and promoting teamwork12.
In Ethiopia, the rates of positive responses regarding patient safety practices among healthcare professionals varied. For instance, a minimum of 44% reported in Addis Ababa Hospitals and a maximum of 49.2% in Western Hospitals13. Research indicates that the culture surrounding patient safety is shaped by multiple factors, such as the organisational culture, the commitment of leadership to patient safety, teamwork deficiencies, workload, staff education and training, communication among healthcare workers, patient engagement, and the processes for reporting and learning from adverse incidents14,15,16. The primary repercussions of inadequate patient safety practices can lead to increased rates of illness, disability, and death globally17,18. Although patient safety practices are recognised as essential for reducing preventable harm and improving healthcare quality, evidence regarding their magnitude and associated factors in primary hospitals, particularly in low-resource settings, remains limited19. Since there are variations in study settings, participant characteristics, sample sizes, and the contextual factors influencing patient safety practices among healthcare providers across different regions, conducting this study is essential. To the best of the investigators’ knowledge, no similar research has been conducted in this specific setting. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the magnitude of patient safety practices and associated factors among healthcare providers working in primary hospitals of the South Wollo Zone, Northeast Ethiopia.
Methods and materials
Study area and period
The research was conducted in the South Wollo Zone, located in the Amhara Region of Northeast Ethiopia. This Zone, which includes the capital city of Dessie, is one of twelve Zones within the Amhara Region. South Wollo is home to nine primary hospitals, four General Hospitals, and one Comprehensive Specialised Hospital. These facilities offer a range of services, including preventive care, treatment, and rehabilitation, catering to both inpatients and outpatients. The healthcare workforce comprises various professionals, including specialist doctors, general practitioners, nurses, midwives, paramedical staff, and laboratory technicians. The study was carried out from January 15 to February 15, 2025.
Study design
A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted at South Wollo Zone Primary Hospitals from January 15 to February 15, 2025.
Source population
The source population consisted of all healthcare professionals employed at the South Wollo Zone Primary Hospitals.
Study population and inclusion criteria
All health care providers who worked at South Wollo Zone Primary Hospitals for at least 6 months and were available during the data collection period were the study population and they were also included in the study.
Exclusion criteria
Health care providers who were on education, long-term training, and leave at the time of the survey were excluded.

Sampling procedures
In the South Wollo Zone, there are a total of 9 primary Hospitals. Samples were taken from all primary Hospitals proportional to their size using the proportional allocation formula: (ni = n/N*Ni). After we took the sampling frame from human resource lists, individual study participants were selected at each primary hospital by using simple random sampling techniques through a computer-generated random number (Fig. 1).
Data collection procedure and tool
Data were collected by using self-administered questionnaires. The questionnaires were developed in English, and then it was translated into the Amharic language to make them simple during administration. The English language was checked by English language experts. Data were collected by 6 BSc. Nurses and the supervisors were 2 public health officers. The Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) tools were adopted to collect data. HSOPC tools consists of 12 dimension and 44 items (team work with hospital unit 4 items, continues improvement 3 items, non-punitive errors 3 items, staffing 4 items, over all perception 4 items, manager expectation 4 items, communication 3 item, feedback about change 3 items, frequency of event report 3 items, patient safety grade 5 items, team across hospital 4 items and hospital handoff and transition 4 items).
Study variables
Dependent variable
Patient safety practices.
Independent variables
- 
                      Socio-demographic characteristics: Age, sex, marital status, religion, education level, salary.
- 
                      Organisational culture: This variable includes complexity of care, workload, an unorganised client education program, and a lack of an error reporting system.
- 
                      Personal-related factors: These variables encompass job satisfaction, direct contact with the patient, taking patient safety training, participation in patient safety programs, willingness to report errors, and work experience.
- 
                      Available resources and support systems: This variable represents the availability of resources and support within the healthcare organisation that facilitate the implementation of patient safety practices. It includes factors such as the presence of adequate staff, availability of necessary equipment and materials, education opportunities, presence of patient safety policies and guidelines.
Operational definitions
- 
                    A healthcare worker: It refers to an individual primarily engaged in actions intended to enhance, protect, or restore health ann it includes direct care providers like doctors, nurses, and midwives, as well as allied professionals, community health workers, laboratory technicians, and support staff4.
- 
                    Patient safety practice: It is measured by healthcare workers’ responses to the HSOPSC questionnaire, which utilises a Likert scale. The overall level of patient safety practices was determined by calculating the percentage of positive responses—those marked as “strongly agree” or “agree,” as well as “most of the time” and “always” across the 12 dimensions of patient safety culture, comprising a total of 44 items4,13.
- 
                    Good patient safety practice: score of ≥ 75% hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) questions13,20.
- 
                    Poor patient safety practice: score of < 75% hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) questions13,20.
- 
                    Workplace characteristics: It refers to a staff relationship in the workplace, and it was measured by 4 items, such as the presence of good cooperation and teamwork among staff members, having adequate staff, good communication with each other, and giving feedback to each other21.
- 
                    Job satisfaction: It was measured by using a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 signifying “very dissatisfied” and 5 denoting “very satisfied.” Participants who rated 75% or more of the items positively were categorised as ‘satisfied,’ which included those who selected ‘very satisfied,’ ‘satisfied,’ or ‘neutral.’ In contrast, those who expressed satisfaction with fewer than 75% of the items were classified as ‘unsatisfied’4,21.
- 
                    Specialist Doctor: A physician who has completed formal postgraduate medical training in a specific clinical speciality and is officially licensed as a specialist by the relevant national regulatory authority.
- 
                    General practitioner: A licensed medical doctor who has completed basic medical education and provides comprehensive primary medical care without formal postgraduate speciality training.
- 
                    Paramedical staff: Healthcare providers who provide diagnostic, therapeutic, preventive, or rehabilitative services in support of medical doctors, and who have completed formal training in health-related fields other than medicine.
Data processing and analysis
Data were entered into Epi-Data version 4.6, and it was exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed using frequencies, proportions, and summary statistics such as mean and standard deviation. Both bi-variable and multi-variable analyses were done. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.25 were entered into multivariable logistic regression to control for confounders, and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The strength of association was measured by the Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval. Multi-collinearity among candidate variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and there was no multi-collinearity among independent variables (VIF < 10). The model fitness test was checked by the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P = 0.211), and it indicates the model fits the data.
Data quality control
To ensure the quality of the data, the questionnaires were pretested on 21 (5%) of respondents at Merisa Primary Hospital before the actual data collection. One-day training was given for data collectors about the purpose of the study, the objectives of the study and the research questions to create a common understanding. Supervision and on-spot checking of the data collection procedure were done by the assigned supervisor and principal investigator, respectively. Every day, at the end of the data collection discussion was conducted with the data collectors and supervisors, and problems encountered were discussed, and timely solutions were given, which enhances the quality of the data.
Ethical considerations
Ethical clearance was obtained from the ethical committee of Wollo University, College of Medicine & Health Sciences. Then, formal permission letters were written to the respective hospitals from school of Public Health. Permission to conduct the study was obtained from all primary hospitals. A letter of informed consent and explaining the purposes of the study to respondents was attached to each questionnaire’s papers. Before administering the questionnaires for the participants, the aims and objectives of the study were clearly explained to the participants, and verbal informed consent was obtained to participate in the study voluntarily. Confidentiality and privacy were ensured throughout the process of the study. To ensure confidentiality, no personal identifiers were collected, and questionnaires were coded. Data were stored securely and accessed only by the research team.
Results
A total of 414 health professionals participated in this study with a response rate of 98.8%. The mean age and standard deviation of the participants were 37.17 ± 9.778 years, respectively. The majority, 326 (78.7%) of health professionals who participated in this study were male. In this study, 152 (36.7%) of the study participants were degree holders. More than half (61.4%) of the study participants were married. More than half, 228 (55.1%) of the study participants were Muslim by religion. In this study, 200 (48.3%) of the study participants had a monthly income of less than 7000 Ethiopian birr. On the other hand, 211 (51%) of the healthcare professionals had less than or equal to 5 years of work experience (Table 2.).
Availability of resources and organisational factors
In this study, more than half, 255 (61.6%) of health professionals reported that there was no adequate staff in the hospital. The majority of the study participants, 347 (83.8), stated that there were patient safety policies and guidelines in their working hospital. In this study, 342(82.6%) of the study participants got educational opportunities. On the other hand, 353 (85.3%) of the study participants said that there is an adequacy of logistics and materials. Nearly half, 224 (54.1%), of study participants reported that there are good work characteristics in the work area. (Table 3).
Personal-related factors
In this study, more than half, 238 (57.5%) of health professionals were dissatisfied, and only 85 (20.6%) were satisfied with their work. Of all study participants, 364 (87.9%) had direct contact with the patient. On the other hand, 322 (77.7%) of the study participants had taken patient safety training. Besides, 220 (53.1%) of the study participants were willing to report errors (Table 4).
Patient safety practice dimensions
The maximum patient safety practice was in teamwork across hospital units, 62.1% with 95% CI, (57, 66.4). On the other hand, the minimum patient safety practice was in staffing, 12.8% with 95% CI (9.7, 15.9). The overall magnitude of patient safety practice among health professionals working in primary hospitals was 30.7% with 95% CI, (26.1, 35.3) (Table 5).
Overall patient safety practice
The overall magnitude of good patient safety practice among health professionals was 30.7% (95% CI, (26.1–35.3) (Fig. 2).
Factors associated with patient safety practice among health professionals
In the bi-variable analysis, seven variables, namely age, sex, taking patient safety training, willingness to report errors, participating in a patient safety program, reporting adverse events, and workplace characteristics, were candidate variables for multivariable logistic regression at a p-value of less than 0.25. In the multivariable logistic regression, four of them, such as taking patient safety training, willingness to report errors, participating in a patient safety program, and workplace characteristics, were independently associated with patient safety practice.
Those who took patient safety training were 2.31 times more likely to have good patient safety practice as compared to those who did not take training (AOR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.43–3.75). On the other hand, those who were willing to report errors were 5.02 times more likely to have good patient safety practice as compared to those who were not willing to report errors (AOR = 5.02, 95% CI: 3.31–8.35). Those who participated in patient safety programs at least monthly were 1.76 times more likely to have good patient safety practice as compared to their counterparts (AOR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.01–3.26). On the other hand, those who had good workplace characteristics were 1.68 times more likely to have good patient safety practice as compared to those who had poor workplace characteristics (AOR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.10–2.56) (Table 6).
Discussion
In this study, the overall magnitude of good patient safety practice among healthcare professionals was 30.7% with 95% CI, (26.1–35.3). This study finding was lower as compared the studies conducted in Saudi Arabia 56.3%22, Tunisia 57.8%23, Bahir Dar City Northwest, Ethiopia 50.9%1, Bale Zone Hospitals; Southeast Ethiopia 44%3, Gamo Zone, Southern Ethiopia 50.8%19, University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia 45.3%21, Jimma Zone Primary Hospitals, Southwest Ethiopia 46.7%20, Dilla University Teaching Hospital 37%13 and a systematic review and meta- analysis conducted on Patient safety culture in public hospitals of Ethiopia (45.52%)24. In this study, the magnitude of patient safety practice varies across dimensions of patient safety measures. The maximum patient safety practice was practised in teamwork across hospital units (62.1%), and the minimum patient safety practice was done in the staffing dimension (12.8%). The discrepancy could be due to variations in Hospitals with strong safety cultures that prioritise patient safety, have strong leadership commitment, provide staff education and training, encourage open communication, have established reporting and learning systems, and ensure adequate staffing.
In this study, taking patient safety training was positively associated with good patient safety practice among healthcare professionals. Those who took patient safety training were more likely to perform good patient safety practices as compared to those who didn’t take patient safety training. This finding was in line with the study conducted in Bahir Dar City, Northwest, Ethiopia1. This could be because those who took patient safety training could have a better understanding of patient safety precautions as compared to their counterparts. It also enhances understanding of the patient’s safety principles, increases awareness of risks such as medication errors, infections, and falls in the hospital.
In this study, willingness to report errors was also positively associated with patient safety practice among health care professionals. Those who were willing to report errors were more likely to have good patient safety practices as compared to those who were not willing to report errors. This finding was also similar to the study done in South India25. When errors regarding patient safety practice are reported to the responsible body, mistakes could be corrected early, and different corrective measures would be taken.
On the other hand, participating in a patient safety program was positively associated with good patient safety practice. Those who participated in patient safety programs at least monthly were more likely to have good patient safety practices as compared to those who never participated in patient safety programs. This study finding was in line with the study conducted in Bale Zone Hospitals, Southeast Ethiopia3. This might be because those who participated in patient safety programs would have better awareness than those who didn’t participate in patient safety programs.
The other important variable that showed a positive association with good patient safety practice among health care professionals was workplace characteristics. Those who work in good characteristics were more likely to have good patient safety practices as compared to those who work in a poor workplace characteristic. This finding was similar with the study conducted in A study done in Dilla University Teaching Hospital13. The implication of poor patient safety practice would result in increased risk of adverse events, injuries, preventable deaths and reduced trust among healthcare providers and hospitals. This study used a cross-sectional design, which allowed assessment of the prevalence and associated factors of patient safety practices. However, the design limits causal inference, capturing only a snapshot in time, use of self-administered questionnaire and might be subject to recall, selection, and social desirability bias.
Conclusion
In this study, the overall magnitude of good patient safety practice among health care professionals was found to be low, indicating notable gaps in the consistent application of recommended patient safety measures in routine clinical practice. On the other hand, taking patient safety training, willingness to report errors, actively participating in patient safety programs, and favourable workplace characteristics were variables that showed a positive association with good patient safety practice among healthcare professionals.
Recommendations
To healthcare professionals
Healthcare professionals should consistently report all medical errors, near misses, and adverse events occurring within their working units through the established reporting systems to enhance learning and prevent recurrence. In addition, healthcare professionals are strongly encouraged to actively participate in organised patient safety programs, including training, safety audits, and quality improvement initiatives.
To the South Wollo Zone, Primary Hospitals
All primary hospitals should provide adequate training on patient safety practices for their health care professionals. Primary Hospitals should place great emphasis on staffing dimensions of patient safety practice.
To programmers/policy makers
Policy makers should integrate patient safety training into pre-service and in-service education for all healthcare workers (physicians, nurses, midwives, and paramedical staff).
To other researchers
Other researchers should conduct longitudinal studies to assess changes in patient safety practices over time, and interventional studies to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions, including patient safety training programs, error-reporting systems, and workplace improvement strategies.
