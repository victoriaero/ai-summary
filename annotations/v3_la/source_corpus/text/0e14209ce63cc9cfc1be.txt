Millions of Americans are losing access to food assistance as new federal restrictions on the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) take effect across the country.
According to the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (CBPP), more than 4.5 million people have lost SNAP benefits since the Republican budget law was enacted in July 2025. By April 2026, national SNAP enrollment had fallen by about 11%, marking one of the steepest declines in the program’s history.
The law expanded work requirements and made it more difficult for many people to qualify for or maintain benefits. While supporters argue the changes encourage employment and reduce federal spending, advocates warn that many people losing assistance are already working, face barriers to employment, or care for children or family members.
The cuts come as many families continue to struggle with the high cost of groceries and other essentials. Anti-hunger organizations report that food banks and community pantries are experiencing increased demand as households that lose SNAP benefits seek emergency assistance. However, advocates emphasize that charitable food programs cannot replace the scale and reliability of the federal nutrition program.
SNAP is the nation’s largest anti-hunger program, serving low-income families, children, older adults, veterans, and people with disabilities. Research has consistently shown that the program reduces food insecurity, improves health outcomes, and helps families afford basic necessities while supporting local grocery stores and economies.
The SNAP cuts are expected to disproportionately impact women, who make up the majority of adult SNAP recipients and are often the primary caregivers responsible for feeding their families. Women, particularly single mothers, Black and Latina women, and older women living on fixed incomes, already face higher rates of poverty, food insecurity, and unpaid caregiving responsibilities. “SNAP helps millions of women feed their families, especially women who are raising families on their own. Kicking them off food assistance so that billionaires can get more tax breaks is simply cruel,” said Courtney Anderson from the National Women’s Law Center.
The CBPP argues that the sharp decline in SNAP participation reflects policy changes, not a reduced need for food assistance. With grocery prices remaining high and economic pressures continuing for many households, advocates say the loss of benefits could leave millions of people at greater risk of hunger.
As states continue implementing the new federal law, anti-hunger advocates are closely monitoring enrollment declines and urging policymakers to protect access to nutrition assistance for vulnerable families.
