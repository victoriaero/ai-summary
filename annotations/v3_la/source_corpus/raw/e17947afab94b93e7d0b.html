Abstract

Homelessness disproportionately impacts sexual and gender minority (SGM) people, however, few studies have examined factors that predict homelessness among SGM adults. The present secondary analysis of a survey of SGM adults living in the Southeastern U.S. in 2016 (N = 427) assessed factors associated with report of past or current homelessness, which was endorsed by 17% of participants. Congruent with our hypotheses, past socioeconomic status (SES), current SES, mental health, and race were all significant predictors and accounted for 50.5% of variance in homelessness in this population. Discrimination and housing access difficulties did not account for a significant portion of variance over and above these factors. Factors contributing to poverty and psychological distress are needed to address housing disparities for SGM adults. Discrimination factors may have become more salient since 2016. Future research is warranted to better support SGM individuals, particularly those living in the South.

Keywords: Homelessness, housing instability, transgender, risk factors, sexual and gender minorities

Housing is a key social determinant of health (Flentje et al., 2016). In 2019 and before the COVID-19 pandemic, approximately 568,000 (0.17%) individuals in the United States were homeless (HUD AHAR Report). Sexual and gender minorities (SGMs) experience homelessness at disproportionate rates relative to cisgender and heterosexual people due to violence from family and friends, substance abuse, and mental health concerns (Clatts et al., 2005; Cusack et al., 2023; Flentje et al., 2016). These risk factors were exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic, emphasizing the need to identify evidence-based approaches to intervention for this vulnerable population (Abramovich et al., 2022).

Research exploring different risk factors for homelessness among SGM individuals using an intersectional lens has identified twelve primary factors that contribute to homelessness in this group, including poverty, mental health, substance abuse, racism, and discrimination (Fraser et al., 2019). It is noteworthy, however, that the vast majority of articles cited in this review refer to SGM youth and adolescents, raising concerns about the generalizability of the findings to SGM adults (Ecker et al., 2019). Certain factors may be more relevant later in life. For example, Ecker et al. highlight familial rejection, economic and legal issues, intersectional oppression, homophobia and transphobia, substance abuse, mental illness, and lack of access to SGM inclusive housing as possible antecedents of homelessness in SGM adults. These hypotheses have yet to be tested.

Notably, certain risk factors for homelessness may be particularly salient for SGM individuals living in the Southeastern United States. For example, the South is characterized by disproportionately low levels of household income and has the lowest level of social acceptance towards SGM individuals in the United States (Harless et al., 2019; Hasenbush et al., 2014). SGM individuals living in the South also report disproportionately high rates of psychological distress and suicidal ideation as well as low rates of seeking healthcare (Harless et al., 2019), emphasizing the importance of examining these risk factors for homelessness at the intersection of SGM and Southern identities.

The present study explores risk factors for homelessness in a sample of SGM adults in the Southeastern United States. Consistent with Fraser et al.’s (2019) model, we hypothesized that poverty, race, substance use, mental health, and discrimination would be significantly associated with past and current homelessness among SGM individuals living in the South in 2016. Consistent with Ecker et al.’s (2019) hypothesis, we predicted that housing discrimination, health discrimination, and housing difficulties would account for significant variability in homelessness above and beyond poverty, race, substance use, and mental health. Notably, data collection for the present study occurred in 2016, prior to increases in discrimination following Donald Trump’s election as well as the COVID-19 pandemic (Abramovich et al., 2022; Kuroki, 2021), raising the possibility that these factors may be increasingly salient for SGM adults today.

Methods

Participants

SGM adults living in the Central Savannah River Area in Georgia and South Carolina were invited to participate in a health needs survey (Stepleman et al., 2019). The total sample of participants from the Health Needs Survey was 436. Data from 17 participants were excluded because these individuals did not disclose housing history. All participants identified themselves as a SGM person. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 76 with a mean age of 35 (SD = 13.5). Regarding gender, participants identified as “female” (36.7%), “male” (44.7%), “transgender” (11%), “genderqueer” (4.4%), “other” (2.5%), and “intersex” (0.5%). When asked if they identify as transgender or gender non-conforming, 18.4% stated “yes.” Regarding sexual identity, 39.2% identified as “gay,” 24.1% as “lesbian,” 13.3% as “bisexual,” 8.3% as “pansexual,” 5.0% as “straight/heterosexual” (in which case they identified as transgender or gender non-conforming), 4.1% as “other,” 3.0% as “queer,” 1.6% as “questioning,” and 1.4% preferred not to answer.

Procedure

This study was determined to be exempt from review by the Institutional Review Board at the Medical College of Georgia because it was a non-clinical survey study of community members. The original survey was developed by an interdisciplinary team of researchers, some of whom identify as SGMs, with the intention of identifying health needs among SGM people living in the South. Survey questions were primarily derived from previously conducted health needs assessment surveys, which typically include measures with low item burden. A pilot was conducted with five SGM adults who gave feedback on their experience with the survey prior to study onset. Recruitment was primarily conducted at SGM friendly venues and events by researchers equipped with iPads between February and June of 2016. In addition, flyers were distributed throughout several LGBTQ + friendly venues and events including health clinics, bars and clubs, churches, social media sites, and the local Pride event. Recruitment methods involved some efforts to include people who experienced homelessness, including through treatment clinics that serve the underinsured and uninsured as well as an ad that ran in a free alternative newspaper. Snowball sampling was also encouraged, as potential participants were provided with recruitment materials to share with friends and family. Participants completed the survey on their own and took 32 minutes to complete it, on average. Upon completion of the study, participants were given the option to be entered into a drawing for a VIP ticket to the local LGBTQ Pride celebration or one of four $25 gift cards.

Measures

Homelessness.

A dichotomous variable was used to represent whether survey respondents had a history of homelessness. In the survey, participants were asked, “Are you currently homeless or have you ever been homeless or not had a permanent place to sleep at night?” Data from participants who did not respond to this item were excluded from analyses. Among the 436 remaining participants, 347 (82.8%) reported they had never been homeless and 72 (17.2%) indicated they were currently homeless or had been homeless in the past.

Past Socioeconomic Status.

The following survey question was used to evaluate past socioeconomic status (SES): “Growing up, how would you describe your household’s financial situation?” Among participants who responded to this item (n = 395), many reported they never worried about having enough money for necessities (n = 184, 46.6%), 102 (25.8%) reported they were occasionally unable to purchase basic necessities, 86 (21.8%) reported having more than enough money for necessities and for some luxuries, and 23 (5.8%) reported they were routinely unable to afford basic necessities.

Current Socioeconomic Status.

The survey question about current SES was identical to the question about past SES except the response items were in present tense. Among participants who responded to this item (n = 395), many reported they never worry about affording basic necessities (n = 182, 46.1%), 106 (26.8%) reported having more than enough money to afford necessities and some luxuries, 84 (19.3%) indicated they occasionally are unable to afford basic necessities, and 23 (5.8%) reported they are unable to afford basic necessities routinely.

Race and Ethnicity.

A dichotomous variable was created to evaluate the impact of race on homelessness for the present analyses. A total of 287 (67.2%) of participants who reported their race (n = 427) identified as White and 140 (32.8%) participants indicated they were members of a minoritized racial or ethnic group.

Substance Abuse.

A dichotomous variable was created to indicate whether the participant had experienced substance abuse. Participants reported if they were “previously diagnosed with or treated for,” “currently diagnosed with,” or “currently treated for” a series of health conditions related to substance abuse. Though distinctions between current and past diagnosis and treatment likely relate to homelessness in unique ways, a continuous measure was needed for the present analyses. We hoped to capture whether the presence of substance use may represent a significant predictor of experiencing homelessness. Among participants who responded to this item (n = 353), 45 (10.3%) reported a history of substance abuse. Participants who did not have a history of alcohol or drug abuse (n = 308, 87.3%) indicated “does not apply.”

Mental Health.

Survey respondents were asked to indicate whether they received diagnosis of or treatment for a number of mental health conditions, including “Anxiety,” “ADHD,” “Bipolar Disorder,” “Depression,” “Suicidal thoughts,” and “Suicide attempts.” Given that this is not a continuous variable, a dichotomous variable was created to indicate whether participants endorsed history of or treatment for any of these psychological conditions. Most respondents who responded to this question (n = 341) endorsed a history of diagnosed mental health conditions (n = 206, 60.4%).

Physical Health.

Survey respondents were asked to indicate whether they received diagnosis of or treatment for a number of chronic health conditions, including “Asthma,” “Cancer,” “Diabetes,” “Heart disease,” “High cholesterol,” “Hypertension,” “Obesity or overweight,” “HIV,” and “Hepatitis.” A summary score was created to reflect participants’ physical health. Of those respondents who answered this question (n = 340), 155 did not report any history of any of the included chronic health conditions (45.6%).

Access to Housing.

Participants rated the extent to which “lack of adequate and affordable housing” has been a problem for them. Most participants who responded to this question (n = 372) denied any problems with accessing housing (n = 230, 61.8%), 49 (13.2%) endorsed very slight problems with accessing housing, 46 (12.4%) reported some problems with accessing housing, and 47 (12.6%) indicating having major problems with accessing housing.

Housing Discrimination.

Participants responded “yes” or “no” to the following question about housing discrimination: “Have you ever been unfairly prevented from moving because the landlord or a realtor refused to sell or rent you a house or apartment?” Among participants who responded to this question (n = 356), 330 (92.7%) responded “no” and 26 (7.3%) responded “yes.”

Health Discrimination.

A series of questions from the Seattle LGBT Commission (2011) were used to evaluate experiences of discrimination in healthcare settings. A sample item states: “Do you feel that you have been treated poorly by a health care provider or refused health care in the past five years because of your gender identity?” Responses of yes or no were entered into a summary variable (n = 262), with higher scores reflecting more experiences of discrimination in healthcare settings. The mean score was 0.86 out of 4.00 (SD = 0.95) and internal consistency was α = 0.64.

Analytic plan

Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27. Pearson product-moment and point-biserial correlations were utilized to examine relationships between variables, particularly correlations between proposed independent variables and homelessness. Hierarchical logistic regression was then utilized to explore pathways to homelessness for those variables significant at the bivariate level. The conceptualized relationships between pathways as proposed by Fraser et al. (2019) were utilized to guide construction of blocks, such that variables most proximal to homelessness were entered first and variables most proximal to SGM identity were last: mental health, substance abuse, financial, and race variables were entered in the first block; discrimination variables were entered in the second block; housing difficulties were entered in the third block. Physical health was not entered into the logistic regression model due to non-significant bivariate correlation with homelessness.

Results

Bivariate analyses

Correlations were used to explore the relationships among all included variables. Homelessness was significantly associated with past socioeconomic status (r = − .27, p < .01), current socioeconomic status (r = − .34, p < .01), history of substance abuse (Φ = .27, p < .01), history of psychological disorders (Φ = .26, p < .01), race (Φ = .11, p = .03), experiences of housing discrimination (Φ = .22, p < .01), experiences of health discrimination (r = .28, p < .01), and difficulties accessing affordable housing (r = .31, p < .01). Nonsignificant correlations were found between homelessness and physical health (r = .03, p = .58). Variables with significant correlations were included in regression analyses.

Regression analysis

First Block.

Factors most proximal to homelessness according to Fraser et al. (2019), including socioeconomic status, race, mental health, and substance use were entered into the first block. This model accounted for 50.5% of the variability in homelessness in our sample (regression results summarized in Table 1). Among the included variables, past socioeconomic status, current socioeconomic status, mental health, and race were significant predictors of homelessness, such that increased risk of homelessness was associated with greater financial difficulty, a history of psychological disorder, and identifying with a minoritized racial/ethnic group. Substance use was not a significant predictor of homelessness.

Second Block.

Discrimination variables were added to the model in the second block, accounting for an additional 0.7% variability in homelessness. Neither housing discrimination (β = 0.08) nor health discrimination (β = 0.24) were significant predictors of homelessness after accounting for factors most proximal to homelessness.

Third Block.

The addition of housing access difficulties accounted for an additional 0.5% variability in homelessness. Difficulty accessing affordable housing was not a significant predictor of homelessness after accounting for factors most proximal to homelessness.

Discussion

The present study offers support for Fraser et al.’s (2019) model identifying relevant factors that increase risk for homelessness among SGM individuals. We expand upon the studies reviewed by Fraser et al. (2019) by evaluating the impact of these factors among SGM adults living in the Southern United States. Specifically, our results indicate lower SES, presence of psychological disorders, and minoritized race/ethnicity were all significant predictors of having experienced homelessness. These findings are congruent with existing research conducted prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (Cusack et al., 2023; Glick et al., 2019; HUD AHAR Report, 2019).

We also tested Ecker et al.’s (2019) hypothesis that discrimination and lack of access to affordable housing may be particularly relevant for predicting homelessness among SGM adults. Results from the present analyses are inconsistent with this hypothesis. Null findings may be due to the characteristics of our sample, which was not originally recruited using methods targeting people experiencing homelessness, thus limiting power to detect significant predictors, especially if they correlate significantly with more proximal variables. In the current sample, 17% of participants reported having experienced homelessness. This finding is directly consistent with the Williams Institute Homelessness Adult Report (2019), which also found that 17% of SGM adults reported experiencing homelessness. Though our study differs from other studies which actively recruit SGM adults who have experienced homelessness, analyzing data from this broader sample was intended to allow for identification of risk factors that may lead to homelessness among the subgroup of those who experience it. In addition, data for this study were collected in 2016, prior to impactful events that may have exacerbated the effect of risk factors on homelessness for SGM people as they face greater levels of legislative and social discrimination (Abramovich et al., 2022; Kuroki, 2021).

An additional limitation worth considering is the cross-sectional nature of our analysis. This method is insufficient to establish casual relationships among variables. Future research should explore risk factors for homelessness in Southern SGM adults using experimental and longitudinal designs. This may be important for enhancing our understanding of how these factors may differ across the lifespan. Future work should also include evaluation of protective factors, such as sense of community (Griffin et al., 2022).

While the current study supports Fraser et al.’s (2019) theory, more research is needed to better inform how to effectively intervene for Southern SGM people, particularly Southern SGM people of color. While evidence-based approaches to addressing relevant factors such as mental health services and poverty have begun to be identified (Whitman et al., 2022), future research is needed to identify the unique challenges that may be relevant for SGM adults living in different regions, states, and counties from an intersectional lens. Given increasing legislation and discrimination against the SGM community (Peele, 2023), this work is imminently critical.
