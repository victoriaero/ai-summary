At a time when employers routinely complain that they can’t find qualified workers, millions of potential employees continue to face an additional hurdle in getting hired: a disability. 

National Disability Employment Awareness Month is a great time to remember that overlooking those candidates isn’t just unfair (and disability discrimination is illegal). Employers may be passing up talented workers.  

A report from Accenture found that companies leading in disability inclusion generated 1.6 times more revenue, 2.6 times more net income, and twice the economic profit of other companies participating in the study. They were also 25% more likely to outperform industry peers in productivity.

Yet a substantial employment gap remains. There are 17 million working age adults with disabilities in the United States, and just 38 percent are employed, compared to 75 percent of people without disabilities. That leaves a labor pool of some 10 million individuals looking for work.

Why does such a large employment gap persist? 

Disability discrimination in hiring rarely looks like an outright refusal to hire someone because of a disability. More often, it’s embedded in processes, language, and assumptions that haven’t been examined. 

  • Job postings often include requirements that have nothing to do with the essential functions of the role. Physical requirements, travel demands, or degree requirements may screen out qualified candidates with disabilities before they ever apply. 
  • Online application platforms are frequently inaccessible to people who use screen readers, voice recognition software, or other assistive technology. Accessible technology in the workplace extends to every touchpoint in the employment lifecycle, including recruitment.
  • Standard in-person interviews in non-accessible spaces, timed assessments without accommodation options, or video interviews without captioning can all disadvantage candidates with disabilities. 
  • Finally, even well-meaning hiring managers carry assumptions about what a “capable” candidate looks like. Disability-related stereotypes — that someone with a disability will have more absences, or that a candidate with a speech difference won’t communicate well with clients — can quietly influence hiring decisions in ways that are both discriminatory and factually wrong. 

Removing those barriers doesn’t require HR professionals and hiring managers to become experts on every disability. It starts with making the hiring process accessible, focusing on the candidate’s ability to perform the essential functions of the job, and asking candidates what accommodations they may need rather than making assumptions for them.

Here are some practical guidelines employers can follow when interviewing candidates with different types of disabilities.

Deaf or Hard-of-Hearing Candidates

Ask the candidate how they would prefer to communicate — whether they use an interpreter, read lips, or prefer written questions and answers. If using an interpreter, speak to the candidate, not the interpreter. If the candidate reads lips, then be sure to look directly at the candidate. And do not raise your voice.

Blind or Low-Vision Candidates

Introduce yourself with your name and title and describe your appearance and how the interview setting is laid out.  When offering seating, offer to place the person’s hand on the back or arm of the chair.  To initiate a handshake, say something like, “May I shake your hand?” Ask what assistance, if any, the candidate would find helpful. Remember that blind and low-vision candidates cannot “read” body language or facial expressions.

Candidates with Speech Disabilities

People who stutter or have other speech disabilities often just need a bit more time to form and express answers to questions. You can make the process easier if you ask the candidate if they would prefer to use writing materials such as a whiteboard to write responses to questions. Other tips include:

  • Ask questions that can be answered with short responses.
  • Give them your total attention.
  • Do not complete their thoughts for them or suggest answers.
  • Do not pretend to understand if you are having difficulty doing so.
  • Speak with a normal tone of voice.

Candidates with Mobility Disabilities

Some wheelchair users may prefer to transfer from their wheelchair to a chair for the interview. In addition, some wheelchair users consider a wheelchair to be part of their body space, so don’t lean against it or move it without permission. Sit down so you can be at eye level with the candidate and allow people with canes or crutches to keep them within easy reach.

Neurodivergent Candidates

Neurodiversity is a broad category of people with developmental, mental health, and intellectual disabilities like Down Syndrome, autism, or PTSD. Communication skills can vary widely in this population, and it is important to remember that soft skills like small talk, eye contact, and humor are not necessarily job qualifications. 

In addition: 

  • Offer a quiet interview area for individuals who may be highly sensitive to noise.
  • Keep in mind that communication is not the same for all individuals.
  • Eye contact can be difficult for neurodivergent individuals, so do not expect it. If the individual is not making eye contact, do not stare into their eyes or otherwise try to force them to look into yours.
  • Handshakes can be awkward and are often not extended or welcomed.
  • Do not assume reading capabilities or that they have read information about a position.
  • Do not complete their thoughts for them or suggest answers.
  • Use plain, literal language as much as possible. Metaphors and analogies may not be familiar or understood.

Ultimately, creating a more inclusive hiring process doesn’t mean lowering standards or giving candidates with disabilities special treatment. It means removing unnecessary barriers so every applicant has a fair opportunity to demonstrate what they can do.

For employers struggling to find qualified workers, that’s more than a matter of inclusion. By taking a fresh look at job requirements, application systems, interview practices, and their own assumptions, employers can see the skills candidates have to offer, not the disability, and discover some of the capable workers they’ve been looking for have been there all along.
