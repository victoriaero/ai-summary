ABSTRACT

Aim

To explore men's health status and lifestyle risk profile and understand how they engage with preventive health care.

Design

A cross‐sectional survey within a sequential mixed‐methods project.

Methods

Four hundred thirty‐one adult males, working or volunteering for the New South Wales Rural Fire Service (NSW RFS) completed the survey between September and November 2022. The survey captured demographic data, health status and lifestyle characteristics, as well as engagement with preventive health care.

Results

Nearly three‐quarters of respondents (n = 314; 72.8%) described themselves as being in good or very good health. Just 18.6% of respondents recorded a ‘healthy’ body mass index (BMI), despite only 29.9% having been told by a doctor that they were overweight/obese. Most (n = 344; 79.8%) respondents identified having a regular general practitioner (GP)/general practice. Nearly all respondents described having had blood pressure measurements (n = 403; 93.5%) and lipid profile (n = 346, 80.3%) in the last 2 years. Having a regular GP/general practice was significantly associated with engaging in all preventive and screening activities, except having a dental check.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate a significant opportunity to support men to reduce lifestyle risk, despite their current engagement with general practice. Strategies need to support men and health professionals to have conversations about risk and risk reduction to promote behaviour change. Nurses are well placed to provide preventive health care to men in general practice. The general practice nurse has a key role in communicating lifestyle risk, supporting patients in modifying their behaviours and reducing the impact of such factors on their health and well‐being.

Implications for the Profession and/or Patient Care

Communicating the importance of lifestyle risk factors is imperative in supporting men to achieve behavioural change in the reduction in lifestyle risk. Nurses are well‐placed to take a leading role in this area.

Reporting Method

The STROBE checklist guided reporting.

Patient or Public Contribution

Survey development was undertaken in collaboration with members of the NSW RFS. Key contacts within the organisation were involved in reviewing the analysis and interpretation of findings.

Keywords: chronic disease, healthcare engagement, lifestyle modification, men, preventive care

1. Introduction

Health outcomes and disease prevalence are impacted by gender (Mauvais‐Jarvis et al. 2020). Males and females are inherently different both biologically and socially, and while a person's sex is a function of biology, gender are social norms and constructs along which the individual identifies (Hossin 2021). While sex can impact disease risk, progression and outcomes, gender impacts the vulnerability and susceptibility to illness and health risks, as well as impacting access and engagement with health care (World Health Organization 2021). In most countries, males consistently have a lower life expectancy than females (Worldometer 2023). In Australia, while the current life expectancy for females is 85.4 years, it is only 81.3 years for males (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023a). The main causes of death for Australian men are coronary heart disease, dementia, lung cancer, cerebrovascular disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023a). Preventive health care and lifestyle risk modification could reduce the risk of developing many of these conditions.

Three in five Australian males rate their health as being very good or excellent (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023b). Despite this, nearly half (49%) have at least one chronic condition (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023b). Chronic health conditions result in poor health, disability and reduced quality of life (Australian Government The Treasury 2023). The World Health Organization (2023) reports that some 74% of all deaths globally each year are attributed to chronic conditions. Chronic conditions, including many cancers, diabetes, kidney disease and cardiovascular diseases, are inherently linked to common modifiable lifestyle risk factors (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023b).

Around 40% of ill health and premature death in males is potentially preventable through the reduction in modifiable risk factors (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023b). Some 75% of Australian males are overweight or obese (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023b). Other modifiable lifestyle risk factors are also common among males including smoking, poor diet, excessive alcohol consumption and being physically inactive (Fetherston and Craike 2020; World Health Organization 2023).

2. Background

Effective preventive care reduces preventable morbidity and mortality by reducing the incidence of chronic conditions and, subsequently, enhances the quality of life and health outcomes (AbdulRaheem 2023). Delaying seeking health advice for a concern or worrying symptoms can impact early diagnosis, delaying the illness trajectory and worsening prognosis (Yousaf, Grunfeld, and Hunter 2015). General practice is often the first point of contact that a person has with the health system (The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners 2017). Contemporary general practices are staffed by multidisciplinary health professionals, including doctors and nurses, with many practices collaborating with either co‐located or local allied health professionals (The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners 2017). The general practice team supports individuals and their families to maintain their physical and mental well‐being, as well as prevent future ill health through proactive screening and risk reduction programs (Swerissen, Duckett, and Moran 2018). Nurses, in particular, have an important role to play in screening, preventive health care and lifestyle risk modification (James et al. 2021).

In Australia, males are seen to be less proactive than females in seeking health advice from their general practice (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2022–2023). While it is reported that men do not appreciate the role of general practice in preventive care and health advice (Ashley et al. 2020), a recent integrative review identified a gap in the knowledge of the impact of gender on the effective utilisation of general practice (Mursa, Patterson, and Halcomb 2022). This paper contributes knowledge to this gap, reporting on a survey undertaken as part of a mixed methods study exploring Australian men's help‐seeking and engagement with general practice.

3. Methods

This paper seeks to explore men's health status and lifestyle risk profile and understand how they engage with preventive health care. Such new knowledge will inform how general practice nurses (GPNs) can develop their role in preventive health to improve access to such services. The STROBE checklist was used to guide reporting (Data S1).

3.1. Study Design

This paper reports a cross‐sectional online survey conducted as the first phase of a sequential mixed‐methods study. The study consisted of an initial quantitative survey (Phase 1) to gather descriptive data, followed by qualitative interviews (Phase 2) to more deeply explain and explore the survey data (Halcomb, Massey, and Oozageer Gunowa 2023). The survey was undertaken between September and November 2022. Given the large volume of data and somewhat disparate foci, survey data about health literacy (Mursa et al. 2024) and the interview data are reported elsewhere.

3.2. Respondents

This study sought to recruit Australian males aged 18 years and over, from diverse socioeconomic and educational backgrounds, and geographical distribution across metropolitan and regional/rural areas. To achieve this diversity in a readily accessible group of men, researchers partnered with the New South Wales Rural Fire Service (NSW RFS). The NSW RFS is the world's largest volunteer fire service (NSW Rural Fire Service 2022). It comprises a diverse mix of volunteers and paid employees who are engaged in various activities from active emergency response (e.g., firefighting, car accidents, storm damage/floods), to hazard reduction and operational firefighting support and office‐based roles that provide training, emergency planning, support and community education (NSW Rural Fire Service 2022). People were eligible to participate if they were an NSW RFS volunteer or employee who identified as male.

3.3. Survey Tool

The survey tool was designed following a review of the literature and expert input. A combination of investigator‐developed items and a validated tool were contained within three sections. The survey commenced with items asking about respondent demographics, including age, ethnicity, residential location, relationship status and educational background. In the second section, respondents provided information about their health status (e.g., height, weight, self‐rated health and medical history), the presence of lifestyle risk factors (e.g., smoking, diet, alcohol intake and physical activity) and engagement with general practice and health screening. Items within Sections 1 and 2 were developed by the research team based on standardised items and previous literature (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2022; The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners 2015, 2018). Items combined dichotomous yes/no questions with Likert scales to demonstrate the frequency or extent of risk/engagement. Section 3 comprised the Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ) (Osborne et al. 2013). The HLQ consists of 44 items across nine domains, each of which measures an aspect of health literacy (Swinburne University of Technology 2022). The HLQ has been widely used in health research and has good internal consistency with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.8 (Osborne et al. 2013). Given the volume of data and the distinct focus of the HLQ on health literacy, full information about this scale and these data are reported separately (Mursa et al. 2024).

Before widespread dissemination, the face and content validity of the survey tool were established by pilot testing. This is considered sufficient validation for a descriptive survey tool (Jackson, Halcomb, and Walthall 2023). The draft survey tool was reviewed by three community‐based men, three NSW RFS staff and three experienced nurse researchers. Feedback during this pilot testing resulted in minor amendments to the wording and format of the survey tool, as well as the sequencing of questions.

3.4. Data Collection

The NSW RFS Facebook page and e‐bulletin were used to disseminate information about the study and a link to the REDCap survey platform (Harris et al. 2019). During the 10 weeks of respondent recruitment, key contacts within the NSW RFS provided regular reminders, and additional social media posts were made to optimise the response rate (Shiyab et al. 2023). A prize draw was also offered as an incentive for survey completion, with four AUD$100 gift vouchers provided to randomly selected respondents. As this was a descriptive survey it was not possible to calculate a desired sample size using a power calculation, and so recruitment was undertaken to gather the most responses possible within the study period.

3.5. Data Analysis

Data were exported into IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (Version 28.0) from REDCap for analysis. A process of data cleaning was undertaken, and missing values were identified. Responses missing >50% of the survey items or those from the same respondent were excluded. Where responses were missing <50% of the survey items, they were included, and incomplete data were labelled as missing.

The sample was described using a combination of frequencies, percentages, means and standard deviations. Body mass index was calculated from the height and weight provided by each respondent (The Heart Foundation 2022). In the absence of more physical measures, the calculated BMI was used as a proxy for body size (The Heart Foundation 2022). Where this is used to refer to body size the use of calculated BMI is noted in the results. Fruit and vegetable intake and physical activity were dichotomised based on whether responses met current guidelines (The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners 2015). Pearsons' chi‐squared test was used to examine the association between categorical variables (Bennett, Heritage, and Allen 2023). A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3.6. Ethical Considerations

Before data collection, ethical approval was granted by The University of Wollongong Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval no 2022/225), and the NSW RFS gave permission to undertake the research. Consent was sought at the beginning of the survey and respondents could choose to cease the survey at any time.

4. Results

4.1. Respondent Characteristics

Of the 505 responses, 74 (14.7%) were excluded as greater than 50% of survey data were missing or they were duplicate responses. This left 431 (85.3%) responses included in the analysis (Table 1). Most respondents (n = 327; 75.9%) were NSW RFS volunteers, with 16.2% (n = 70) both volunteers and employees. Just over half of respondents (n = 228; 52.9%) resided in rural areas. The majority of respondents (n = 345; 80.0%) lived with a partner. Respondents' ages ranged from 18 to 90 years (SD 16; Mean 52.7 years). Most respondents' (n = 284; 65.9%) household income was between AUD$45 K and $180 K and over two‐thirds of respondents (n = 309; 71.7%) held private health insurance.

4.2. Health

Nearly three‐quarters of respondents (n = 314; 72.8%) described themselves as being in good (n = 185, 42.9%) or very good (n = 129; 29.9%) health (Figure 1). Self‐rated health was not significantly related to either respondents' age (p = 0.430), location (p = 0.152), relationship status (p = 0.478), education (p = 0.557) or income (p = 0.218).

The most common diagnosed health conditions were high blood pressure (n = 140, 32.5%), overweight/obesity (n = 129; 29.9%), mental health issues (n = 113, 26.2%) and high cholesterol (n = 101, 23.4%) (Table 2).

4.3. Lifestyle Risk Factors

The mean calculated body mass index was 29.8 (Range 18.01–59.40; SD 6.05). While some 29.9% (n = 129) of respondents identified having had a diagnosis of overweight/obesity from their doctor (Table 2), 77.9% (n = 336) respondents were considered overweight/obese based on their calculated BMI (The Heart Foundation 2022). Indeed, only 18.6% (n = 80) of respondents were within the healthy body mass index range based on their calculated BMI (The Heart Foundation 2022).

Few respondents reported smoking. Only 8.4% (n = 36) of respondents currently smoked tobacco and 3.3% (n = 14) currently smoked vapes/e‐cigarettes. While 26.9% (n = 116) reported having given up smoking previously, over half of respondents (n = 279; 64.7%) reported having never smoked.

Only 11.1% (n = 48) of respondents reported eating the recommended five servings of vegetables per day, with some 52.7% (n = 227) respondents consuming the recommended daily fruit intake of at least two serves (The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners 2015) (Table 3).

Few respondents (n = 47;10.9%) abstained from alcohol, with just under half of respondents (n = 208; 48.2%) consuming alcohol at least two–three times per week. While most respondents (n = 313; 81.9%) consumed <4 drinks on a typical day, 21.8% (n = 94) consumed >6 drinks on one occasion at least monthly.

4.4. General Practice Engagement

Over three‐quarters of respondents (n = 344; 79.8%) had a regular GP/general practice. Some 42.9% (n = 185) of respondents attended their general practice 2–4 times per year, and 22.7% (n = 98) attended at least annually.

There was a significant association between having a regular GP/general practice and both age (p ≤ 0.001) and income (p = 0.028) (Pearson chi‐squared test) (Table 4). Older men (>65 years vs. ≤39 years) and those on lower incomes (≤$45,000 vs. >$180,000) were more likely to have a regular GP/general practice. While those with a partner were somewhat more likely to have a regular GP/general practice, this did not reach significance (p = 0.058). There was no difference between having a regular GP/general practice and living in a rural or urban location (p = 0.791) or self‐rated health (p = 0.464).

4.5. Engagement With Preventive Health

Most respondents reported having undertaken some health screening/preventive health care in the last 2 years. The most common of these were blood pressure measurements (n = 403; 93.5%) and lipid profile (n = 346, 80.3%). Some 72.4% (n = 312) reported having had a fasting blood sugar test and just over two‐thirds of respondents (n = 293; 68.0%) had undergone a dental check‐up. Just over half of respondents reported having had a skin check (n = 234; 54.3%) and a healthy heart check (n = 218; 50.6%). The uptake of influenza vaccination was consistent across both 2021 (n = 264; 61.3%) and 2022 (n = 276; 64.0%).

Having a regular GP/general practice was significantly associated with all preventive and screening activities, except having a dental check (p = 0.174) (Table 4).

5. Discussion

This study has explored the health status and lifestyle risk profile of a cohort of Australian men. Additionally, it provides new knowledge of how they manage their health and engage with preventive health care. Being able to consider both the risk profiles and engagement with preventive care in the same cohort provides a unique insight into health behaviours and factors that may impact healthy lifestyles.

This cohort included a level of diversity in socioeconomic, educational and professional characteristics. Respondents worked across various roles from on‐call active firefighters with diverse full‐time work roles to full‐time office‐based staff who may or may not contribute to active emergency firefighting. While there may be a public perception of good health among emergency services, cardiovascular incidents are reported to account for almost half of the firefighter duty‐related deaths (Storer et al. 2014). Indeed, Chizewski et al. (2021) identified a mismatch in the physical demands of firefighting with current physical fitness levels of career and voluntary firefighters and that heightens their cardiovascular risk. As such, the findings of this study provide an important insight into both Australian men in general, and this specific occupational group.

Most respondents in this study reported having a regular GP/general practice. This is consistent with broader Australian data which reports that some 79% of Australians have a regular primary care provider (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2022–2023). Having a regular GP/general practice supports continuity of care and healthcare coordination (Wright and Mainous 2018). Findings from this study demonstrate that having a regular GP/general practice is also a significant factor in accessing regular preventive care. This in part may be attributed to the established relationships with GPs and GPNs that support the provision of holistic long‐term care, including preventive health, education, support and advice, as well as addressing acute care needs as they arise (James et al. 2021; Wright and Mainous 2018). In recognition of the value of continuity of care, the Australian Government has just commenced trials of voluntary patient registration with their general practice. This programme seeks to formalise the relationship between patients with their general practice to support continuity of care, and strengthen health outcomes (Department of Health and Aged Care 2022). While such a programme has been relatively successful internationally (Marchildon et al. 2021), its impact on the Australian healthcare system and patient outcomes is yet to be evaluated.

Similar to the findings in the broader Australian population (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023b), just over three‐quarters of respondents in this study were overweight/obese based on BMI calculations from self‐reported height and weight data. While BMI is not the most accurate measure of overweight/obesity, it is widely recognised as a suitable proxy (Gutin 2018). However, just 29.9% of respondents reported that they were advised by their GP that they were overweight/obese and most considered themselves to be in good or very good health. This is concerning given the number of respondents who had a regular GP/practice and engaged in preventive care. Lifestyle modification advice provided by health professionals, including nurses, doctors and pharmacists, has been shown to positively influence behavioural change (Treciokiene et al. 2021). Indeed, a key role of general practice nurses is to provide health screening and promote risk reduction (James et al. 2021). However, before lifestyle advice can be provided healthcare professionals must open the dialogue about lifestyle risk and identify risk factors. While barriers such as time, provider confidence and prioritisation of acute problems challenge general practice clinicians to engage in lifestyle communication (James et al. 2021), opening the conversation is an important step in assessing the level of risk, health literacy and readiness to change. To slow the significant growth in chronic conditions within the community it is vital that risks are identified and discussed. Study findings highlight a gap in service provision that could be addressed by nurses taking a more proactive role in preventive care. Data from this study provide a strong consumer voice to guide such intervention.

The high rate of overweight/obesity seen in this study was perhaps unsurprising given the low proportion of respondents meeting guidelines related to physical activity and diet. The benefits of maintaining a healthy lifestyle are without doubt (Glechner et al. 2018), and diet and exercise are key components. However, people often do not recognise aspects of their lifestyle that impact their health (Páscoa et al. 2021). For example, Smith et al. (2023) found that more than one‐third of men do not recognise the importance of weight management, physical activity, and the daily consumption of fruits and vegetables and more than 50% of men do not place importance on limiting alcohol consumption. Our findings highlight that more needs to be done to support men in understanding the importance of lifestyle factors and encourage them to achieve lifestyle changes to reduce risk (Páscoa et al. 2021). While guidelines for healthy lifestyles exist (The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners 2015), there is a need to translate these into the community and support interventions to achieve lifestyle change. There is clear scope to build the role of nurses within general practice to opportunistically engage patients about lifestyle risk and modification (Halcomb et al. 2021). However, more needs to be done to prioritise the essential delivery of preventive care by nurses within general practice (James et al. 2021). Further research to explore the avenues to support preventive healthcare engagement and ways to enhance the translation of guidelines into practice is required to improve health and stem the impact of modifiable lifestyle risks.

5.1. Strengths and Limitations

A significant strength of this study is the diverse sample of men across the adult lifespan with varying educational levels, demographic characteristics and geographical dispersion across rural and urban areas. However, there were several limitations. Firstly, the descriptive cross‐sectional design does not determine the causality. In addition, as purposive sampling was used in recruiting participants there is an inherent degree of research bias, and the sample may not be representative of the wider community. In particular, those of diverse ethnic and cultural backgrounds were not well‐represented. Therefore, care should be taken in generalising the findings. However, the use of the NSW RFS provided a degree of diversity in health, education, socioeconomic background and rurality than would have otherwise been possible. Finally, as the survey was completed online there is also a potential of self‐reporting bias.

5.2. Recommendations for Further Research

Further research testing interventions to raise awareness of lifestyle risk and support behaviour change are needed. Given the significant work already undertaken in this area, future research needs to consider the translation of evidence‐based strategies into practice and the focus of work on specific high‐risk population groups. Such groups include those working in occupations such as firefighters, who face workplace challenges that potentially impact their health and well‐being on a daily basis, and men, who are at high risk for chronic conditions. Additionally, research needs to focus on the development and testing of preventive health interventions delivered by general practice nurses to improve health outcomes and lifestyle risk.

6. Conclusion

By exploring men's self‐reported measures of health and health behaviours, our findings have enhanced the understanding of their health status and engagement with general practice for preventive care. To improve men's health outcomes, need to implement deliberate strategies for opportunistic intervention and community engagement within general practice. Providing screening to identify modifiable risk factors and health education to highlight their importance is vital in improving the health‐related practices of men and their health outcomes.

7. Relevance to Clinical Practice

Men who live with poor diet, insufficient exercise and high alcohol consumption are at risk of premature morbidity and mortality. The development of chronic conditions also represents a loss in productive life years and high health system costs. This study has demonstrated that although men are attending general practice and guidelines are in place to support healthcare delivery, there is a mismatch in translation into practice, negatively impacting health and well‐being. It is vital that GPNs and GPs communicate the impact of lifestyle risks and promote health through support, education and preventive healthcare delivery.
