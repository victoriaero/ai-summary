Challenges of achieving financial inclusion for individuals with visual impairments

Abstract

This research paper evaluates the success of the Financial Inclusion Strategy announced by the Palestinian Monetary Authority (PMA) (2018–2025) in achieving financial inclusion for the visually impaired in Palestine as a poor and marginalized group within the strategy's purview. Although the promotion of financial inclusion is widely acknowledged as a crucial means to achieve economic development objectives, there remains a gap in national research concerning the willingness of banks in Palestine to develop their services to cover individuals with visual disabilities. This research aims to explain the reasons behind the financial exclusion of this group and highlight the areas where support is needed to overcome these reasons. A free discussion was conducted with representatives of all banks operating in Palestine to conclude their satisfaction regarding the financial inclusion of visually impaired individuals and to gain insight into their perspectives on the challenges they face for this inclusion. The collected data were systematically tabulated. Similar responses were consolidated under each question, and discrepancies between the symbols were emphasized. The commonalities and variances in respondents' answers were scrutinized, categorized, and juxtaposed with findings from analogous research conducted in other jurisdictions. The research revealed that insufficient financial knowledge, a lack of adaptive technologies, communication barriers, and elevated rates of poverty and unemployment account for the hesitancy of commercial banks to extend financial services to individuals with disabilities. The research concluded that commercial banks are not interested in attracting this group, so the Monetary Authority is recommended to legislate a checklist of specific obligations that consider these challenges for banks operating in Palestine to ensure financial inclusion for this group.

Keywords: Financial inclusion, Financial exclusion, Visually impaired, Disability, Palestine

1. Introduction

Poverty is a significant challenge in Palestinian society, affecting various groups of the population.; it is a condition where individuals do not have enough financial resources to cover their basic needs and maintain a decent standard of living. The World Bank defines poverty as living on less than US$1.9 per day [1]. The population of the Occupied Palestinian Territories in 2023 is 4.48 million, and almost one-third of the population (29.2 %) is struggling with poverty (The Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics, PCBS 2023). Recent estimates from the United Nations Development Programme and ESCWA reported that the percentage of people living in poverty is expected to reach 39 % by the end of 2023 due to the ongoing war in the Gaza Strip.

Numerous studies showed a significant relationship between poverty and disability [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. For example, visually impaired persons (VI) often live in difficult circumstances, and this issue has gained significant attention in discussions about social justice and equal access to public resources. The tenth goal of the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (UN, 2015) encourages nations to prioritize inclusive development and ensure that everyone can participate in economic activities to overcome the challenges of poverty marginalized groups face [4]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 90 % of VI have low incomes [10]. In Palestine, there is a lack of specific statistical data linking disability to the extent of poverty. However, data related to people with disabilities below mentioned supports this conclusion.

Developing countries are pushing for financial inclusion in their economies due to its potential benefits. It has been a significant policy objective for the governments of numerous developing and emerging nations, anticipating that it will integrate the excluded population into the formal financial sector, thereby providing them access to formal financial products and services [11]. Several literatures reported the positive relationship between livelihood indicators and financial inclusion in developing countries [1,[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Financial inclusion is linked to reduced poverty, decreased income inequality, and improved livelihoods for individuals in various countries [6]. According to the World Bank Report 2014, developing economies' financial systems provide low-cost, customized, and easily accessible financial services to meet the needs of low-income and disadvantaged groups [16]. It also enables individuals to escape poverty by generating income, mobilizing savings, and accumulating wealth through livelihood activities [17]. It enables efficient and secure financial transactions, broadening investment possibilities, facilitating savings, and better risk management [16,18].

Financial inclusion has become important in Palestine's development and reform policy. The Palestine Monetary Authority (PMA) joined the Alliance for Financial Inclusion in 2010 (AFI), which is dedicated to advancing financial inclusion through global insights. After the Maya Declaration introduced during the 2011 AFI Global Policy Forum hosted in Riviera Maya, Mexico, by the members of the AFI, the PMA, in collaboration with the Palestinian Capital Markets Authority, committed to developing a National Financial Inclusion Strategy for the period 2018–2025 (NFIS).1 This strategy was formulated based on a national survey on financial inclusion in Palestine conducted by the PMA in 2016, which revealed that 40 % of the adult population is financially excluded. The study highlighted the lack of financial products for certain groups, such as low-income and less-educated women, people with disabilities, small-scale farmers, and small entrepreneurs, as a significant limitation to financial inclusion. The Strategy assists the PMA in achieving its primary objective of ensuring economic and monetary stability. This can be done by increasing the number of saving, investment, and consumption decisions made within the formal financial sector and reducing cash management costs. A key objective outlined in this strategy to enhance financial inclusion in the Palestinian territories is to enhance access to and utilization of formal financial products and services among marginalized groups such as women and individuals with disabilities. This approach is in consonance with the global approach, which suggests that the primary concern regarding financial inclusion is whether efforts should be directed toward individuals who have never been included in the formal financial sector or have been relatively infrequent users of formal financial products [11].

To achieve financial inclusion for people with disabilities per the objectives of the 2017 Strategy, the PMA issued “Resolution No. 8 of 2017 Related to the Appropriateness of Banks Buildings and Services to Meet the Needs of Disables”.2 The Resolution aims to protect their rights as banking and financial services consumers, thereby improving their financial inclusion rates. The Resolution provides better banking services to the needs of people with disabilities, enabling them to manage their financial services independently (Article 2). To achieve this, the resolution mandates banks to consider the needs of people with disabilities when accessing bank branches and using banking services independently. Specifically, Articles 5 and 6 of the resolution address the needs of VI individuals. Banks are now required to provide account opening forms in Braille, and an ATM machine in every city is equipped with Braille-inscribed keys and offers Arabic language support.

The success of this strategy requires identifying obstacles to financial inclusion for marginalized groups and developing appropriate solutions to address them. The difficulties that prevent VI individuals from accessing banking services in Palestine have not been thoroughly examined. Some national literature examined the difficulties of financial inclusion in general [2]; they have neglected to address the specific needs of marginalized groups, including VI individuals. As a result, these studies did not investigate the necessary strategies and measures to eliminate financial exclusion for these groups. The significance of this research is grounded in the importance of managing personal finances as a crucial component of independence, empowerment, and daily responsibility for VI individuals who require effective financial management to sustain independent living, boost self-confidence, and enhance self-esteem [2].

This research aims to scrutinize the challenges VI individuals face while interacting with Palestinian banks and the challenges that prevent them from opening bank accounts independently through conducting a free discussion with a representative from each bank operating in Palestine. The study also aims to identify the key variables that affect the accessibility of digital banking services, along with any factors that mediate or moderate this accessibility. Despite recognizing the importance of other financial services such as insurance, pensions, and remittances [19], financial inclusion indexes have mainly focused on the banking system, so this research paper will concentrate on the banking sector. It also focuses on the classification of blindness and aims to gain insights from individuals with visual impairments about the accessibility of banking services.

2. Background

Visual impairment refers to a range of conditions that affect one's ability to see, including partial and complete blindness, despite corrective measures. The International Classification of Diseases has classified visual functions into four levels: normal vision, moderate visual impairment, severe visual impairment, and blindness. People with moderate and severe visual impairments are categorized under the umbrella term of visual impairment [20]. The statistical authorities in Palestine have committed noteworthy to collecting data on this party, representing individuals with visual disabilities. The 2017 general census of population and establishments, which follows the World Health Organization's classification for measuring disability difficulties, showed that 255,224 people (5.8 % of the population) in Palestine have some form of disability. The data also showed that 115,634 females (5.3 %) and 139,590 males (6.2 %) experience varying degrees of difficulty. According to the 2017 general population census issued by the PCBS in 2019, the number of VI individuals in Palestine was 28,950. (0.6 %), 16 % are under 18, leaving 84 % eligible to conduct banking transactions (PCBS 2023).

National statistics also show that 37.6 % of VI individuals never enrolled in education, while 33.8 % dropped out. Notably, 53.1 % of them are reported as being illiterate, while the illiteracy rate in Palestine, in general, is the lowest in the world, with an average of 2.2 % (PCBS 2022). These numbers are not surprising; they align with the findings of another research conducted in another developing country in 2019, which showed that sixty percent of VI individuals interviewed reported being illiterate [21]. These low percentages were justified because the nearby schools lacked educational services and resources tailored to the student's needs. Many of these students came of age before university education was promoted. “Financial literacy theory of financial inclusion” argues that improved financial literacy encourages people to engage in the formal financial sector. This theory suggests that promoting financial literacy through education can encourage financial inclusion. As people gain financial literacy, they are more likely to seek formal financial services [11]. This theory is crucial in increasing awareness of available formal financial services. It encourages individuals to open formal accounts and become a part of the formal financial sector. When people are informed about the formal financial services that can improve their well-being, they are more likely to engage with the formal financial sector. Moreover, improved financial literacy enables individuals to use other advantages the formal financial sector offers, like investment and mortgage products. Financial literacy also helps promote personal self-sufficiency and financial stability by distinguishing between needs and wants, creating and managing budgets, saving money to pay bills on time, and planning for retirement. Lastly, promoting financial literacy can be an effective national strategy for financial inclusion for governments facing constraints on public funds or tax revenue to support financial inclusion programs. Educating the population about financial management and the benefits of formal financial services is a relatively cost-effective approach [11].

Regarding employment rates in Palestine, 87.3 % of VI individuals are unemployed (PCBS 2023). According to the Palestinian National Information Center, only 1300 people with disabilities have been employed by the public sector in the past 30 years, with a focus on those with mobility disabilities. According to a report by the International Labour Organization in 2016, people with disabilities in the Asia-Pacific region face unemployment rates ranging from 50 % to 90 % [[22], [26]]. The difficulty in securing employment for individuals with disabilities often arises from a combination of factors, including inadequate transportation, inaccessible workplaces, subpar educational services, and societal stigma [7].

Regarding the legislative perspective, the Palestinian Authority ratified the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities on April 1, 2014, without any reservations on its articles. The Palestinian Basic Law was amended, including Article 9, which declares that Palestinians are equal before the law and judiciary and no discrimination is allowed based on gender, color, religion, political opinion, or disability. Law No. 4 of 1999 and its 2004 executive regulations cover the rights of persons with disabilities, emphasizing their entitlement to lead a free life, enjoy a decent living, and access various services on equal grounds with other citizens. They have the same rights and duties in accordance with their abilities and capacities. The law also highlights the Palestinian state's responsibility to protect the rights of persons with disabilities and ensure access to legally guaranteed rights (Articles 2 and 3). Furthermore, Article 12 of the law mandates that individuals with disabilities have access to all facilities and services, enabling them to manage their daily lives independently.

2.1. Research questions

While there have been positive studies on achieving an inclusive society in developing countries, view studies in Palestine have highlighted numerous barriers to financial inclusion [[19], [20], [21]]. As identified in some national studies, these challenges include poverty, unemployment, and religious beliefs prohibiting certain banking transactions. Notably, these national studies have not sufficiently delved into researching the specific challenges confronted by individuals with visual disabilities in Palestine. The lack of attention to these challenges hinders the identification of successful mechanisms to overcome them. This research aims to assess commercial banks' effectiveness in facilitating financial inclusion for individuals with visual impairments in Palestine. The aim is to pinpoint the current obstacles to achieving broad financial inclusion of banking services for this group and to suggest changes to the frameworks and systems of financial inclusion to guarantee equal access to banking transactions for individuals with visual impairments in Palestine. This research aims to address the following questions.

  • 1.

    How successful have commercial banks promoted financial inclusion for VI individuals in Palestine?

  • 2.

    What challenges exist in achieving financial inclusion of banking services for the VI individuals in Palestine?

  • 3.

    How can the structures and systems of financial inclusion for the VI individuals in Palestine be modified to ensure universal access to banking transactions?

2.2. Novelty of the study

This study is the first attempt to evaluate the effectiveness of the Financial Inclusion Strategy introduced by the Palestine Monetary Authority (PMA) in involving individuals with visual impairments and helping them access banking services. This addresses one of the marginalized groups targeted by the strategy. The study offers recommendations to policymakers on the barriers to financial inclusion for this demographic and the ways to overcome such obstacles based on comparisons of relevant literature from other developing countries and the results of field research.

The study starts by explaining the national strategy and emphasizing the importance of financial inclusion for marginalized groups in strengthening the national economy. It then explains the concept of financial inclusion for individuals with visual disabilities, outlining its requirements and challenges. The study concludes by presenting field research findings compared with the results and recommendations from relevant studies in other developing countries.

3. Conceptual framework

This part of the research focuses on financial inclusion, discussing its requirements, challenges, and importance in the economic development of developing countries. It also explores the social and human aspects of financial exclusion, especially its effects on marginalized groups such as individuals with visual impairments. Furthermore, it includes a review of various research findings from studies conducted in developing countries with economic structures similar to that of Palestine.

3.1. Financial inclusion

Financial inclusion is the provision of practical and affordable financial products and services that meet the needs of individuals and companies. This includes transactions, payments, savings, credit, and insurance. It is also the availability of financial services to marginalized groups, including weaker sections and low-income individuals [6]. Many governments are making substantial efforts to achieve high levels of financial inclusion through different initiatives. These initiatives include simplifying the process of opening bank accounts with minimal documentation requirements, requiring the acquisition of bank identification numbers, offering free debit cards and insurance policies, using mobile technology to improve financial access, introducing direct government-to-person (G2P) payment systems, allowing mortgage enrollment without mandatory down payments, and widely utilizing bank correspondents, among other measures [11]. Financial inclusion means easy access to financial services and adequate credit for all segments of society, including poor and marginalized groups such as residents in rural areas, unemployed women, and people with disabilities [16,19]. It is the ability of everyone to have easy access to basic formal financial services. This means that individuals and businesses should be able to obtain affordable and beneficial formal financial services that meet their needs responsibly and sustainably [11]. Financial institutions can enhance financial inclusion by utilizing different channels. These channels may include expanding their branches, offering simple and basic services, easing Know Your Customer (KYC) requirements for vulnerable groups and low-income earners, implementing mobile banking and agent banking, and promoting financial literacy, among other strategies [6]. The aim is to integrate the unbanked population into the formal financial system by providing timely and sufficient credit at an affordable cost. This process ensures that vulnerable groups can access various financial services, such as savings and payments, at a reasonable cost. The ultimate goal is to provide financial stability and security to all individuals, regardless of their economic status [6].

Financial inclusion is measured by using different indicators. Scholars often use the number of people with bank accounts as a primary measure. It is usually calculated as the average number of bank accounts per 1000 people. Sarma developed a multidimensional index of financial inclusion (IFI) in 2008, which considers three important dimensions: accessibility, availability, and usage of banking services [9]. Access indicators consider the availability of bank branches and ATMs in terms of area and population. Usage indicators are a crucial measure of financial services usage. They include loan accounts per capita, loan income ratio, deposit accounts per capita, and deposit income ratio [9]. Honohan highlights the importance of understanding demand and supply factors when interpreting data on the usage of financial services. This involves examining cost conditions and identifying barriers to access [19].

3.1.1. Financial inclusion and sustainable livelihoods

The financial inclusion of persons with disabilities can help achieve the Sustainable Development Goals set by the United Nations in 2015 [4]. Several relevant studies support this conclusion [2]. Financial inclusion is a policy approach to make financial services accessible, available, and affordable for marginalized segments of society, particularly those disadvantaged or with disabilities [4]. Financial inclusion involves connecting individuals without access to banking services and those with limited access to affordable, transparent, and dependable financial services, resulting in significant economic benefits [3].

Many studies reported a positive relationship between financial inclusion and livelihood indicators. The World Bank Report 2014 suggests that financial systems in developing countries can provide affordable, accessible, and customized financial services that meet the needs of low-income and disadvantaged groups in society [16]. Evidence shows that developing countries experience ethnicity bias and social exclusion; empirical evidence indicates that individuals from black and minority groups are more prone to financial exclusion, primarily attributable not to their ethnicity or gender but largely to their lower incomes [8]. Choudhury and Bagchi (2016) suggested that financial exclusion contributes to developing countries' ongoing poverty and financial struggles [1]. Scholars have studied how financial inclusion can reduce poverty in emerging economies. For example, Muhammad et al. (2017) analyzed the factors that determine financial inclusion and its relationship with poverty reduction in 35 developing countries [1]. They argued that the primary beneficiaries of financial inclusion are low-income people, particularly those living in poverty. Furthermore, they emphasized that financial inclusion can significantly improve impoverished populations' well-being [1]. A research paper conducted in 2016 identified all the factors that impact financial inclusion through literature published in developing countries. The study found that promoting the development of an inclusive financial system requires attention to marginalized poor individuals and infrastructure aspects of financial services [19]. Therefore, financial inclusion bridges the gap between the wealthy and the less fortunate by giving everyone equal access to financial services [22]. Financial inclusion can help individuals generate income, mobilize savings, and accumulate wealth. This can be a way for them to break free from poverty and income inequality. Conceptual and functional definitions suggest that financial exclusion mainly affects marginalized groups [9].

Governments worldwide have recognized the importance of having a financial system accessible to everyone, regardless of their economic status. As a result, many countries have implemented laws and regulations to promote financial inclusion. For example, the United Kingdom established a Financial Inclusion Task Force in 2005, while French law (1998) stipulates that every individual is entitled to a bank account. In the United States, the Community Reinvestment Act (1997) requires banks to provide credit equally to all areas they serve, regardless of their financial status. In 1996, the German Bankers Association introduced a voluntary code to encourage the creation of a basic and affordable current bank account suitable for essential banking transactions [2]. Also, financial institutions in developed countries have taken significant steps to aid individuals with visual impairments. They have integrated devices to promote financial inclusion for the visually impaired, enhancing their overall quality of life. In parallel, countries like the United States have implemented regulations, such as the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), to guide banks in their transactions with visually impaired individuals [21].

3.1.2. Humanitarian financial inclusion

In contrast to financial inclusion, financial exclusion refers to the lack of accessibility to financial services such as banking, credit, savings, and insurance. It aims to provide accessibility to all. Financial exclusion can be caused by factors such as lack of financial literacy and awareness, unfavorable demographic and geographical conditions, self-exclusion, low income per capita, limited internet access, inflation, and bank concentration. Financial exclusion is often associated with social exclusion and poverty, and it poses a significant challenge for vulnerable groups that lack adequate social amenities and education [3]. The literature on financial exclusion is often discussed in the context of social exclusion, which refers to the marginalized status of certain groups within mainstream society [9]. “The vulnerable group theory of financial inclusion” suggests that programs aimed at financial inclusion should focus on the most vulnerable members of society disproportionately affected by economic hardship and crises. This theory argues that vulnerable individuals are often the hardest hit by financial crises and economic downturns, making their inclusion in the formal financial sector especially crucial. One approach to achieving this is to encourage other vulnerable populations, such as those living in poverty, young people, women, and the elderly, to join the formal financial sector by obtaining formal accounts. This would enable them to access government-to-person social cash transfer programs, thereby boosting financial inclusion rates among vulnerable groups [11].

Financial inclusion plays a crucial role in augmenting the productive assets of marginalized individuals by investing in education, health, and new technologies. This, in turn, enables job creation and facilitates increased government social spending on education, health, and social protection interventions for the marginalized [1]. It also aims to promote equal treatment and societal inclusivity, which serves a social purpose aligned with the broader concept of social inclusion. It advocates for equal opportunities and works to reduce the growing disparity between the affluent and the underprivileged. As a result, financial inclusion contributes to a comprehensive transformation of individuals' socioeconomic and financial landscape [8]. In its annual report, the International Finance Corporation (2016) underscored the social function of financial inclusion, providing marginalized individuals the opportunity to actively engage in economic activities and thereby enhance their overall quality of life, contributing to positive impacts on the country's economic growth when these disadvantaged members participate in productive endeavors [21]. A research paper on the role of mobile banking in promoting humane financial inclusion among visually impaired individuals in Zimbabwe in 2019 recommended that the government champion humanitarian financial inclusion as a social goal. It also emphasized the commitment of companies to initiate initiatives supporting financial inclusion for people with visual disabilities [21].

3.2. Barriers to financial inclusion

Numerous studies investigated VI individuals' difficulties in accessing banking services [2,4,9,10,16,[20], [21], [22],27]. Although these studies approach the issue from different perspectives, they share some common ground. These barriers fall into three categories: the commercial strategies banks use, financial independence for people with visual impairments, and accessibility to technology. Evidence shows that these barriers significantly disrupt efforts to provide financial services to people with disabilities.

3.2.1. Commercial banking perspective

Commercial banks invest in activities or individuals who will bring huge returns due to the commercialization of these banks. These banks do not grant any privileges or preferential treatment to disabled people because their activity lacks a social dimension of solidarity and is dominated by a competitive character [4]. Even at the international level, other groups who could benefit from financial inclusion, like young people, elderly individuals, people with disabilities, and those excluded from the financial sector due to criminal offenses, have often been ignored in the literature [11]. Another study revealed that the majority of people with visual impairments are unemployed. Employer employees' wages are comparatively low compared to other categories, making them less attractive to commercial banks [2,28]. This business orientation is evidently in conflict with the social orientation of financial inclusion, which positively impacts people's daily lives by promoting access to diverse financial products, facilitating smoother consumption, encouraging savings mobilization, and minimizing risk exposure [8]. A research paper conducted in 2019 delved into the realm of mobile banking and its role in promoting humane financial inclusion for VI individuals in a developing country, proposed a definition of humanitarian financial inclusion, emphasizing the respectful and dignified engagement of individuals in financial activities to enhance their well-being [21]. The study suggested that the government should promote humanitarian financial inclusion as a social goal, and the corporate sector should actively support it as part of its social responsibility, contributing to society through initiatives that advance humane financial inclusion for all marginalized groups. This obligation demands that banks be responsible for informing marginalized individuals about their services and products and the benefits specific to these groups [8]. Participants in a related study reported that it can be challenging to make communication accessible for people who have visual and auditory impairments. Even though people with disabilities may be interested in the services and products that are advertised, their limited accessibility could lead to their exclusion. Therefore, it is crucial for banks to ensure that everyone can access their services and products [4].

3.2.2. Digital banking accessibility

The second reason why VI is excluded from financial services is the challenges of Internet banking accessibility; they may encounter difficulties in registering for Internet banking, and even if they successfully register, they often face various accessibility issues on the website [10]. Numerous studies' findings have identified obstacles affecting the equal access of visually impaired individuals to technology-enhanced banking. These barriers include non-user-friendly website interfaces, ATMs lacking speech-operable features, the absence of headphone jacks on ATMs for audio instructions, the absence of audio-enhanced security dongles for online transactions, and poor contrast and fonts on ATM screens, among other factors. A research paper conducted qualitative, semi-structured interviews with managers from commercial banks in Malawi in 2022. The study aimed to gain insights into their perspectives on the extension of financial services to individuals with disabilities, and the findings highlighted the importance of investing in technology, which has been emphasized as a key factor in extending banking services to marginalized individuals in society. In areas lacking physical banking branches, technology, including mobile phones and other electronic devices, enables people with disabilities to access financial services [4].

Also, another research paper conducted in 2020 in Egypt focused on the primary obstacles confronting individuals with visual disabilities and reported that despite the widespread adoption of technology in the country, with nearly all banks launching websites and providing digital services through ATMs, payment cards, and phone banking, numerous bank and ATM sites remain inaccessible. Furthermore, ATMs lack the necessary technology to accommodate individuals with visual impairments, leading to their financial exclusion [20]. Another research paper investigated the challenges individuals with visual impairments encounter when interacting with electronic banking services in Egypt. The study involved distributing a questionnaire to a random sample of 426 visually impaired individuals in 2021. The research recommended enhancing the accessibility of all Egyptian banking websites by ensuring that functions such as balance inquiries, money transfers, credit card settlements, and various other features are accessible to people with visual impairments [10].

Individuals with visual impairments lack equal access to technology-driven platforms for financial transactions compared to those with sight, contributing to their financial exclusion [2]. Addressing this discrimination is a pressing matter for various reasons. Firstly, denying the right to access transactions through technology deprives individuals with vision impairments of the opportunity to exercise other rights, such as making electronic payments for goods and services, but it also hinders their full participation in society, exacerbates exclusion, and diminishes financial independence. Secondly, those who cannot access online transaction systems may face additional service costs, as the Central Bank in developing countries has mandated banks to introduce administrative charges for physical cash deposits and withdrawals. With businesses and government services increasingly transitioning to online platforms, many users can gain better control over their finances and enjoy the enhanced convenience of this approach. Unfortunately, individuals with vision impairments are excluded from these benefits due to unequal access to technology-driven platforms for financial transactions compared to sighted individuals. Arguably, this exclusion from the digital world marks the commencement of a lifetime of marginalization from mainstream society [29].

Also, failure to prioritize banking services for visually impaired individuals and other marginalized groups may hinder the attainment of digital inclusion. Article 9 of the UNCRPD explicitly outlines the right to empower persons with disabilities to live independently and fully participate in all aspects of life. State parties must implement suitable measures to guarantee that individuals with disabilities enjoy equal access to Information and Communication Technologies and other facilities and services available or provided to the public. Tackling accessibility challenges to technology-driven financial platforms is crucial for inclusion and empowerment under national and international legal standards [2]. The existing extent of exclusion faced by individuals with visual impairments, resulting from obstacles to their access, represents a violation of their rights (UN, 2006). For instance, equal access to public services is a universally acknowledged human right delineated in the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 21, Section 2). Various agreements have further reinforced this right, including the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights (1981) and the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Additionally, the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities underscores the significance of social inclusion by asserting the right of individuals with disabilities to receive measures implemented within social structures to enhance their independent living to the greatest extent possible (UN, 2006) [2]. Also, article 9 of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities distinctly mandates member states to facilitate independent living and full participation in all facets of life for individuals with disabilities. States Parties must adopt suitable measures to guarantee that those with disabilities enjoy parity in accessing information and communication technologies and other facilities and services accessible or offered to the public.

Considering the obstacles to internet access and the growing reliance on online services, addressing the impediments restricting access is crucial. This approach aims to promote the adoption of online banking among VI individuals, address concerns related to perceived security risks in online banking, and alleviate the discomfort associated with depending on others to facilitate access to their banked funds. In several developed countries, many banks formulate policies to collaborate with software companies, seeking integrative technologies to assist individuals with disabilities [2].

3.2.3. Security and privacy challenges

The failure of banks to identify electronic banking services catering to the requirements of visually impaired individuals, enabling them to independently manage their bank accounts in alignment with their right to self-determination, poses a challenge in terms of security and privacy, hindering their financial integration [10]. A significant challenge individuals with visual impairments encounter revolves around maintaining privacy and security in their daily activities [20]. Security and privacy within digital banking refer to the potential risk of loss resulting from fraud or a breach of a bank user's online security. Using IT devices for financial transactions presents a significant threat to customers, as criminal activities can be executed swiftly without physical interaction [20]. It can also be addressed within the framework of banking confidentiality and the incapacity to conceal financial transactions. Individuals with sight have the ability to perceive their surroundings and safeguard their privacy [10]. Unlike sighted individuals who can monitor their surroundings to safeguard against privacy threats, individuals with visual disabilities face limitations. For instance, in public settings, sighted individuals can strategically position their mobile devices to conceal the screen from onlookers. Similarly, when sharing a photo online, individuals with visual impairments lack the ability to visually inspect it for private content before sharing it [24]. Before electronically sharing a photo, they can visually inspect it to ensure it does not include sensitive content [10].

VI might find themselves compelled to depend on family members, friends, or, in certain instances, unfamiliar individuals to carry out banking transactions, such as making a credit card purchase or accessing an inaccessible internet banking website [4]. This heightened reliance on others raises security risks [10]. A 2019 research paper on mobile banking and the humanitarian financial inclusion of visually impaired individuals in a developing country revealed that ten percent of visually impaired individuals with mobile phones who regularly receive money from relatives faced challenges accessing mobile banking services. The study also highlighted that, citing security concerns, these individuals had no alternative but to visit banks to obtain their funds [21]. Another research paper conducted in 2023 in Fiji undertook 59 semi-structured interviews with financial institutions, technology companies offering digital solutions, government policymakers, and officials from the Federation of the Blind and Visually Impaired. The objective was to scrutinize the challenges encountered by the blind and visually impaired. The findings revealed that only 38 percent of respondents felt secure utilizing bank websites [22].

4. Methods

The study's main objective was pursued using a systematic approach. The research methodology comprised desk research and a quantitative questionnaire, as detailed below.

4.1. Desk research

Desk research was conducted over two months (July–August 2023) to gather and analyze existing studies. Relevant literature reviews were located in electronic databases for peer-reviewed research papers published in Arabic and English from 2010 onwards (including Scopus, EBSCO Discovery, and ProQuest One Academic). "The search strategy included the following relevant terms:": “Vissulay Impaired Individuals, OR Vissual Imaprament, OR Low Vision AND Finacial Inclusion", “Self Determination AND VI”, “Financial Inclusion AND Livelihood Activities”, “Financial Inclusion AND Poverty”, “Financial Inclusion AND Internet Banking”, “Financial Inclusion AND Palestine”, “Usability AND Bank AND Website”, “Financial Inclusion AND Internet Banking Accessibility”, “Financial Inclusion AND Economic Growth”. The search included querying the Arabic databases (AL-Manhal and East Law) and exploring the websites of Palestinian research centers for studies in Arabic relevant to the research site. At this stage, 38 references were used to develop the theoretical framework and discuss the field research findings.

4.2. Qualitative questionnaire

Due to the absence of statistics, studies, or information regarding the number and distribution of VI covered by financial inclusion in Palestine, a qualitative questionnaire involving open discussions with fourteen representatives from Palestinian banks was conducted. Ethical approval was granted by the relevant academic departments. This method was chosen to gather data and gain a comprehensive understanding of the perceptions and actions taken by commercial banks to integrate people with disabilities into financial services. Semi-structured interviews were deemed suitable for this research, as they enable a deeper exploration of decision-makers perspectives within Palestinian banks. A semi-structured interview guide based on a literature review relevant to the study topic was devolved [1,2,4,5,7,10,12,[14], [15], [16],[20], [21], [22],28,30].

To understand the perspectives on the realities and challenges of financial inclusion for VI individuals, the PMA arranged interviews with representatives from the 13 banks operating in Palestine and the head of the Association of Palestinian Banks. The PMA emailed the research group with the consent of all banks to participate and the interviewees' names and contact information (Table 1). The research objectives and questions, along with the interview topics and questions, were shared with each bank and the Association of Palestinian Banks via the PMA. Each bank then nominated a representative to be interviewed with the Authors; they were given enough time to coordinate and prepare the responses within their financial institution to provide comprehensive responses.

The interview questions were categorized into three main areas: assessment of the success of the PMA legislative policy on including VI individuals into banking services, identification of obstacles to financial inclusion for those with visual impairments, and assessment of banks' efforts toward financial integration. Each interviewee was asked about the number of VI customers at the bank and their perspectives on the current state and challenges of including this group in Palestine. General questions were also formulated to guide the free discussions, which were reviewed by two Academics or feedback. The research team discussed the experts' comments and incorporated them into the final draft.

All meetings were conducted in September 2023 online via ZOOM, averaging 50 min per interviewee. All interviews were arranged under the auspices of the PMA. The participants were informed that anonymous quotes may be used for academic research, ensuring their anonymity and confidentiality. A standardized list of questions tailored to each interviewee's specialization and professional experience was used, and the interviews were documented in Arabic Word files. Verbal consent was obtained at the initiation of each interview. Each participant was assigned a code based on the order presented in Table No. 1. For instance, the code B1 indicates to the first person listed in Table No. 1. The second author transcribed the interview data verbatim and sent it to each interviewee the following day for review and comments on their statements.

4.3. Data analysis

After conducting the interviews, the results were analyzed and categorized. Each question was given a sub-heading, the corresponding answers were listed beneath, and similar answers were arranged in order. The data was then tabulated, with similar answers grouped under each question and highlighting variations between the codes. For instance, responses related to a bank's commitment to adapting its website for individuals with visual disabilities were grouped together under that specific question. Finally, the research team convened multiple times to discuss the results, analyze similarities and differences in respondents' answers, and compile the study findings.

5. Results

All interviewees unanimously acknowledged their awareness of the Financial Inclusion Strategy issued by the PMA and its positive role in integrating marginalized groups within the banking sector. They also agreed that decision-makers had given insufficient attention to empowering the VI for financial integration, hindering fulfilling the bank's objectives for this group. Several interviewees shared statistics on their VI customers in addition to the number of all customers; others disclosed the number of their VI customers without disclosing the numbers of all their customers, while two interviewees did not disclose any numbers (Table 1).

The percentage of VI individuals having bank accounts (487) in relation to those VI eligible to open bank accounts (24,318) shows a clear financial exclusion within this demographic (2 %). This percentage is low compared to Palestine's overall financial integration rate of 60 %. Notably, 52 % of this amount of VI are customers of a national bank (BI), which successfully conducted the Ministry of Social Affairs to facilitate the transfer of Social Security salaries for visually impaired individuals enrolled in national social support programs. In contrast, two banks reported having no customers with visual impairments (B6–B10).

All interviewees reported the measures they have implemented to enhance the financial inclusion for individuals with disabilities in adherence to the provisions outlined in PMA Resolution No. 8 of 2017. Each interviewee affirmed their commitment to implementing the financial inclusion standards detailed in this Resolution. These standards encompass various aspects, such as providing accessible entry areas for individuals with mobility disabilities, deploying talking ATMs in central locations with Braille-embossed buttons, printing certain transaction forms in Braille, and assigning an employee in each branch to address the needs of individuals with disabilities.

However, despite these measures, many interviewees were dissatisfied with their services and their perceived ability to achieve financial integration. This sentiment was shared by participants (B2, B5, B10, B11, B12, and B13). For example, the interviewee (B10) reported that:

“They regulated methods for achieving financial inclusion for individuals with disabilities without accounting for the specific needs of each disability type. These measures do not consider the special needs of VI individuals, and the banks did not provide any initiatives to attract this group”.

Also, all interviewees discussed the barriers impeding efforts to achieve financial inclusion in Palestine. These barriers were categorized under several sub-topics, including the commercial banks model, poor communication, inadequacy of technological resources, and subjective challenges.

5.1. Commercial banks model

Many interviewees highlighted that a key factor contributing to the financial exclusion of VI individuals is the profit-driven approach of banks in Palestine (B2, B3, B5, B6, B10, B11, B12, B13, B14). While these banks maintain a non-discriminatory approach based on social dimensions, they operate as public joint-stock companies driven by profit objectives. They focus on attracting groups with tested financial and competitive advantages in the market. The interviewee (B3) reported that:

"The main reason for this category's financial exclusion is that it doesn't receive sufficient attention from financial institutions; it is not a target group for us."

"Banks adhere to the PMA instructions only to a minimum extent. We follow the financial inclusion requirements outlined in Resolution No. 8 of 2017, with no interest in incurring additional financial costs to conduct this group," added another interviewee (B2).

Another interviewee highlighted that a key problem is that the PMA instructions do not establish specific requirements (Checklist) for national banks to achieve financial inclusion for the VI group (B14). The financial returns of attracting this group are not commensurate with the expenses required to achieve such inclusion (B2–B3–B6–B14). This disparity was also evident in the statements of the majority of respondents, asserting that individuals with VI receive no special privileges or financial exemptions compared to others, except for two respondents (B2–B8) who mentioned receiving exemptions from periodic fees related to withdrawal and money transfer transactions.

5.2. Poor communication

All interviewees reported that the challenge of communicating with VI contributes to their financial exclusion. According to the PMA Resolution No. 8 of 2017, banks must employ a qualified staff member in each branch to assist individuals with disabilities without requiring proficiency in communicating with VI individuals. Each branch has an employee available to aid them. All agreed that there is a special protocol for conducting customer transactions.

When a VI customer visits the bank, an employee assists them in ensuring the seamless completion of any banking operation (B5, B7, B8).

"The PMA Resolution requires us to assign an employee to receive the disabled person and exclusively facilitate their transactions. We are not mandated to introduce other facilities for this group," reported B4.

The field research showed a unanimous agreement among interviewees that bank branches lack qualified staff trained to conduct Braille. Some interviewees noted that a single workshop organized by the PMA and the Association of Palestinian Banks in 2017 highlighted the importance of Braille in reading but fell short of providing comprehensive training (B10). Most current employees have not participated in this workshop, leading to a deficiency in the minimum requirements for effective interaction (B11– B3). Some interviewees do not remember organizing that workshop (B4, B6, B7, B8, B9, B12, B13).

5.3. Poor accessibility of technological resources

All interviewees reported that electronic services provided by Palestinian banks fail to accommodate the needs of individuals with visual disabilities. Notably, websites lack screen reader programs, and mobile applications are unreadable. Several interviewees have approached these accessibility requirements solely from a profit perspective, questioning the allocation of resources when VI customers are absent (B6, B10, and B3). B13 reported that:

“The financial gains from implementing these services do not justify the associated preparation expenses. Consequently, Bankers are reluctant to invest in these requirements unless they are legally mandated”.

Even the bank with the highest number of VI customers acknowledged that most VI transactions are conducted through their guardians, as they lack the qualifications to navigate electronic services independently (B1).

“Banks have cautiously adhered to the PMA instructions, evident in their commitment to providing only one Braille-speaking ATM in each city center, while the remaining ATMs do not meet these specifications,” reported B 14. “The VI individuals residing in remote villages do not benefit from these devices because they must travel through occupation army checkpoints and incur expenses to access banking services,” reported B8.

Interviewees explored VI individuals' perspectives on electronic services and mobile applications, questioning the feasibility of demanding banks invest in accessibility measures. Additionally, inquiries were made regarding the percentage of VI individuals proficient in Braille (B2, B4, B7, B9, B12). Addressing the issue of banking secrecy, some interviewees expressed concerns about allowing this group to use electronic services and ATMs. An interviewee reported that VI individuals used to rely on others when conducting bank transactions, raising questions about the confidentiality of financial transactions (B1). Notably, talking ATMs were highlighted as potential violators of banking secrecy, as financial movements could be overheard by anyone at the machine (B9, B14).

5.4. Subjective challenges

Several interviewees reported various subjective barriers preventing the inclusion of VI in financial transactions, such as unemployment and illiteracy (B1–B2–B4–B7–B11–B13). Most VI are challenged by their social environment, which doubts their ability to independently manage their affairs and daily needs (B1–B2–B4–B14–B6). All banks refuse to deal with VI individuals unless certain handling protocols are observed to express their will.

"A significant proportion of VI individuals struggle with illiteracy, necessitating using their fingerprints for banking transactions. According to the operational protocols in Palestinian banks, two witnesses must attest to the completion of such transactions," reported B1.

"While all banks have taken steps to accommodate VI individuals by printing banking transactions in Braille, the practice shows that they use escorts from their relatives to help them carry out banking transactions,’ reported B5.

Another interviewee shared a positive experience, indicating VI's ability to manage their bank accounts independently. "I have a young visually impaired friend, whom I got to know during his frequent visits to the bank to manage his accounts; he is one of the most prominent lawyers in the city and does all his banking transactions independently," added B2.

6. Discussion

The financial inclusion of VI individuals is key to the 2030 Strategy for Sustainable Development framework, unveiled by the United Nations in 2015. This Strategy aims to provide concrete solutions to address discrimination, inequality, and poverty eradication by integrating marginalized and economically disadvantaged groups into the economic development policies of member states. It emphasized that financial inclusion is a fundamental tool to ensure easy access to financial services and ample credit opportunities for all groups, including the less affluent and marginalized communities [1,8,9,[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17],31]. The PMA Financial Inclusion Strategy (2018–2025) outlined goals that include eliminating poverty in Palestine by ensuring the financial inclusion of marginalized and economically disadvantaged groups. Numerous studies have established a direct relationship between poverty and visual impairment, attributing it to various social factors such as illiteracy and unemployment [4,5,9,12,21]. The fulfillment of the Palestinian Authority's commitments to the International Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and numerous national legislations necessitates financial inclusion for individuals with visual disabilities. This is a vital aspect of empowerment and self-determination, as emphasized in various legal texts [23,[32], [33], [34], [35],36,37]. The above-mentioned results underscore the significance of the “financial literacy theory of financial inclusion,” which posits that enhanced financial literacy fosters greater participation in the formal financial sector. This theoretical framework suggests that financial inclusion can be significantly promoted by advancing financial literacy through educational initiatives. As individuals acquire financial literacy, they are more inclined to utilize formal financial services [11].

This study examined the success of initiatives implemented by the PMA to achieve financial inclusion for VI individuals in Palestine, as perceived by representatives of banks operating in the Palestinian Territories. The interviewees unanimously acknowledged the significance of this financial inclusion in overcoming poverty and ensuring the empowerment and self-determination of this group, as highlighted in various relevant studies [1,3,4,8,12,13,17,38], they expressed dissatisfaction with the outcomes of these initiatives due to the failure to take into account the needs of this group and identify the challenges that hinder their financial integration in Palestine. The field research statistics showed that 98 % of VI individuals eligible for financial inclusion are financially excluded. Even at the international level, other groups who could benefit from financial inclusion, such as young people, elderly individuals, people with disabilities, and those excluded from the financial sector due to criminal offenses, have often been overlooked in the literature [11].

The main challenge highlighted by the interviewees is the profit target of the banks ' work, which does not encourage them to attract this group, especially considering that the PMA instructions do not address financial exclusion effectively. These banks seem to ignore the social function of financial inclusion (human financial inclusion), a point of view supported by several relevant studies [2,4,8,15,21]. Consequently, they seem to fulfill their obligations outlined in the monetary authority's instructions merely at a basic level, without proactively conducting this group or offering services tailored to enable them to manage their banking transactions independently. Also, these banks do not provide financial privileges to this group, a key component for achieving financial integration, as outlined in various studies [2,4,8]. These findings are consistent with numerous relevant studies conducted in other developing countries [2,4,15,25]. Commercial banks are not expected to undertake these initiatives without specific legislative regulations.

Poor communication between banks and VI is a key reason for financial exclusion for this group [2,4,10,20,22,28]. The research findings showed several subjective factors among the visually impaired hinder communication success. These factors include the prevalence of illiteracy and the unemployment rate. Banks in Palestine do not make efforts to communicate with these groups, as they perceive that the financial return achieved is not proportional to the expenses of such communication. This perception comes from the belief that most VI individuals are either poor or unemployed, leading to the notion that providing support and credit programs to economically less influential individuals is not worthwhile. These findings align with the outcomes of many studies that establish a connection between unemployment, poverty, and financial exclusion [1,4,8,9,12,19].

These subjective obstacles are prevalent in most developing countries [[2], [3], [4],6,22,28,31], so this does not exempt banks from training an employee in each competent branch to address this group's needs. This includes skills such as reading Braille and using electronic applications to assist people with visual disabilities. This outcome is consistent with other relevant studies in developing countries that advocate for banks to equip some of their staff to effectively communicate with this group [4,20,21]. It clearly indicates that the efforts made by banks to facilitate communication with individuals with disabilities have fallen short, particularly concerning the awareness of visually impaired individuals about campaigns and information related to financial services implemented in banks. This finding is consistent with a recent study emphasizing the importance of reviewing bank advertisements to meet the needs of people with disabilities [4]. Banks operating in Palestine should serve as integral components of the financial development tools for this group rather than merely benefiting from them [2,4,8,15,21].

Poor technology has been identified as a contributing factor to the financial exclusion of VI individuals. All interviewees emphasized that the mobile applications and websites of banks operating in Palestine do not consider the needs of this group. While these banks complied with the minimum requirements of the PMA instructions by providing a talking ATM in the center of each city, they ignored the needs of VI individuals in remote rural areas. In communities lacking bank branches, technology is crucial in enabling VI individuals to access financial services [4]. This finding is consistent with the findings of related studies that have identified a lack of technology as one of the causes of financial exclusion [2,4,7,10,20,22]. There is a possibility that Palestinian banks have yet to recognize technology as a valuable tool to address the financial exclusion of the VI individuals. This is particularly noteworthy given the unique security challenges in the Palestinian territories, characterized by difficulties in moving between West Bank villages under the Israeli occupation army security and city centers under Palestinian security control due to numerous checkpoints and settler attacks on the Palestinian population during such movements.

The development of such technology should also prioritize the privacy and security of this group. The research findings showed that privacy and security violations obstruct using visually impaired people's technology. These findings are partially consistent with several studies that have identified privacy and customer security concerns as factors contributing to customers' reluctance to adopt e-banking services [4,10,16,[20], [21], [22]]. The research findings showed the limited success of national policies in incorporating individuals with visual impairments into financial services. While bankers acknowledge the significance of financial inclusion for people with visual impairments to foster self-determination and societal integration, commercial banks must actively contribute by offering comprehensive initiatives to overcome the barriers to financial inclusion. Additionally, the PMA is recommended to constitute specific criteria through legislation, ensuring this group initiates inclusive measures and investigates the reasons behind their financial exclusion. “The community echelon theory of financial inclusion” states that marginalized populations should access formal financial services through their community leaders. These leaders greatly impact community members and can help improve their well-being. Through this influence, community leaders can encourage community members to participate in the formal financial sector, improving their access to formal financial services [11].

7. Conclusion

This study highlighted the inadequacy of the PMA instructions on financial inclusion in effectively addressing the underlying causes of financial exclusion among individuals with visual disabilities. These instructions lacked a foundation in studies examining the reasons for this financial exclusion, and they did not consider the unique needs of this particular group. The identified challenges encompassed a lack of financial knowledge, limited access to technology, communication barriers, and unemployment among VI individuals. These challenges have the potential to impede the use of financial inclusion as a tool for alleviating poverty among this group in Palestine. Additionally, banks operating in Palestine are required to recognize the significance of the social aspect of financial inclusion for impoverished and marginalized groups. It is crucial for them to engage with these groups and act as development partners proactively. The findings of this study bear implications for policymaking aimed at achieving financial inclusion and reducing poverty among VI individuals in Palestine. Decision-makers in Palestine are recommended to establish a community partnership, with the active participation of banks, to eradicate illiteracy among people with visual disabilities. The parties involved in this association are recommended to engage with VI and provide the necessary qualifications to address the root causes of financial exclusion. These efforts will facilitate the exchange of ideas among banks regarding innovative communication technologies, fostering their adoption to enhance accessibility for people with disabilities. Moreover, the government should follow and supervise the implementation of laws that integrate VI individuals into the public and private sectors and Social Security programs. This offers individuals with disabilities a more favorable opportunity for a sustainable source of income and the chance to engage in financial services. Upon establishing their means of support, they are more inclined to avail themselves of bank credit services.

Research limitations

Drawing broad conclusions from the research findings necessitates further investigation and scrutiny. The insights and results derived in this study primarily reflect interviewees' viewpoints from banks operating in Palestine. Consequently, the comprehensiveness and accuracy of these results cannot be categorically confirmed or refuted. There were difficulties in establishing communication with visually impaired individuals to authenticate these results. The absence of VI associations in Palestine created challenges for reaching and conducting representatives of this group to establish a quantitative survey. Furthermore, interviewees refused to share contact information for their VI customers because of bank secrecy. There is a need to initiate future studies to illuminate the perspectives of VI individuals regarding the factors contributing to their financial exclusion despite these limitations. This study is the first initiation in exploring the reasons for the financial exclusion of VI individuals in Palestine, constructing the groundwork for subsequent research endeavors.
