Arrest and detention decisions by police for people without disabilities are primarily influenced by offense severity, suspect demeanor, and situational evidence.
## Offense Characteristics

* Severity of the Crime: Police are far more likely to arrest individuals when the alleged offense is a felony or a violent crime rather than a minor misdemeanor. [1] 
* Presence of Evidence: Clear physical evidence, visible property damage, or immediate contraband strongly increases the likelihood of a formal detention.
* Victim Preference: If a victim or complainant demands police action or wishes to press charges, officers are more prone to execute an arrest.

## Suspect Behavior and Demeanor

* Compliance: Suspects who follow instructions and remain calm are less likely to be detained or arrested for minor infractions.
* Resistance or Hostility: Verbal hostility, physical resistance, or fleeing from officers frequently converts a minor stop into a full custodial arrest.
* Presence of Witnesses: The reactions of bystanders or escalating tensions at the scene can pressure officers to make an arrest to regain control.

## Social and Environmental Factors

* Neighborhood Context: High-crime areas or heavily policed neighborhoods experience elevated rates of proactive stops and subsequent detentions.
* Demographics: Research indicates that race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status can impact police discretion, resulting in disproportionate stop-and-arrest rates for certain minority groups even without underlying disabilities. [1] 
* Discretionary Authority: Police officers hold broad legal discretion to issue warnings or citations instead of arresting individuals, depending on department policy and officer judgment.
