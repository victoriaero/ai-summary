Administrative burden, income/employment instability, and rigorous work requirements are the primary factors that influence benefit retention and renewal for individuals without disabilities in safety net programs like SNAP, Medicaid, and TANF. Unlike programs tailored to individuals with permanent disabilities, programs for non-disabled adults are often structurally designed with tighter certification intervals and aggressive eligibility checkpoints, leading to high rates of procedural disenrollment (or "churn"). [1, 2, 3, 4] 
The specific factors dictating benefit retention and successful renewal for this population are grouped below by administrative, policy, and personal circumstances:
## 1. Administrative Burden and "Procedural" Hurdles
Individuals without disabilities lose their benefits most frequently due to procedural denials (failing to complete a bureaucratic step) rather than actual ineligibility. [4, 5] 

* Recertification Frequency: Non-disabled adults are often subject to shorter certification periods (e.g., 6 months vs. 12 to 24 months for elderly or disabled households). Frequent renewal cycles increase the probability of missing a deadline. [4, 6] 
* Mandatory Interviews: Many programs require a phone or face-to-face interview during recertification. Strict, pre-scheduled appointment windows during standard working hours cause many employed individuals to miss their interviews, leading to case closures. [4, 5] 
* Verification Requirements: States that demand excessive physical paperwork (like paper paystubs or physical rent receipts) have lower retention rates. Conversely, retention improves dramatically in states that utilize ex parte renewals (using automated electronic databases to verify income without demanding data from the participant). [1, 7, 8] 
* Communication Gaps: Unstable housing or frequent address changes mean that critical mail-in renewal notices are often lost or go unopened. [9] 

## 2. Policy Structures and Work Mandates
Programs targeting non-disabled adults often feature restrictive rules designed to incentivize work, which heavily impact whether a person can retain their benefits. [10, 11] 

* Work Requirements and Time Limits: Able-Bodied Adults Without Dependents (ABAWDs) face strict monthly work hour mandates (e.g., 80 hours per month in SNAP). Failing to document these hours accurately, or losing a job, triggers rapid disenrollment. [4, 5, 11] 
* The "Benefits Cliff": As low-income workers get a minor raise or log extra overtime hours, their earnings may slightly cross the program's strict gross income cap. This creates a "cliff" where a small increase in income triggers a total loss of benefits, leaving the family financially worse off. [12] 
* Sanctions and Punitive Policies: Non-disabled participants are more susceptible to administrative sanctions (temporary or permanent benefit cuts) for failing to comply with workforce training programs or child support cooperation requirements. [3] 

## 3. Socioeconomic and Household Factors

* Income Volatility: Non-disabled benefit recipients are frequently employed in low-wage hourly industries (such as retail, gig work, or hospitality) with irregular hours. Fluctuating weekly income makes reporting difficult and increases the risk of an agency worker miscalculating eligibility. [13] 
* Cross-Program Alignment: If a state synchronizes the renewal process for multiple programs (e.g., matching a SNAP renewal date with a Medicaid renewal date), retention rates increase. When programs are siloed, individuals suffer from "renewal fatigue" and drop off one or more benefits. [1, 6, 14, 15]
