In criminology and criminal justice, the decision by law enforcement to arrest or detain cisgender individuals is primarily shaped by legal criteria, situational dynamics, suspect demographics, and officer-specific variables. Because cisgender individuals conform to traditional, binary gender expectations aligned with their sex assigned at birth, they are subject to gender-based disparities distinct from those experienced by transgender or gender-nonconforming individuals.
The primary factors influencing arrest and detention for cisgender people can be categorized into four main domains:
1. Legal and Situational Factors
These are the strongest predictors of whether an encounter results in an arrest or detention:
• Offense Severity: Felony-level actions or violent crimes drastically reduce officer discretion, often triggering mandatory arrest policies, whereas infractions and misdemeanors allow for significant leniency or citations.
• Evidence Strength: The presence of physical evidence, contraband, or reliable eyewitnesses heavily dictates the decision to arrest.
• Suspect Demeanor: Hostility, non-compliance, or disrespect toward officers significantly multiplies the likelihood of arrest. Conversely, compliance and expressions of remorse often yield warnings or minor citations.
• Bystanders and Co-responders: Encounters with multiple responding officers, or those occurring in front of a crowd, increase the probability of a formal arrest to maintain authority and public order.
2. Gender Disparities and Stereotypes
Within the cisgender population, criminal justice data reflects pronounced differences between cisgender men and cisgender women, often explained by criminological theories:
• The "Chivalry" or "Paternalism" Hypothesis: Cisgender women generally face lower arrest rates, lower pretrial detention rates, and less severe charges than cisgender men for the same offenses. Studies indicate that law enforcement is traditionally more reluctant to arrest women due to perceived vulnerability or caretaking roles.
• The Male Penalty Effect: Cisgender men account for the overwhelming majority of arrests and federal criminal charges. They are heavily scrutinized under the assumption of greater capacity for physical violence and threat.
• Selective Chivalry: The leniency granted to cisgender women often disappears if they violate traditional middle-class gender expectations—such as acting aggressively, neglecting children, or engaging in non-traditional crimes—yielding harsher treatment.
3. Intersectional Demographics and Neighborhoods
An individual's identity and surroundings heavily influence police discretion:
• Race and Ethnicity: Even among cisgender individuals, stark racial disparities persist. Cisgender Black and Hispanic individuals are stopped, searched, arrested, and subjected to the use of force at vastly disproportionate rates compared to cisgender white individuals.
• Socioeconomic Status: Living in poverty, experiencing homelessness, or navigating a history of substance abuse greatly correlates with frequent "nuisance" arrests for low-level property crimes, loitering, or public intoxication.
• Neighborhood Context: Arrests occur more frequently in low-income neighborhoods or areas with higher non-white populations, driven by increased patrol density and systemic "focal concerns" regarding community protection.
4. Officer and Departmental Characteristics
The personal profile of the interacting officer alters the trajectory of a police stop:
• Officer Demographics & Experience: Younger, less experienced officers generally display a higher propensity to arrest. Statistical models also show that female and college-educated officers are correlated with a higher likelihood of formal arrest bookings depending on the jurisdiction.
• Implicit Bias: Implicit associations linking specific races, styles of dress, or socioeconomic presentations with criminality heavily direct an officer's sub-second decisions to detain an individual.
