While statistical data consistently highlights that transgender and non-binary students face significantly higher baseline rates of victimization, harassment and bullying among cisgender students in education are heavily influenced by gender composition, sexual orientation, adherence to traditional gender norms, and institutional policy environments.
The primary factors influencing peer aggression, sexual harassment, and social exclusion for cisgender students include:
1. Gender Composition and Academic Environment
The structural gender makeup of an academic setting impacts harassment rates, particularly for cisgender women. Research published in PMC notes that cisgender women enrolled in male-dominated fields (such as engineering) experience significantly higher rates of student-perpetrated harassment (44.2%) and faculty-perpetrated harassment (29.5%) than those in more gender-balanced fields like education or health sciences. However, this variation indicates that institutional culture—not just numerical ratios—plays a key role.
2. Sexual Orientation and "Perceived" Identities
Cisgender students who identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, or queer (LGBQ) face a drastically higher risk of bullying than their cisgender heterosexual peers.
• Data from The Trevor Project indicates that roughly 32% of cisgender LGBQ youth experience in-person bullying.
• Harassment often targets "perceived" identities; cisgender heterosexual students are frequently targeted with homophobic slurs or rumors if peers mistakenly assume they are part of the LGBTQ+ community.
3. Gender Policing and Role Conformity
Bullying heavily relies on "gender policing"—the enforcement of normative masculinity and femininity. Cisgender students whose appearance, voice, hobbies, or mannerisms deviate from traditional cultural expectations face elevated verbal and social harassment. Perpetrators frequently target a student's expression of masculinity or femininity to assert dominance or enforce social hierarchies.
4. Institutional Policies and Safety Contexts
The presence and enforcement of strict school policies dictate the prevalence of bullying for all students, including cisgender youth.
• Lack of Policy Support: Environments with no clear anti-bullying guidelines correlate with the highest rates of in-person bullying (55%) and lower overall school connectedness.
• Systemic Reporting Failures: A major compounding factor is the perceived ineffectiveness of authority figures; among students who report harassment to school staff, a vast majority report that educators fail to address the issue effectively.
5. The Perpetrator-Victim Cycle
Peer-based sexual and gender harassment is frequently driven by a cycle of past victimization. According to data on gender-based bullying, a vast majority of student perpetrators (80% of males and 92% of females) report that they have also been targets of harassment themselves. Furthermore, nearly 44% of harassers do not view their actions as serious, often dismissing harmful behavior or sexual harassment as "misperceived humor".
