Homelessness among men is primarily driven by a combination of severe economic hardship, personal health struggles, and systemic gaps in safety nets. While men make up the majority of the single adult unhoused population (roughly 70% nationally), several specific risk factors heavily influence their transition from housing instability into homelessness. [1, 2] 
## Economic and Structural Factors

* Lack of Affordable Housing: A critical shortage of low-cost rental units leaves low-income men vulnerable when rent prices rise. [3, 4] 
* Job Instability and Low Wages: Sudden job loss, reduced hours, or stagnant minimum wages mean many men cannot recover from unexpected expenses like a car repair or medical bill. [3, 5] 
* Poverty and Weak Safety Nets: Inadequate public benefits and declining general assistance programs provide minimal cushion for unemployed or underemployed single adult men, who often qualify for fewer support programs than families with children. [6] 

## Health and Personal Vulnerabilities

* Substance Use and Addiction: Heavy alcohol and drug use frequently strain personal relationships, cause job loss, and deplete financial resources, acting as a primary catalyst for male housing loss. [5, 7] 
* Untreated Mental Illness: Conditions like depression, anxiety, or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)—common among veterans and civilian men alike—can severely disrupt a person's ability to maintain employment and steady rent payments. [5, 8] 
* Physical Health Crises: Chronic medical conditions or sudden, uninsured health emergencies quickly drain savings and lead to eviction or displacement. [1, 5] 

## Systemic and Institutional Failures

* Inadequate Discharge Planning: A lack of supportive transition programs when leaving institutional settings—such as prisons, jails, hospitals, or mental health facilities—frequently drops vulnerable men directly into homelessness. [1, 9] 
* Social Isolation: Weak community and familial support networks leave single men with fewer places to turn during personal crises compared to individuals living with family structures. [8]
