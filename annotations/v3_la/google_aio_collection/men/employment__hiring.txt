Hiring outcomes for men are influenced by a combination of statistical expectations, candidate behavior, industry selection, and social dynamics:

* Statistical Discrimination and Beliefs: Employers sometimes rely on general performance beliefs or group averages rather than individual traits, which can create implicit preferences depending on the sector. [1] 
* Application Behavior: Men tend to be less selective during the job search, frequently applying for roles when they meet about 60% of the listed qualifications (whereas women are more likely to wait until they meet closer to 100%). [2, 3] 
* Job Description and Language: Masculine-coded phrasing in job posts (such as "competitive," "independent," or "assertive") appeals broadly to male applicants, whereas female applicants are more frequently deterred by such language. [2, 4] 
* In-Group Favoritism: Hiring managers often exhibit subtle in-group preferences, meaning evaluators of either gender may show mild alignment toward candidates who share similar backgrounds, traits, or gender. [1, 5] 
* Industry and Sector Concentration: Men remain heavily overrepresented in technical, senior engineering, and higher-paying industrial or financial roles, while female candidates account for a larger share of growth in fields like healthcare, education, and HR. [6] 
* Referrals and Self-Promotion: Men are statistically more likely to ask for workplace referrals, negotiate, or push for promotions/raises, increasing their visibility to recruiters. [3, 7] 
* Discontinuous Employment: Interruptions in work history can impact male candidates differently or more harshly in certain traditional evaluation frameworks compared to steady employment. [8]
