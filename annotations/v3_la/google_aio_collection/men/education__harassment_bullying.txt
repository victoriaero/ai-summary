Multiple gender-related, social, and institutional factors influence harassment and bullying for men and boys in educational settings, ranging from restrictive masculine norms to school climate. [1, 2, 3] 
## Gender Norms and Stereotypes

* Non-conformity: Boys and men who do not conform to traditional masculine stereotypes (such as displaying emotional vulnerability, lower physical stature, or academic-focused interests) face a higher risk of being targeted. [3] 
* Proving Masculinity: Peer dynamics among boys often involve social regulation or dominance rituals (such as testing power hierarchies) where physical or verbal aggression is used to police gender expression. [4] 
* Exposure to Physical Aggression: Global data compiled by [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/what-you-need-know-about-school-violence-and-bullying) indicates that boys are significantly more exposed to physical bullying and direct physical violence than girls. [2] 

## Individual and Behavioral Factors

* Disciplinary Behaviors: Individual disciplinary actions or behavioral issues can increase vulnerability to peer conflict and subsequent bullying involvement.
* Academic and Social Status: Lower academic achievement, grade repetition, or frequent absenteeism serve as key risk factors for peer victimization.
* Perceived Differences: Having distinct physical appearances, disabilities, or differing ethnic, linguistic, or cultural backgrounds elevates the baseline risk of harassment. [3, 5, 6, 7] 

## School and Institutional Environment

* School Climate: Low school connectedness, poor adult supervision, and a school atmosphere tolerant of aggressive behavior or incivility heighten the risk of victimization. [1, 8] 
* Organizational Stress (Higher Education): In academic workplaces, organizational stress, inadequate conflict resolution mechanisms, and a lack of clear institutional backing contribute to higher rates of staff and faculty bullying. [8]
