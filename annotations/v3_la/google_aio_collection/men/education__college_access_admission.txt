College access and admission for men are shaped by a combination of academic achievement, socioeconomic background, institutional policies, and cultural attitudes. [1, 2, 3] 
## Academic Preparation and Performance

* Reading and Literacy Skills: Early academic gaps—particularly lower reading proficiency scores for boys at age 15 (such as PISA metrics)—significantly predict lower male enrollment rates in tertiary education. [2] 
* High School GPAs and Ranks: Female students on average achieve higher high school grade point averages (GPAs) and class ranks, which gives them an early advantage in standard review processes. [4, 5] 
* Standardized Testing: Men sometimes perform slightly higher on specific standardized admissions tests (like certain math or track-specific entry exams), which can partially offset lower GPAs in test-based or test-optional alternative paths. [5] 

## Socioeconomic and Demographic Factors

* Family Income and Wealth: Socioeconomic status strongly dictates college-going rates regardless of gender; students from the top income quintile (84%) enroll at much higher rates than those from the middle class (72%) or bottom income quintile (63%). [1] 
* Family Structure and Parent Education: Parental education levels and stability (such as growing up in single-parent households or experiencing parental divorce) heavily influence whether male students persist through high school and apply to college. [6] 
* Race and Ethnicity: Intersectionality plays a major role; Black, Hispanic, and Native American men face lower college access and persistence rates compared to White and Asian male peers. [1, 7] 

## Institutional and Policy Influences

* Gender-Balancing Preferences: Because women currently outnumber men in U.S. undergraduate enrollment (roughly a 3:2 ratio or 64% to 36%), some private and liberal arts universities use informal gender-balancing practices or Title IX flexibilities to slightly favor qualified male applicants to maintain campus parity. [3, 4, 8] 
* Targeted Recruitment: Colleges increasingly use specialized outreach to attract men, including promoting outdoor/forestry programs, esports, entrepreneurship competitions, and male-of-color mentorship initiatives. [8] 
* Perceived Value and Opportunity Costs: Higher immediate opportunity costs (such as entering the workforce directly after high school) and a growing cultural devaluing of four-year degrees among certain segments of men lead to lower application rates. [3, 8]
