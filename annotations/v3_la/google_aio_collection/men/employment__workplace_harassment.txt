Organizational conditions, power dynamics, and cultural stereotypes heavily influence workplace harassment for men in employment. [1, 2, 3] 
## Organizational and Environmental Risk Factors
According to the [U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)](https://www.eeoc.gov) guidelines on workplace risk factors, several environmental conditions increase the likelihood of harassment for any worker, including men: [1, 4] 

* Power Disparities: Imbalances where a supervisor or high-value employee holds absolute control over a subordinate's career can lead to coercion or retaliatory harassment. [1, 4, 5] 
* Young or Inexperienced Workforces: Younger male employees may be more vulnerable to hazing, boundary-crossing "banter," or exploitation due to naivety about workplace norms. [4, 5] 
* Customer Service Emphasis: Jobs prioritizing client satisfaction where the "customer is always right" can expose male workers (such as those in hospitality or retail) to unpunished harassment from clients or customers. [4, 5] 
* Isolated Workplaces: Remote sites, late-night shifts, or isolated settings reduce oversight and make reporting difficult. [1, 4, 5] 
* Workplace Culture and Alcohol: Environments that tolerate heavy alcohol consumption or coarsened social discourse see higher rates of disinhibited and inappropriate conduct. [4, 5, 6] 

## Cultural and Gender-Specific Factors for Men
Specific social expectations and workplace dynamics uniquely shape how harassment affects men and why it persists:

* Stigmatization and Stereotypes: Societal expectations that men should "toughen up" or welcome sexual attention create a severe barrier to reporting. [3] 
* Underreporting: Male victims frequently underreport harassment out of fear of embarrassment, professional ridicule, or disbelief from human resources. [2, 3] 
* Non-Conforming Norms: Men who do not conform to traditional masculine stereotypes may experience targeted bullying, verbal hostility, or harassment from peers or superiors seeking to enforce conformity. [1, 4] 
* Perpetrator Dynamics: Harassment against men is not limited to male peers; it can stem from female superiors, co-workers, or clients using power plays, job threats, or unwelcome advances. [2]
