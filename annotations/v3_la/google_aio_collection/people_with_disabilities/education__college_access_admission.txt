Several key factors influence college access and admission for people with disabilities, spanning legal frameworks, disclosure decisions, institutional readiness, and personal advocacy.
## Legal Protections and Self-Advocacy

* Shift in Legal Standards: In K–12 education, the [Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/) requires schools to proactively identify needs and provide customized support. In higher education, the [Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)](https://www.ada.gov/) and Section 504 govern colleges, requiring reasonable accommodations only when students formally request and document them. [1] 
* The Disclosure Dilemma: Disclosing a disability during or after the admissions process is voluntary. While disclosure can help explain grade discrepancies or testing gaps, many students hide invisible or psychiatric disabilities out of fear of social stigma, ableism, or differential treatment. [1, 2, 3, 4] 
* Documentation Hurdles: Colleges typically require up-to-date professional evaluations or medical records (often within the last three years) to approve accommodations. Accessing these costly evaluations can be a major barrier for low-income or unrepresented families. [3, 5, 6] 

## Institutional and Systemic Barriers

* Attitudinal Barriers and Ableism: Entrenched ableism, low expectations from educators, and faculty resistance or lack of training regarding accommodations impede smooth integration. [4, 7] 
* Infrastructural and Resource Limitations: Physical inaccessibility, lack of centralized support services, and scarce assistive technologies restrict students' choices in where they can comfortably study. [5, 8] 
* Siloed Support Systems: Admissions offices, disability resource centers, and compliance departments often operate in isolation, making the transition confusing for incoming students. [4] 

## Intersecting Identities and Support Systems

* Compounded Disadvantage: Students with disabilities who also belong to BIPOC, LGBTQ+, or low-income communities face intersecting and compounding barriers that affect preparation, exposure to resources, and a sense of campus safety. [6] 
* Facilitators of Success: Strong family involvement, early transition planning, peer mentoring, and self-advocacy skills heavily improve a student's ability to enroll and persist through higher education. [9, 10]
