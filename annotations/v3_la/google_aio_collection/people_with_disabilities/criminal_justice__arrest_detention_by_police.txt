Several critical behavioral, systemic, and demographic factors influence the arrest and detention of people with disabilities by police in the criminal justice system. Research from organizations like [The Arc](https://thearc.org/our-initiatives/criminal-justice/) and academic studies indicate that disabled individuals face a significantly higher probability of arrest compared to non-disabled peers. [1, 2, 3] 
## Behavioral and Communication Misinterpretations

* Invisible Disabilities: Intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD) or mild cognitive impairments are often not immediately obvious to police officers, meaning necessary communication accommodations are frequently omitted. [3, 4] 
* Misread Symptoms: Involuntary movements from conditions like Tourette syndrome, speech difficulties (stuttering or slurring), or sensory overload can be incorrectly perceived by officers as non-compliance, aggression, or intoxication. [5] 
* Failure to Comply: Individuals who are d/Deaf, hard of hearing, or experiencing cognitive delays may not hear or mentally process complex verbal commands during high-stress police encounters, leading to charges like resisting arrest. [5] 

## Intersectional and Systemic Disparities

* Racial Disparities: The "disability penalty" disproportionately impacts people of color; data highlights that African-American men with disabilities face vastly higher cumulative arrest rates by early adulthood than white individuals with disabilities. [2] 
* Institutional Pathways: Zero-tolerance policies in schools and healthcare facilities often criminalize mental health crises or behavioral incidents, routing vulnerable individuals directly into police custody instead of medical treatment. [6, 7] 
* Lack of De-escalation Training: Insufficient officer training regarding behavioral health and neurological differences can cause routine encounters to escalate unnecessarily into physical detention or force. [2, 3]
