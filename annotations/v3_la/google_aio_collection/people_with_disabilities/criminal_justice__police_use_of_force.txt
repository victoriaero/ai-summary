Several key behavioral, systemic, and perceptual factors influence police use of force against people with disabilities in the criminal justice system. [1, 2] 
## Misperception of Symptoms and Behaviors

* Behavioral misinterpretation: Officers frequently mistake physical or neurological signs of a disability (such as avoiding eye contact, repetitive movements, lack of verbal responsiveness, or slurred speech) for intoxication, defiance, or noncompliance. [1] 
* Command-and-control reliance: Standard law enforcement training emphasizes immediate compliance with verbal commands. When individuals with intellectual, developmental, or cognitive disabilities (IDD) fail to process or follow these rapid orders, officers often interpret the delay as active resistance and escalate to physical force. [2] 

## Training and Communication Deficits

* Lack of specialized training: Many law enforcement agencies lack mandatory, standardized training regarding intellectual, developmental, or psychiatric disabilities, leaving officers unequipped to recognize or safely de-escalate crises. [3] 
* Communication barriers: Officers often fail to adapt communication strategies for individuals who process language differently or have sensory overload, increasing the likelihood of a volatile encounter. [2, 4] 

## Intersectional Marginalization (Race and Ableism)

* Combined biases: The intersection of racism and ableism significantly heightens the risk of severe violence and arrest for Black and minority individuals with disabilities, who face disproportionate targeting by law enforcement. [2, 5] 
* Criminalization of routine behaviors: Normal activities or manifestations of distress, such as wandering or public meltdowns, are frequently criminalized as "disorderly conduct" or "resisting arrest". [2, 6] 

## Systemic and Legal Gaps

* Absence of comprehensive data: As noted by the [Ruderman Family Foundation](https://rudermanfoundation.org/white_papers/media-coverage-of-law-enforcement-use-of-force-and-disability/), there is a severe deficit in federal, state, and local reporting requirements tracking police use of force against disabled civilians, which obscures the true scale of the issue. [7] 
* Legal and policy limitations: According to analysis in the [Stanford Law Review](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/print/article/policing-under-disability-law/), courts have inconsistently applied Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) to street-level arrests, narrowing protections and failing to mandate operational accommodations during volatile encounters. Many departments also lack explicit written policies for interacting with individuals with IDD. [2, 8, 9]
