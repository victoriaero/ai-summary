Several systemic, social, and institutional factors influence the overrepresentation and heightened rates of incarceration for people with disabilities in the criminal justice system. [1, 2] 
## Deinstitutionalization and Lacking Community Support

* Closure of Care Facilities: [Deinstitutionalization and underfunded community alternatives](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/disabled-behind-bars/) left a severe gap in public health infrastructure, shifting people with psychiatric or intellectual disabilities into the criminal legal system. [1] 
* Scarcity of Resources: A lack of community-based healthcare, stable housing, and mobile crisis response teams means police are often the default responders to behavioral health emergencies. [3, 4] 

## Law Enforcement and Procedural Misinterpretation

* Behavioral Misinterpretation: Behaviors stemming from neurodivergence (such as autism, ADHD, or sensory overload) or psychiatric crises are frequently misread by law enforcement as non-compliance or aggression. [5] 
* Disproportionate Force: People with disabilities face a significantly higher risk of experiencing police violence or arrest during initial encounters. [3, 6] 

## The "School-to-Prison" Pipeline

* Disciplinary Disparities: Children with disabilities are disproportionately disciplined and suspended in elementary and secondary schools, which drastically increases their risk of future dropout and juvenile detention involvement. [7, 8] 
* Unmet Educational Accommodations: A lack of adequate special education resources and unrecognized learning differences create early systemic alienation. [8] 

## Court and Legal System Barriers

* Coerced Plea Bargains: Inaccessible court proceedings, communication barriers, and a lack of disability competency among legal staff often force defendants into unfavorable plea agreements. [3] 
* Flawed Credibility Assessments: Individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD) are sometimes deemed poor witnesses or incapable of navigating complex legal jargon, undermining their self-advocacy in court. [5, 9]
