Maintaining and renewing government disability benefits (such as SSDI, SSI, or Medicaid) depends on a complex mix of financial thresholds, administrative processes, health statuses, and structural policies.
The primary factors that influence benefit retention and renewal for people with disabilities include:
## 1. Financial and Work Thresholds

* Substantial Gainful Activity (SGA): For programs like Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI), earning over a specific monthly limit can trigger a benefit suspension or termination. In 2026, the standard SGA threshold is $1,690 per month (or $2,830 for legally blind individuals). [1, 2] 
* Asset and Resource Limits: Means-tested benefits like Supplemental Security Income (SSI) require recipients to stay under strict asset caps (typically $2,000 for individuals). Exceeding this via savings, unexpected inheritances, or gifts will result in a loss of eligibility unless shielded in a specialized vehicle like an [ABLE Account](https://www.ssa.gov/pubs/EN-05-10029.pdf) or a Special Needs Trust. [3, 4] 
* In-Kind and Household Income: Receiving non-cash assistance (such as a family member paying for food or rent) or experiencing a change in marital status can alter financial calculations and reduce or terminate benefits. [4, 5] 

## 2. Medical Evidence and Health Progress

* Continuing Disability Reviews (CDRs): Government agencies periodically review cases to evaluate whether a recipient's medical condition has improved. The frequency of these reviews varies based on the likelihood of recovery (ranging from every 6–18 months to every 7 years). [2] 
* Objective Medical Documentation: Ongoing retention heavily relies on regular physician documentation. A lack of current, objective medical files or failing to follow prescribed treatments can lead an examiner to determine that the beneficiary is no longer disabled. [6, 7, 8] 
* Age Milestones: Turning 18 years old triggers a mandatory adult medical re-evaluation for childhood SSI recipients, which often results in a high rate of disqualification. Reaching full retirement age automatically transitions SSDI recipients over to standard retirement benefits. [5, 9] 

## 3. Administrative Burdens and System Obstacles

* Staffing and System Delays: Sweeping agency overhauls and staffing cuts create severe communication loops, causing beneficiaries to miss crucial renewal windows due to dropped phone calls or unprocessed paperwork. [10, 11] 
* Complex Recertification Paperwork: The administrative burden of completing long renewal forms, responding to time-sensitive mailings, and tracking strict deadlines is a major reason individuals experience "administrative churn" (losing benefits despite still being medically and financially eligible). [12, 13] 
* Socioeconomic Barriers: Beneficiaries facing housing instability, language barriers, or a lack of reliable internet access face significantly higher hurdles when trying to submit digital or paper recertifications. [10, 11]
