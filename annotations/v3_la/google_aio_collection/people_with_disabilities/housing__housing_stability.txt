Severe cost burdens, physical inaccessibility, and systemic discrimination are the primary factors that influence housing stability for people with disabilities. [1, 2, 3] 
## Financial Constraints and Affordability

* Fixed Incomes: Many individuals rely on Supplemental Security Income (SSI) or low wages, which often fail to cover rising market rents. National averages show rent often far exceeds monthly SSI benefits. [1, 2, 4] 
* High Cost-Burden: Millions of disabled renters spend over 30%—and frequently over 50%—of their income on housing and utilities, leaving little for health care or food. [1, 5, 6] 
* Gaps in Assistance: The vast majority of low-income disabled households receive no federal housing assistance due to funding shortages and long waitlists. [3, 4] 

## Physical Accessibility and Design

* Shortage of Accessible Units: Fewer than 5% of homes are accessible for moderate mobility needs, and less than 1% accommodate wheelchairs. [2] 
* Costly Modifications: Finding or retrofitting a home with ramps, widened doorways, or universal design features often requires expensive renovations that landlords or tenants cannot afford. [6, 7] 

## Systemic and Structural Barriers

* Discrimination: Disability-related complaints make up over half of all fair housing discrimination reports, including denied reasonable accommodations or inaccessible properties. [3] 
* Caregiver Disruptions: Sudden changes or aging in a primary caregiver network can destabilize housing for individuals with developmental or intellectual disabilities. [4] 
* Compounding Marginalization: Disabled people of color—particularly Black and Hispanic renters—face disproportionately higher rates of housing insecurity and eviction risk. [1]
