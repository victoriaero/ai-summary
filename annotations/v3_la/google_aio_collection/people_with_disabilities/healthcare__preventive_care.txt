Several interconnected physical, systemic, financial, and social factors influence access to preventive care for people with disabilities.
## Physical and Environmental Barriers

* Inaccessible facilities: Buildings with stairs, heavy doors, narrow hallways, or lacking ramps prevent easy entry.
* Inappropriate medical equipment: Exam tables that do not adjust in height, heavy or non-adjustable mammography equipment, and scales lacking wheelchair accessibility stop proper screenings. [1, 2] 
* Transportation challenges: A lack of accessible public transit, high costs for specialized transit, or distance from clinics restrict regular checkups. [3, 4, 5] 

## Provider and Communication Factors

* Provider knowledge gaps: Clinicians often lack specialized training in treating specific disabilities or recognizing secondary health conditions. [2, 4] 
* Negative attitudes: Implicit bias or service refusal by healthcare staff can deter patients from seeking routine care. [3, 5, 6] 
* Communication barriers: Information not provided in braille, large print, sign language, or easy-to-read formats prevents informed preventive choices. [4] 

## Financial and Systemic Factors

* Cost and insurance: High out-of-pocket costs, lack of insurance coverage, or limited Medicaid/insurance reimbursement protocols restrict the time clinicians can spend with patients. [2, 3] 
* Short appointment times: Standard visit lengths do not accommodate the extra time needed for safe transfers, communication, or examinations. [4] 

## Social and Personal Factors

* Caregiver influence: Unpaid family members or paid support workers heavily shape whether an individual attends preventive appointments. [7, 8] 
* Perception of health: A lack of apparent symptoms often leads patients or providers to skip preventive screenings. [9]
