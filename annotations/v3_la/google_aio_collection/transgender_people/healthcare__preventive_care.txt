Several key factors influence the delivery and use of preventive care for transgender individuals, ranging from systemic provider training gaps to state-level policies. [1, 2] 
## Provider Knowledge and Education Gaps

* Insufficient Training: Medical and nursing school curricula often lack comprehensive instruction on transgender health and gender-affirming care. [1] 
* Lack of Knowledgeable Clinicians: A shortage of providers who understand transgender-specific health needs remains a major barrier to routine screenings and checkups. [3] 

## Discrimination and Stigma

* Mistreatment in Settings: Elevated rates of discrimination, harassment, and denial of care discourage patients from seeking routine wellness visits. [1, 4] 
* Cisnormativity and Cissexism: When clinics and intake systems assume everyone is cisgender or rigidly couple gender identity with anatomy, patient discomfort and distress can lead to delayed or avoided care. [4, 5] 

## State Policies and Legal Environments

* Policy Disparities: Research shows wider gaps in wellness visits, vaccinations, and routine screenings between transgender and cisgender adults living in gender-nonaffirming states compared to affirming states. [2] 
* Legal Protections: Access to supportive policy environments heavily influences whether patients feel safe establishing long-term relationships with a primary care provider. [2] 

## Insurance and Financial Barriers

* Coverage Gaps: Inadequate insurance coverage or high out-of-pocket costs limit access to regular primary care, laboratory tests, and specialized preventive services. [4, 6] 
* Socioeconomic Factors: Broader social determinants—such as housing instability, transportation hurdles, and lower income—indirectly reduce preventive care utilization. [3] 

## Clinical and Anatomical Complexity

* Anatomy-Based Screenings: Preventive screenings depend on remaining organs rather than gender identity (e.g., individuals with a cervix or breast tissue need standard cancer screenings regardless of gender-affirming procedures or hormone use). [7] 
* Monitoring Hormone Therapy: Gender-affirming hormone therapy requires ongoing oversight for specific risk factors like cardiovascular health, lipid changes, and bone density, which shapes the scope of routine preventive evaluations. [7, 8]
