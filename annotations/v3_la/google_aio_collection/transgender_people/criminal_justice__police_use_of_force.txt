============================================================

Several systemic, institutional, and demographic factors influence the likelihood and frequency of police use of force against transgender and gender-diverse individuals within the criminal justice system. According to comprehensive data from organizations like the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU), the Williams Institute, and the National Center for Transgender Equality, transgender people experience significantly higher rates of physical force and police misconduct compared to cisgender individuals. [1, 2] 
The primary factors driving these interactions and the subsequent use of force include:
## 1. Intersectional Subjection (Race and Class)
The intersection of racial bias and transphobia heavily compounds the risk of physical violence by law enforcement. [3] 

* Racial Profiling: Black transgender and nonbinary individuals face the highest risk of experiencing physical force by law enforcement. Reports from the Williams Institute indicate that up to 61% of Black transgender respondents have experienced police mistreatment. [4, 5] 
* Economic Vulnerabilities: Transgender individuals face disproportionate rates of homelessness, poverty, and unemployment. Being visible in public spaces due to a lack of housing often subjects them to heightened police surveillance, frequent stops, and aggressive anti-loitering enforcement. [6, 7, 8] 

## 2. Over-Policing and Profiling of Sex Work
Law enforcement officers frequently utilize gendered, transphobic stereotypes to justify profiling transgender individuals as sex workers, a phenomenon often described as "Walking While Trans". [3, 9] 

* Research published in the Journal of Interpersonal Violence indicates that actual or perceived engagement in sex work is one of the strongest predictors of police interaction, verbal harassment, and physical or sexual violence. [10] 
* Transgender sex workers or those falsely profiled as such are highly vulnerable to carceral violence, with some being subjected to extortion or physical abuse to avoid arrest. [8, 10] 

## 3. Deviance Perceptions and Gender Nonconformity
Police officers often strictly enforce binary and conventional gender norms, framing gender nonconformity as "deceptive" or suspicious. [11, 12] 

* A landmark study analyzing data from California’s Racial and Identity Profiling Act found that individuals perceived by officers as transgender were searched and arrested at substantially higher rates than cisgender individuals, despite having identical rates of contraband discovery. [13] 
* This institutional bias alters the trajectory of standard encounters, escalating routine traffic or pedestrian stops into high-scrutiny, punitive events where physical force is more likely to be deployed. [13] 

## 4. Institutional Gaps and Lack of Departmental Policies
A widespread deficit in formalized, trans-sensitive law enforcement protocols directly correlates with escalated custody environments. [14] 

* Comprehensive audits of major police departments by Advocates for Trans Equality (A4TE) reveal that a majority of departments lack explicit guidance on objective search procedures, respectful communication, or non-binary recognition. [6, 15] 
* Invasive gender-determination searches or forced removal of gender-affirming appearance items often serve as catalysts for escalation and non-consensual physical contact. [2, 12] 

## 5. Carceral Vulnerabilities and Custody Dynamics
Once processed into jails, prisons, or immigration detention facilities, the risks of physical and sexual abuse escalate drastically. [14] 

* Incarceration facilities frequently place transgender inmates based on their assigned sex at birth rather than their gender identity, exposing them to heightened violence from both staff and other inmates. [16, 17] 
* According to the Vera Institute of Justice, a notable percentage of incarcerated transgender people report facing direct verbal discrimination and physical harm from carceral staff. [18]
