Patient safety for transgender individuals in healthcare is shaped by provider knowledge, institutional systems, communication practices, and socioeconomic factors. [1, 2] 
## Provider Knowledge and Cultural Competence

* Lack of Training: Insufficient formal education in medical and nursing schools regarding transgender health leaves many clinicians unequipped to provide competent care. [3, 4] 
* Patient Burden: Patients often have to educate their own healthcare providers, which delays proper treatment and erodes trust. [4, 5] 
* Implicit Bias and Discrimination: Provider insensitivity, misgendering, verbal harassment, and refusal of treatment create hostile environments that compromise safety. [6, 7] 
* Fear and Avoidance: Anticipated or past discrimination leads many transgender people to delay or completely avoid necessary routine and preventive care. [6, 7, 8] 

## Health System and Infrastructure Barriers

* Rigid Electronic Health Records (EHRs): Systems that fail to record chosen names, correct pronouns, or accurate sex assigned at birth separate from current anatomy cause administrative errors, lost medical files, and repeated misgendering. [4, 9, 10] 
* Anatomy vs. Identity Confusion: Misunderstanding that preventive screenings (such as for cervical, breast, or prostate cancer) depend on existing organs rather than gender identity can lead to missed screenings or clinical errors. [1, 11] 
* Lab Reference Ranges: Inability of diagnostic systems to correctly interpret lab results affected by gender-affirming hormone therapy can result in misdiagnoses or inappropriate clinical responses. [1] 

## Communication and Clinical Safety

* Withholding Information: Patient discomfort or fear of mistreatment prevents individuals from sharing complete gender histories or sexual health information. [1, 12] 
* Missed Interventions: A lack of open dialogue increases the risk of missed diagnoses, unaddressed adverse drug interactions, or overlooked safety checks (such as pregnancy screenings prior to diagnostic imaging for transgender men). [1] 

## Structural and Socioeconomic Factors

* Financial Constraints: High rates of uninsurance, underemployment, and insurance coverage denials for routine or transition-related care limit access to monitored medical supervision.
* Geographic Disparities: A severe shortage of local specialists forces patients to travel long distances, disproportionately affecting rural residents.
* Intersectionality: Layered discrimination involving race, socioeconomic class, and geographic location amplifies health disparities and safety risks, particularly for transgender people of color. [3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 13]
