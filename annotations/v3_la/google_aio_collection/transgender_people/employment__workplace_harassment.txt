Several structural, cultural, and interpersonal factors heavily influence workplace harassment and discrimination against transgender employees. [1, 2] 
## Cultural and Organizational Drivers

* Cisnormativity and Role Incongruence: Workplaces structured around rigid, traditional expectations of biological sex and binary gender norms often treat transgender expression as "unnatural" or disruptive, fueling interpersonal conflict and hostility. [1, 2, 3] 
* Masculinity Contest Cultures: Environments that prioritize hyper-masculinity, aggressive competition, and rigid alpha-male or patriarchal dynamics exhibit significantly higher rates of anti-transgender harassment and policing of gender presentation. [1] 
* Lack of Explicit Policies: The absence of clear, enforced corporate guidelines protecting gender identity—coupled with superficial diversity declarations rather than active backing—leaves employees vulnerable to unmanaged bias. [1, 4] 

## Intersectional and Demographic Vulnerabilities

* Race and Ethnicity: Transgender people of color face compounded marginalization, reporting drastically higher frequencies of harassment, hiring bias, and economic precarity compared to white peers. [5, 6] 
* Age and Income: Younger workers (under 35) and lower-income earners (under $50,000 annually) report disproportionately higher rates of experiencing severe workplace hostility and mistreatment. [5] 
* Visibility and Transition Status: Employees who are actively undergoing a physical or social transition at work, or whose gender expression does not conform to traditional binary standards, are more frequently targeted for scrutiny, deadnaming, and misgendering. [7, 8, 9] 

## Legal and Administrative Climate

* Shifting Legal Enforcement: Ambiguity or rollbacks in federal and state regulatory guidance regarding protections (such as Equal Employment Opportunity Commission protocols or Title VII interpretations) can embolden bad behavior and create deep anxiety around reporting retaliation. [3, 10]
