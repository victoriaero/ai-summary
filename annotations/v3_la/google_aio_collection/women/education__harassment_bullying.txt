Harassment and bullying for women in education—whether students or educators—are influenced by a complex interplay of cultural, institutional, and interpersonal factors.
## Cultural and Societal Norms

* Patriarchal Hierarchies: Deep-rooted cultural norms and gender stereotypes often police traditional behavior, reinforcing power structures that devalue or marginalize women. [1, 2] 
* Appearance and Cyber Dynamics: According to [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org), girls and young women face high rates of psychological bullying, heavily driven by appearance-based judgements and online cyberbullying. [2] 
* Tolerant School Climates: Environments that implicitly tolerate sexist comments, sexual jokes, or gender-based microaggressions create a baseline culture where harassment can thrive. [3] 

## Institutional and Environmental Factors

* Lack of Adult Intervention: Educators or administrators frequently fail to intervene against early verbal or identity-based harassment, which tacitly condones discriminatory attitudes. [1] 
* High-Pressure Workplaces: For female educators and staff, the high-stress, under-resourced nature of academic institutions can foster toxic blame cultures where workplace bullying becomes commonplace. [4] 
* Deficient Curricula: Many sex education and professional training programs fail to explicitly address sexual harassment or power dynamics, missing crucial opportunities to build communication and protective boundaries. [3] 

## Interpersonal and Group Dynamics

* Classroom Status Hierarchies: Peer groups with rigid social hierarchies or norms that accept aggressive behavior increase the vulnerability of marginalized individuals. [3] 
* Identity-Based Targeting: Intersectionality plays a significant role; women and girls who face overlapping biases regarding race, sexuality, or physical ability experience heightened risks of targeted harassment. [5]
