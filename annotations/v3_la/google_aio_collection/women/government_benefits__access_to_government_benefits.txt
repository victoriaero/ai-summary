Multiple systemic, economic, and structural factors influence women's access to and receipt of government benefits.
## Economic and Occupational Factors

* The Gender Wage Gap: Women earn less on average than men, which reduces their lifetime earnings, contributions to social insurance programs, and overall benefit payouts. [1, 2] 
* Occupational Segregation: Women are disproportionately concentrated in low-wage, part-time, or informal sector jobs that often lack employer-provided benefits or coverage under traditional social insurance schemes. [1, 2, 3] 
* The Gender Wealth Gap: Women possess fewer liquid assets and savings, making them more vulnerable to economic shocks and increasing reliance on means-tested safety net programs. [1] 

## Caregiving and Social Expectations

* Unpaid Care Work: Women perform over 75% of unpaid domestic and caregiving labor globally, restricting their continuous workforce participation and reducing eligibility for employment-tied benefits. [3, 4] 
* Lack of Care Infrastructure: The scarcity of affordable childcare and paid family leave systems forces many women to step out of the workforce, lowering their long-term benefit accrual. [1] 

## Systemic and Administrative Barriers

* Program Design and Implementation: Complex application processes, rigid asset limits, and restrictive work requirements in welfare programs (such as TANF in the U.S.) can disproportionately penalize primary caregivers. [5] 
* Intersectional Discrimination: Women of color, LGBTQ+ women, and women with disabilities face compounded systemic hurdles, experiencing higher rates of poverty and heightened barriers when navigating public assistance programs. [1, 2] 

## Political Representation and Policymaking

* Representation in Government: Higher numbers of women in parliaments, cabinets, and local governments positively correlate with broader prioritization of social protection, healthcare, and education funding. [6]
