Several key demographic, situational, and systemic factors influence police use of force involving women as both subjects and officers in the criminal justice system.
## Factors Influencing Force Against Women (As Subjects/Civilians)

* Type of Police Contact and Environment: Encounters that begin as routine traffic stops or domestic dispute calls can rapidly escalate. Research highlighted by the [Prison Policy Initiative](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/blog/2019/05/14/policingwomen/) notes that police contacts and use of force against women have grown substantially over recent decades, driven in part by rising arrests for drug offenses and increased proactive policing. [1] 
* Intersectional and Racial Disparities: Race heavily intersects with gender during police interactions. Data shows that Black women face higher rates of traffic stops, searches, and subsequent arrests compared to white women, compounding their vulnerability to escalated police actions and use of force. [1] 
* Socioeconomic and Health Vulnerabilities: Marginalized women—including those experiencing poverty, mental illness, homelessness, or substance use disorders—frequently encounter heightened levels of coercive interventions and force during police-public interactions. [2] 

## Factors Influencing Force By Women (As Law Enforcement Officers)

* De-escalation and Communication Styles: Studies on police behavior indicate that female officers generally rely less on physical or excessive force. They frequently utilize communication, empathy, and de-escalation strategies to diffuse volatile situations, resulting in fewer citizen complaints and civil lawsuits. [3, 4, 5] 
* Departmental Culture and Representation: Agencies with higher percentages of female representation often cultivate organizational changes, such as enhanced community-oriented policing and victim-centered units, which alter the operational climate and lower overall aggressive tactical approaches. [6] 
* Occupational Stress and Burnout: Female officers report distinct internal stressors, including organizational bias, underestimation, or lack of supervisory backing. High levels of fatigue or burnout can impact officer wellness and daily operational stress responses. [7, 8]
