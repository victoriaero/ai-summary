Several key economic, social, and systemic factors influence whether women receive recommended preventive healthcare services. [1] 
## Insurance and Financial Status

* Cost and Coverage: Out-of-pocket costs and lack of insurance remain major barriers. While laws like the Affordable Care Act eliminate cost-sharing for many preventive services, uninsured women use preventive care at significantly lower rates. [1, 2, 3] 
* Income Disparities: Low-income women report higher rates of fair or poor health and face greater hurdles like transportation limits and unaffordable copays. [1, 3] 

## Access to Providers

* Primary Care Shortages: A lack of nearby primary care physicians or women's health clinics reduces routine screening rates. [2] 
* Provider Gender: Studies show women are often more likely to receive gender-specific preventive screenings (such as mammograms and Pap smears) when treated by female physicians. [4] 
* Regular Source of Care: Having a regular primary care provider drastically increases the likelihood of receiving timely vaccinations and cancer screenings. [3] 

## Social and Personal Demands

* Caregiving Responsibilities: Women frequently prioritize the health needs of children or aging family members over their own, leading them to neglect routine preventive visits. [5] 
* Time Constraints: Busy work schedules and a perceived lack of symptoms cause many women to skip annual wellness exams. [5] 
* Health Literacy: Low public awareness regarding which preventive services are fully covered at no cost can prevent patients from scheduling appointments. [2] 

## Systemic and Demographic Factors

* Demographics and Identity: Disabled, low-income, and LGBT+ women report higher rates of unmet health needs and lower overall satisfaction or perceived safety within the medical system. [3] 
* Discrimination and Bias: Past negative experiences or perceived discrimination from healthcare providers can discourage marginalized women from seeking ongoing preventive care. [1]
