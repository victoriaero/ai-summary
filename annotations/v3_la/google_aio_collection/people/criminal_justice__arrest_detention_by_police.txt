Police decisions to arrest or detain individuals are influenced by a combination of legal standards, situational dynamics, officer characteristics, and neighborhood context. [1] 
## Legal Standards

* Reasonable Suspicion: Required for a temporary investigative detention (a stop and frisk). It relies on specific, articulable facts suggesting a person is connected to ongoing or imminent criminal activity. [2, 3] 
* Probable Cause: Required to make a formal arrest. It demands a "totality of the circumstances" showing a strong, reasonable probability that the suspect committed a crime. [2] 
* Seriousness of the Offense: The severity of the suspected crime heavily dictates whether an officer moves from a brief stop to an arrest or continued booking. [4, 5] 

## Situational and Incident-Level Factors

* Nature of the Incident: Research from the [Office of Justice Programs](https://www.ojp.gov/library/publications/understanding-police-decisions-arrest-impact-situational-officer-and) shows that incident-level characteristics account for the largest share of variation in arrest decisions. Arrests are often more likely during property calls, self-initiated encounters, or incidents involving victims present. [1, 6] 
* Suspect Behavior: Active resistance, attempts to flee, or aggressive behavior toward officers escalate the likelihood of physical restraint and formal arrest. [4] 

## Officer Discretion and Characteristics

* Individual Propensity: Studies indicate that individual officer tendencies—driven by factors such as years of experience, age, and personal arrest thresholds—significantly sway arrest rates. [7] 
* Demographics and Background: Research notes variations in discretion based on officer attributes, such as education level or background, though findings vary by department and jurisdiction. [1, 6] 

## Neighborhood and Extralegal Factors

* Community Demographics: Studies analyzing neighborhood context find that arrest and booking rates can differ based on local racial demographics or neighborhood socioeconomic factors, pointing to persistent systemic or extralegal disparities. [1, 8]
