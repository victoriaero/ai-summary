Multiple individual, organizational, and systemic factors influence whether people receive or provide preventive care in healthcare.
## Individual Factors

* Demographics and Socioeconomic Status: Age, income level, education, and race or ethnicity heavily shape awareness and utilization. Younger adults or those with lower income often use fewer preventive services. [1, 2] 
* Personal Health Knowledge: Patients who understand the value of healthy diets, vaccines, and early screenings are more proactive. [2] 
* Lifestyle and Beliefs: Personal health behaviors, cultural backgrounds, and perceived personal risk affect a person's willingness to engage in preventive measures. [3] 

## Organizational Factors

* Provider Constraints: Doctors and nurses often face a lack of time and heavy workloads, limiting their ability to deliver thorough preventive counseling. [3, 4] 
* Practice Infrastructure: Clinics need proper equipment, reminder systems, and efficient layout planning to support routine wellness visits. [2, 3] 
* Competing Priorities: Treating acute symptoms often takes precedence over long-term prevention during a standard medical appointment. [3, 4] 

## Systemic and Economic Factors

* Health Insurance and Cost: Access to affordable or no-cost coverage for screenings and immunizations greatly increases patient participation. [3, 5] 
* Resource Allocation: Broader healthcare systems frequently prioritize funding and resources for acute disease management over primary and primordial prevention. [4] 
* Policy and Legal Context: Government regulations and national public health campaigns define the scope of preventive services provided. [3]
