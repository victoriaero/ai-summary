Harassment and bullying in educational settings are influenced by a combination of individual traits, family dynamics, school environments, and broader societal norms. [1] 
## Individual Characteristics

* Perceived Differences: Targets are often chosen based on perceived differences in appearance, race, gender, background, sexual orientation, or disability. [2] 
* Behavior and Self-Concept: Low emotional or academic self-concept can increase vulnerability, while disciplinary issues or impulsivity are linked to higher rates of perpetration. [3, 4] 
* Gender Dynamics: Boys are more frequently involved in direct physical and verbal bullying, whereas girls experience higher rates of indirect psychological, relational, and cyberbullying. [2, 5] 

## Family and Social Dynamics

* Parental Support: Inconsistent discipline, lack of emotional support, or uninvolved parenting styles correlate with an increased risk of a student becoming a bully. [6] 
* Family Socioeconomic Status: Lower family income and unstable or separated household structures are often correlated with higher vulnerability to victimization. [7] 

## School Environment and Context

* Supervision and Spaces: Bullying peaks most often in unstructured, low-supervision areas like hallways, cafeterias, and playgrounds.
* School Climate: A negative school climate, lack of clear staff training, or poor intervention by bystanders allows aggressive behavior to persist.
* Digital Access: Unmonitored or excessive screen time and access to social media expand traditional conflicts into cyberbullying. [7, 8, 9, 10]
