Workplace harassment for non-Latino employees is driven by general organizational risk factors, power imbalances, and demographic dynamics identified by the [U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)](https://www.eeoc.gov/chart-risk-factors-harassment-and-responsive-strategies). [1, 2] 
## Key Organizational Risk Factors

* Power Disparities: Hierarchical structures where supervisors hold absolute control can foster a fear-based environment that discourages reporting. [1, 3] 
* Homogenous or Isolated Workforces: Lack of diversity or isolated physical work environments can amplify interpersonal tensions and reduce oversight. [1, 2] 
* Monotonous or Repetitive Tasks: Workplace boredom or repetitive duties can contribute to disengagement and lax cultural norms among staff. [1, 2] 
* Decentralized Leadership: Remote leadership or a lack of direct supervision often reduces accountability and leaves employees without accessible support channels. [1, 2] 

## Interpersonal and Cultural Influences

* Coarsened Social Discourse: External political or societal tensions can bleed into the workplace, resulting in charged or hostile daily interactions. [1, 2] 
* Young or Inexperienced Workforces: Younger or first-time employees may experience higher vulnerability due to unfamiliarity with professional boundaries and reporting protocols. [1, 2] 
* Customer-Facing Roles: High pressure to prioritize client satisfaction can lead to an over-tolerance of inappropriate behavior from customers or third parties. [1, 2] 
* Sub-Demographic and Intersectional Pressures: Dynamics like same-culture bias or personal insecurities among peers and supervisors can cross demographic lines and manifest as targeted bullying. [3]
