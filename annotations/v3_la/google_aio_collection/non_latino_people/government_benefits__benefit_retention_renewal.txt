============================================================

Benefit retention and renewal for non-Latino populations are heavily influenced by structural, socioeconomic, and system-design factors. While Latino and immigrant populations frequently face specific "chilling effects" tied to language barriers, public charge rules, and immigration status fears, research shows that non-Latino recipients—particularly low-income White, Black, and Native American individuals—are primarily impacted by administrative burden, socioeconomic volatility, disability status, and state-level policy variations. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] 
The core factors influencing their benefit retention include:
## 1. Administrative Burden and Procedural Hassles
The primary driver of coverage loss (or "churn") for eligible individuals across all demographics is administrative complexity. [9, 10] 

* Learning and Compliance Costs: Beneficiaries must navigate confusing paperwork, verify income, and track recertification timelines. A study by the [Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (CBPP)](https://www.cbpp.org/research/food-assistance/administrative-burden-and-procedural-denials-in-the-supplemental-nutrition) noted that nearly 45% of SNAP recertification denials for White applicants were due to procedural failures (such as missing a mandatory interview or lacking paperwork) rather than actual income ineligibility. [11, 12, 13] 
* Frequency of Redetermination: State policies dictating how often a person must renew fundamentally impact retention. Shorter certification windows (e.g., checking eligibility every 6 months instead of 12 months) significantly increase the likelihood that an eligible person drops off the program due to administrative fatigue. [9, 13, 14] 
* Staff Workloads and System Capacity: High case manager workloads correlate directly with longer wait times, processing delays, and higher procedural errors. [3] 

## 2. Physical and Mental Health Status

* Chronic Illness and Disabilities: Individuals with severe chronic illnesses or disabilities face higher barriers to maintaining benefits. Navigating physical offices, attending mandatory in-person appointments, or managing complex online portals creates an acute compliance and psychological burden for vulnerable individuals. [8, 15] 
* Healthcare Utilization as a Retention Anchor: Conversely, individuals who actively use their benefits are much more likely to retain them. Research from [National Institutes of Health (NIH)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11069080/) data reveals that Medicaid enrollees in the top quartile of healthcare utilization (those frequently seeing doctors or filling prescriptions) are significantly less likely to lose coverage at renewal, largely because the immediate need keeps them engaged with the system. [16] 

## 3. Socioeconomic Volatility and Income "Churn"

* Fluctuating Earnings: Low-wage workers, particularly those in the service, seasonal, or gig economies, experience frequent shifts in hours and income. Small, temporary bumps in income can push a household slightly above strict eligibility thresholds, causing them to lose benefits like SNAP or Medicaid. [8, 9, 13] 
* Family Structure and Residential Instability: Changes in household size, marital status, or moving to a new address heavily disrupt renewals. If a state relies on paper notices sent via mail, housing-insecure or highly mobile individuals routinely miss critical renewal deadlines simply because the paperwork went to a previous address. [13, 17] 

## 4. Technological Access and Infrastructure

* Digital System Optimization: Increasingly, states utilize online portals and mobile apps for benefit renewals. Non-Latino populations in rural areas or low-income urban tracts are deeply affected by the quality of this technology. Slow application-page loading speeds, poor mobile optimization, and lack of broadband access prevent users from completing forms or uploading required income verification. [3, 18, 19] 

## 5. Racial Equity and Structural Policy Intersections

* Progressive vs. Regressive State Policies: While non-Latino Black and Native American families face higher statistical rates of poverty and benefit dependence due to systemic economic inequalities, structural policy choices dictate whether they keep those benefits. States that implement auto-renewals (ex parte), use integrated databases to share data between SNAP and Medicaid, and offer continuous 12-month eligibility see significantly higher retention rates across all racial demographics. [2, 6, 7, 11, 20] 
* Work Requirements: Programs that tie retention to community engagement or work reporting requirements—such as SNAP's ABAWD (Able-Bodied Adults Without Dependents) rules or localized Medicaid waivers—introduce severe compliance hurdles. Confusion over reporting systems frequently leads to massive benefit drops among eligible people who actually qualify for exemptions but fail to prove it. [13, 21, 22]
