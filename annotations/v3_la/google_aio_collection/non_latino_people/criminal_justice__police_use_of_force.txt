Several systemic, situational, and environmental factors influence police use of force involving non-Latino individuals (including White and non-Hispanic Black populations) within the criminal justice system. [1, 2] 
## Situational and Behavioral Dynamics

* Suspect Conduct and Resistance: Research indicates that active physical resistance, perceived threats, or the presence of a weapon significantly increase the probability of an officer utilizing higher levels of force.
* Encounter Context: Incidents that begin with proactive police-initiated contact (such as investigatory stops or high-crime response calls) carry a higher baseline risk for escalation compared to calls for service generated directly by citizen requests.

## Neighborhood and Environmental Context

* Community Crime Rates: According to social disorganization and conflict theories outlined by criminologists, high-crime neighborhoods experience more intensive police deployment and surveillance. This elevated presence naturally increases the overall volume of police-civilian interactions and potential use-of-force incidents. [1] 
* Economic Disadvantage: Structural factors such as concentrated neighborhood poverty and local social vulnerability correlate with higher rates of police interventions. [1, 2] 

## Officer Perceptions and Implicit Bias

* Implicit Stereotypes: Studies in social psychology show that subconscious racial and behavioral associations can affect how quickly an officer perceives a threat or decides to use force during rapidly evolving encounters. [3] 
* Perceived Threat Assessment: Officers operate under high-stress conditions where split-second cognitive and perceptual distortions can alter their response thresholds during hostile or armed confrontations. [4]
