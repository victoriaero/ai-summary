Harassment and bullying among non-Latino students in educational settings are influenced by a combination of physical appearance, social environment, family dynamics, and demographic or cultural traits. [1, 2, 3] 
## Key Influencing Factors

* Physical Appearance and Body Image: According to data from the [National Center for Education Statistics](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/a10/bullying-electronic-bullying), physical appearance is the single most common catalyst reported by students (about 33% of bullied youth), covering features like body shape, weight, or face. [2] 
* Perceived Differences and Status Quo Deviations: Students perceived as differing from the majority or local social norms—whether in academic performance, weight, or behavioral mannerisms—face heightened vulnerability. [4] 
* Family Structure and Parent Support: For non-Latino groups such as White and Black youth, household stability and parental involvement play a massive protective or risk role. For instance, living with two biological parents and strong parental communication lower the risk of victimization, whereas poor parent-school communication or emotional isolation increase the likelihood of being targeted or acting as a bully. [1] 
* Peer Group Dynamics and Isolation: Social isolation increases a student's risk of being a victim of bullying, while negative peer associations (such as befriending delinquent or disruptive groups) significantly elevate the risk of a student perpetrating bullying. [1, 5] 
* Religion, Culture, and Visible Attire: For students from specific non-Latino minority backgrounds—such as Jewish, Muslim, Arab, Sikh, or Asian American students—factors like visible religious attire (e.g., hijabs, kippahs, patkas), faith practices, and rising social or political events heavily trigger bias-based harassment. [6] 
* Immigrant Status and Generational Gap: Immigrant youth and first- or second-generation students (such as among Asian American or other non-Latino immigrant populations) report higher rates of social exclusion and verbal or cyber harassment compared to native-born peers. [7, 8] 
* School Climate and Support Systems: Unsupportive school environments, weak institutional intervention, neighborhood poverty, and exposure to community violence amplify the risk of bullying across all student demographics. [6, 9]
