## Individual and Situational Factors

* Citizen and Officer Demographics: Studies analyzing police interactions (such as research from the [Dallas Police Department](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21533687251314137)) show that male citizens and male officers correlate with increased force severity in encounters involving Latino individuals. [3] 
* Perceived Physical Size: Data from programs like the [New York Police Department Stop, Question, and Frisk](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4718646/) show that Latino men perceived by officers as larger, taller, or heavier face significantly higher relative odds of physical force, frisks, and searches. [4] 
* Armed Status and Incident Type: Situational variables—such as whether a suspect is armed, displaying a weapon, or involved in a high-intensity crime in progress—strongly predict higher levels of deployed force. [3] 

## Systemic and Structural Influences

* Minority Threat and Conflict Theories: Criminological frameworks suggest that marginalized or non-White populations in majority-minority or high-crime areas often encounter larger police presences and heightened social control due to perceived safety threats. [1] 
* Immigration Enforcement and Local Trust: Local policies tying local law enforcement to federal immigration enforcement (such as immigration checks in jails) heighten fear within Latino communities, reducing cooperation and trust. According to findings from LatinoJustice PRLDEF, a majority of surveyed Latinos express concern over unjust deadly force and racial profiling. [5, 6, 7]
