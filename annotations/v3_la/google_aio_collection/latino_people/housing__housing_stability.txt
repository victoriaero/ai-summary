Housing stability for Latino individuals and families is shaped by a complex mix of economic, systemic, cultural, and policy factors.
## Economic and Wealth Disparities

* Income and Wealth Gaps: Lower median household incomes and a severe lack of intergenerational wealth make saving for down payments and covering escalating rent or home prices difficult. [1, 2] 
* Housing Cost Burden: A high concentration of Latino households in expensive housing markets—such as California and New York—leads to unsustainable cost burdens, with many spending over 30% or even 50% of their income on housing. [3, 4] 
* Alternative and High-Cost Financing: Reliance on alternative financing, lower refinancing rates, and challenges in documenting non-W-2 or self-employment income increase long-term financial strain. [1, 5, 6] 

## Systemic and Financial Barriers

* Mortgage and Credit Obstacles: Higher rates of unbanked status, limited credit histories, and strict underwriting standards result in significantly higher mortgage denial rates for Latino applicants compared to white peers. [3] 
* Discrimination: Documented real estate and lending discrimination—such as steering clients toward lower-income neighborhoods or requiring more extensive paperwork—limits housing choice and quality. [3, 7] 
* Underrepresentation in Safety-Net Programs: Language rules, citizenship verification requirements, and fears regarding immigration status or public charge policies often prevent eligible Latino households from accessing federal housing subsidies and homeless services. [8, 9] 

## Social and Cultural Factors

* Linguistic and Cultural Barriers: Limited English proficiency and strict Social Security number requirements discourage participation in formal credit systems, rental assistance, and housing counseling. [9, 10] 
* Multigenerational Living: Cultural preferences or economic necessities often drive multi-generational households, which can clash with standard occupancy rules or strain available housing space. [5, 7] 
* Health and Stability Links: Access to preventative healthcare and health insurance serves as a protective factor, reducing overall housing insecurity and economic stress. [11]
