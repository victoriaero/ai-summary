Access to housing for Latino people is influenced by financial barriers, systemic obstacles, and linguistic or immigration-related factors, according to research from the Urban Institute and the National Low Income Housing Coalition.
Financial and Economic Factors
• Wealth and Income Gaps: Latino households often face lower median incomes and have less intergenerational wealth or inheritance to put toward a down payment compared to white households.
• Higher Mortgage Denial Rates: Latino mortgage applicants experience significantly higher denial rates and debt-to-income burdens, often driven by strict credit scoring systems and limited formal credit history.
• Unbanked Status: A higher likelihood of being unbanked or lacking traditional financial institution relationships makes it harder to establish conventional credit or secure home loans.
• Housing Cost Burdens: Many Latino families live in high-cost metro areas where skyrocketing rents and home prices consume most of their income, leaving little room to save.
Systemic and Institutional Barriers
• Language and Literacy: Limited English proficiency and materials lacking Spanish-language accessibility create obstacles when navigating complex rental applications, mortgages, and assistance programs.
• Immigration Status and Fear: Concerns regarding citizenship verification rules, public charge policies, and fear of deportation or government distrust lead many eligible or housing-insecure individuals to avoid public housing and municipal assistance.
• Real Estate Discrimination: Steering by real estate agents, discriminatory lending terms, and differential treatment in the rental market limit housing options and neighborhood mobility.
Social and Cultural Dynamics
• Reliance on Informal Networks: Due to institutional distrust or language barriers, Latino households frequently rely on informal social networks—such as doubled-up living arrangements—rather than formal public systems.
• Multigenerational Living: Cultural preferences or economic necessities for multigenerational households can create strains in standard housing stock that is often not designed or zoned for larger family sizes.
