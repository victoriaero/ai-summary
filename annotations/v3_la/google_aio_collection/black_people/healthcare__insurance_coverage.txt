Socioeconomic disparities, geographic policies, and systemic barriers heavily influence health insurance coverage for Black people in the United States.
Economic and Employment Factors
• Income and Poverty: Black adults face higher rates of poverty and lower median household incomes compared to white adults, which directly limits the ability to afford private or premium-based plans.
• Employer-Sponsored Insurance: Because a large share of non-elderly Americans gets insurance through jobs, lower representation in high-wage industries offering comprehensive health benefits reduces access to stable employer-sponsored coverage.
• Coverage Churn: Studies show that Black Americans experience higher rates of annual coverage loss during early adulthood and middle age, disrupting continuous care.
Policy and Geographic Factors
• Medicaid Expansion: State-level policy decisions play a massive role. Black Americans disproportionately live in Southern states, several of which have been slower to adopt Medicaid expansions under the Affordable Care Act (ACA). Living in a non-expansion state increases the likelihood of remaining uninsured.
• ACA Subsidies: Federal enhanced marketplace subsidies have helped lower uninsured rates to historic lows (around 10.1% for Black individuals as of recent KFF data), but gaps compared to white peers persist.
Systemic and Historical Influences
• Historical and Structural Racism: Past discriminatory practices in the broader insurance industry restricted equitable plan offerings and built a legacy of distrust and underrepresentation among insurance professionals.
• Healthcare Discrimination: Higher reported rates of perceived discrimination or bias within healthcare settings can influence how individuals interact with the health system, enroll in programs, or seek timely care.
