Access to housing for Black people is heavily influenced by historical discrimination, persistent wealth gaps, and ongoing lending and rental biases, as documented by the U.S. Department of the Treasury and the Urban Institute.
Historical Housing Policies
• Redlining: Government and private lenders in the 1930s drew red lines on maps around Black neighborhoods. They refused to give loans or mortgages in these areas.
• Exclusionary Laws: Racially restrictive covenants stopped Black families from buying homes in white neighborhoods.
• Urban Renewal: Cities destroyed many Black neighborhoods for new projects, which blocked families from building generational wealth.
Economic and Wealth Gaps
• Income and Wealth: The Bipartisan Policy Center reports that median white family wealth is nearly eight times higher than median Black family wealth.
• Down Payments: A lack of savings and family inheritance makes it hard to pay for large down payments and closing costs.
• Cost Burden: Lower wages and high rents leave many Black renters severely cost-burdened, meaning they spend more than 30 percent of their income on housing.
Lending and Appraisal Biases
• Unequal Credit: Mainstream banks are often absent in low-income areas. Black homebuyers face higher rates of credit denial and predatory loans.
• Appraisal Bias: The National League of Cities notes that homes in Black neighborhoods are undervalued by an average of $48,000 compared to similar homes in white neighborhoods. This reduces equity growth.
Current Real Estate and Rental Barriers
• Agent Steering: Real estate agents sometimes steer Black buyers away from white neighborhoods and toward under-resourced areas.
• Zoning Rules: Strict local zoning laws keep affordable multi-family housing out of many wealthy suburbs, maintaining neighborhood segregation.
• Rental Disparities: Studies show renters of color often pay higher monthly rent for lower-quality units compared to white renters.
