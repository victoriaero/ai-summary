Access to banking and financial services for Black individuals is significantly shaped by a combination of historical, geographic, economic, and institutional factors.
📌 Structural and Historical Barriers
• Generational wealth gaps: Lower levels of inherited wealth and family assets limit the initial capital available for savings and banking.
• Legacy of redlining: Historic housing and lending discrimination restricted investments in Black neighborhoods.
• Persistent underbanking: Black households experience higher rates of being unbanked or underbanked compared to white households.
🗺️ Geographic and Physical Access
• Bank desertification: A scarcity of physical bank branches in predominantly Black and brown communities reduces face-to-face service access.
• Reduced branch usage: Lower utilization rates of physical branches limit personal relationship-building with capital providers.
• Reliance constraints: Disproportionate reliance on alternative or physical services occurs alongside barriers to entering traditional brick-and-mortar locations.
📊 Credit Scoring and Lending Inequities
• Non-neutral credit metrics: Traditional credit scoring models often penalize borrowers who lack generational assets or stable income histories.
• Higher denial rates: Black entrepreneurs and consumers face higher loan rejection rates and less favorable terms even when controlling for credit scores.
• Predatory market exposure: Communities lacking mainstream banks frequently see targeted marketing from high-cost lenders, such as payday loans.
