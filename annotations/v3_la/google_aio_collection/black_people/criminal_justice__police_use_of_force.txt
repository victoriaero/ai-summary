Disparities in police use of force against Black individuals are driven by a combination of departmental policies, neighborhood context, implicit bias, and systemic factors.
Key Factors Influencing Disparities
• Departmental Policies and Culture: Comprehensive use-of-force policies and strict accountability mechanisms limit aggressive actions, whereas organizational cultures that encourage aggressive street-level enforcement in minority neighborhoods heighten the risk of force.
• Neighborhood and Environmental Context: Police are statistically more likely to use coercive or fatal force in economically disadvantaged neighborhoods, high-crime areas, and communities with high populations of color, where proactive policing and social control strategies are more heavily deployed.
• Stop and Search Practices: Black Americans experience significantly higher rates of police-initiated stops and searches without cause compared to white Americans, which structurally increases the total volume of interactions where force can potentially occur.
• Implicit Bias and Stress Responses: Rapid-fire threat assessments and "fight-or-flight" stress responses by officers can disproportionately lead to misperceptions of threat when interacting with Black civilians, particularly young Black males.
• Training and De-escalation Practices: Departments lacking rigorous scenario-based de-escalation training tend to see higher reliance on physical control tactics during tense encounters.
