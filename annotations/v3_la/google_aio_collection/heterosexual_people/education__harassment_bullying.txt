While statistical data consistently shows that LGBTQ+ students experience disproportionately higher rates of school victimization, heterosexual individuals are also impacted by bullying and harassment in educational settings. The factors influencing harassment toward heterosexual students are often tied to rigid social policing, gender-role expectations, and institutional structures. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] 
The primary factors influencing harassment and bullying for heterosexual people in education include:
## 1. Gender Non-Conformity and Heteronormative Policing
Even if a student is heterosexual, failing to conform to traditional masculine or feminine stereotypes is a major trigger for harassment. [6, 7] 

* Perceived Sexual Orientation: Bullies frequently use homophobic language ("fag", "lesbo") as generalized insults to assert dominance or police social behavior. Heterosexual students who do not fit societal expectations of "how a boy or girl should act" are frequently targeted under the false assumption that they are not heterosexual. [6, 8, 9, 10] 
* Proving Heterosexuality: Research shows that middle and high school students often use homophobic teasing and bullying against heterosexual peers as a tool to establish their own dominant status or "prove" their own heterosexuality within peer groups. [5, 11] 

## 2. Overlap of Bullying and Sexual Harassment
Heterosexual students—particularly girls—experience high rates of gender-based and sexual harassment. [12, 13] 

* The Bullying-to-Harassment Pipeline: Longitudinal studies indicate that students (especially boys) who engage in traditional peer bullying in middle school are significantly more likely to perpetrate sexual harassment later in adolescence. [14] 
* Gender Disparities: Heterosexual girls are disproportionately targeted by sexual commentary, unwanted touching, and spreading of sexual rumors. This form of harassment acts as a mechanism to enforce sexist stereotypes and heavily disrupts academic engagement. [5, 12, 15] 

## 3. School Climate and Lack of Staff Intervention
The environment maintained by school administration directly influences the frequency and severity of the harassment. [6, 16] 

* Dismissive Attitudes: In many school environments, faculty and staff fail to recognize peer sexual harassment or dismiss it as harmless "kidding" or a normal rite of passage. [5, 11] 
* Tolerance of Microaggressions: When schools do not actively sanction derogatory language or lower-level bullying, it signals to potential perpetrators that their behavior is acceptable, paving the way for more severe harassment. [9, 16] 

## 4. Socio-Ecological and Demographical Factors
A student's broader demographic background and school environment affect their vulnerability to bullying: [17] 

* Physical and Social Vulnerabilities: Intersecting traits such as body weight, physical disabilities, or socioeconomic status are highly prevalent drivers of bias-based bullying that affect heterosexual students just as heavily as sexual minorities. [7, 18] 
* School Context: Studies utilizing socio-ecological data show that macro-level risk factors like larger school sizes, higher grade levels, and a lack of parental involvement universally increase the frequency of traditional, sexual, and cyberbullying across all demographics. [17]
