## 1. Marital Status and the "Marriage Penalty"
For heterosexual couples, legal marriage is a highly significant structural factor determining benefit retention: [2, 3] 

* The Benefit Cliff: Many safety-net programs—such as Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) and Medicaid—use joint income thresholds for married couples that are lower than the combined thresholds of two single individuals. Getting married or remaining married can cause a household to hit a "benefits cliff," triggering an immediate loss or reduction of benefits during annual renewals. [3, 5] 
* Social Security Privileges: Conversely, in long-term entitlement programs like Social Security, historical program rules heavily favor heterosexual married dynamics. Heterosexual individuals are highly eligible for spousal and survivor benefits, which significantly enhances benefit retention in late life compared to lifelong single or divorced individuals. [2] 

## 2. Socioeconomic and Income Fluctuations
Because public assistance is primarily means-tested, economic mobility directly dictates renewal success: [3, 5] 

* Non-Welfare Income Increases: Minor increases in hourly wages or shifts from part-time to full-time work frequently disqualify recipients during certification periods. [5] 
* Household Composition Changes: The addition or departure of dependents, or changes in shared living expenses, alters the income-to-household-size ratio required for renewal. [3] 

## 3. Administrative and Logistical Barriers
The process of recertification itself is often the leading reason individuals fail to retain benefits, even if they remain financially eligible: [1, 6] 

* Procedural Churn: Complex paperwork, strict deadlines, and a lack of data-driven auto-renewals cause eligible individuals to drop off rolls. [6] 
* Information Hurdles: A lack of program literacy regarding how or when to submit documentation often stops individuals from successfully navigating annual renewals. [1, 6] 

## 4. Societal Stigma and Program Perception
Psychological and social factors play a documented role in whether individuals choose to actively renew their assistance: [1] 

* Take-Up Gap: Pervasive social stereotypes characterizing benefit recipients as "undeserving" create psychological friction. Heterosexual individuals, particularly those experiencing temporary poverty, may choose to let their benefits lapse during the renewal cycle to avoid the perceived social stigma associated with prolonged welfare use.
