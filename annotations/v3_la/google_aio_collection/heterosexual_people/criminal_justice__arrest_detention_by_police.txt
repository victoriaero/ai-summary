In criminology and criminal justice research, the factors influencing an officer's decision to arrest or detain a person are typically divided into legal and extralegal factors. Because heterosexual cisgender individuals comprise the statistical baseline in broad demographic studies of the justice system, their experiences reflect standard operational determinants of police discretion. [1, 2, 3] 
While sexual minorities frequently face specific vulnerabilities or profiling (such as targeting via selective enforcement), police encounters with heterosexual individuals are shaped primarily by the following core categories: [4] 
## 1. Legal Factors (The Strongest Predictors)
Legally mandated criteria are the primary determinants of whether an encounter results in an arrest or detention. [1] 

* Offense Severity: The seriousness of the alleged crime (e.g., a felony vs. a low-level misdemeanor) is the most powerful predictor of formal arrest. [1, 5] 
* Probable Cause and Evidence: The presence of tangible evidence, direct officer observations, or multiple credible eyewitnesses establishes the mandatory probable cause standard required for a lawful arrest. [6, 7] 
* Prior Criminal Record: A suspect's verified history of prior arrests or active warrants strongly tilts an officer's discretion away from a warning and toward formal booking. [1] 

## 2. Situational Factors
Characteristics of the immediate scene heavily dictate how an encounter unfolds: [8] 

* Suspect Demeanor: Hostility, antagonism, or a refusal to cooperate significantly increases the statistical likelihood of being arrested or subjected to a use of force. Conversely, compliance often yields leniency. [9, 10, 11, 12] 
* Victim Preference: If a victim is present at the scene and explicitly requests an arrest, police are significantly more likely to comply, particularly in domestic violence or property crime incidents. [5, 11] 
* Bystander Presence: The presence of witnesses or a crowd alters officer behavior, often escalating the likelihood of an arrest to maintain control of the public space. [2, 11] 

## 3. Extralegal and Demographic Disparities
Even within the heterosexual population, intersectional demographic traits create vastly different rates of arrest and detention: [13] 

* Socioeconomic Status: Low-income individuals—particularly those with an income under $20,000—face a significantly higher risk of police stops, detention, and exposure to use of force. [14] 
* Race and Ethnicity: Massive disparities persist across racial lines. For instance, Black and Hispanic individuals are arrested, booked, and subjected to police surveillance at disproportionately higher rates than White individuals for identical low-level offenses. [1, 15, 16] 
* Gender: Historically, male suspects face a higher likelihood of arrest and physical detention than female suspects for many types of violent or property offenses. Criminologists often attribute this to a combination of gender stereotyping and operational restrictions (e.g., male officers being unable to perform same-gender searches on women at the scene). However, the gap has narrowed in recent decades, with women making up a growing share of arrests. [12, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19] 

## Summary of Differences: Heterosexual vs. LGBTQ+ Experiences
While heterosexual individuals are subject to the standard legal and situational filters above, they generally do not experience the identity-specific pretexts that affect sexual minorities. Research from the [Urban Institute](https://www.urban.org/urban-wire/no-cops-pride-how-criminal-justice-system-harms-lgbtq-people) and the American Bar Association demonstrates that LGBTQ+ individuals face triple the baseline rate of incarceration. This inflation is often driven by discriminatory profiling (e.g., "walking while trans" or treating possession of condoms as intent to engage in sex work), factors that do not typically skew the arrest metrics of the heterosexual population.
