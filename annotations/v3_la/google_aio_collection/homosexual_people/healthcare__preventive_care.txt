Several key social, systemic, and interpersonal factors influence preventive care utilization among homosexual and sexual minority individuals.
## Provider Discrimination and Bias

* Fear of Mistreatment: Past or anticipated experiences of stigma, bias, and homophobia from medical staff lead many sexual minority individuals to delay or completely avoid routine check-ups. [1, 2] 
* Lack of Cultural Competence: Many healthcare providers lack adequate training regarding the specific health needs and risks of LGBTQ+ patients, resulting in communication gaps and inadequate screening recommendations. [1, 3] 

## Access to Affirming Care

* Trust in Providers: Access to an explicitly [affirming healthcare provider](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10251765/) strongly correlates with higher rates of preventive health screenings (such as cancer screenings and vaccinations) and better management of chronic conditions. [4, 5] 
* Geographic and Systemic Shortages: Rural or medically underserved areas often lack knowledgeable or inclusive providers, making alternative options difficult to find. [6, 7] 

## Economic and Insurance Barriers

* Cost and Coverage Gaps: Sexual minorities face disproportionate rates of poverty, lack of health insurance, or underinsurance, leading to cost-related delays in preventive care. [2, 8, 9] 
* Competing Basic Needs: Financial instability forces many to prioritize immediate necessities—like housing, heat, and food—over routine preventive medical visits. [10] 

## Minority Stress and Mental Health

* Psychological Distress: Chronic exposure to societal discrimination creates high rates of minority stress, anxiety, and depression. [3, 11] 
* Unmet Mental Health Needs: Although demand for mental health services is high among sexual minority populations, systemic barriers often prevent access, indirectly hindering overall preventive health behaviors. [10] 

## Anatomical and Risk-Based Guidelines

* Inappropriate Assumptions: Clinicians sometimes incorrectly assume screening needs based strictly on sexual orientation rather than individual behavior and remaining natal anatomy (e.g., assuming lesbian or bisexual women do not need cervical cancer screenings). [1, 12] 
* Targeted Risk Profiles: Tailored preventive approaches—such as routine HIV and STI testing or Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for men who have sex with men—depend heavily on proactive, non-judgmental communication by clinicians. [3, 13]
