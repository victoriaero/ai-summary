Multiple interconnected socioeconomic, systemic, and historical factors drive the overrepresentation and higher rates of arrest and incarceration among lesbian, gay, and bisexual (LGB) individuals within the criminal justice system. [1, 2] 
## Socioeconomic Instability and Homelessness

* Youth Homelessness: LGB youth experience disproportionately high rates of homelessness, comprising roughly 40% of the national homeless youth population, often due to family rejection or discrimination. [3, 4] 
* Survival Offenses: Instability, poverty, and unemployment force many marginalized LGB individuals—particularly youth and people of color—into survival economies, increasing exposure to law enforcement and minor offense charges. [2] 

## Disproportionate Contact with Juvenile and Adult Justice Systems

* School Discipline Policies: LGB youth face harsher enforcement under zero-tolerance school policies, elevating their representation in the juvenile justice system to about 20% of detained youth. [3, 4] 
* Higher Arrest Rates: Data from sources like the [Prison Policy Initiative](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/blog/2021/03/02/lgbtq/) show that LGB individuals are over twice as likely to be arrested as heterosexual peers, with lesbian and bisexual women facing up to four times the arrest rate. [1, 4] 

## Systemic Discrimination and Over-Policing

* Targeted Enforcement: Historical and ongoing profiling of queer spaces, discriminatory policing practices, and biased enforcement of public order laws disproportionately draw sexual minorities into the criminal legal framework. [3, 5] 
* Criminalization of Health and Identity: Certain state laws penalize behaviors associated with marginalized groups or fail to accommodate diverse identities, compounding vulnerability to arrest and prosecution. [4] 

## Intersecting Marginalization

* Demographic Disparities: Race and gender significantly multiply these risks. Lesbian and bisexual women, as well as LGB people of color, experience compounded societal discrimination that heavily skews their representation in jails and prisons. [1, 2]
