Person-centered care for people with disabilities in healthcare is shaped by provider practices, systemic resources, and the active involvement of patients and their support networks. [1, 2, 3] 
## Provider Knowledge and Communication

* Provider training: Many clinicians lack confidence or adequate training in disability care, which leads to biased assumptions or diagnostic overshadowing. [4] 
* Tailored communication: Effective two-way communication—using accessible formats, visual aids, or direct patient engagement—is vital. [5, 6, 7] 
* Relational empathy: Strong relationships between patients, medical staff, and administrative teams help coordinate unique accommodations. [7] 

## Environment and Accessibility

* Physical infrastructure: Accessible buildings, adjustable examination tables, and specialized medical equipment remove physical barriers to basic care. [2, 6] 
* Appointment pacing: Extended appointment durations allow providers to complete thorough, trauma-informed physical and cognitive evaluations without rushing. [2, 6] 

## Autonomy and Support Networks

* Patient agency: Prioritizing self-determination and asking individuals what matters to their daily well-being shifts power to the patient. [8] 
* Involvement of caregivers: Trusted family members, legal guardians, or support brokers provide essential context—especially for non-verbal patients—while balancing individual autonomy. [3, 5] 

## Systemic and Policy Frameworks

* Disability models: Shifting from a strict medical model (focusing solely on deficits) to a social or biopsychosocial model promotes equity and human rights. [4, 9] 
* Resource constraints: Financial limitations, administrative burdens, and misaligned payment or workflow models can restrict the time and tools available for comprehensive planning. [1, 10, 11]
