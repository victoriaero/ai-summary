Administrative burden in government benefits is generally a function of learning costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs, which are influenced by system design, communication, and applicant resources. [1, 2] 
## Key Factors Influencing Administrative Burden

* Learning Costs: The time and effort required to find out that a program exists, understand eligibility rules, and figure out how to apply. Complex rules, lack of clear public information, and poor outreach increase these burdens. [1, 3, 4] 
* Compliance Costs: The "time tax" and effort spent fulfilling bureaucratic requirements, such as filling out multiple forms, gathering extensive verification documents, traveling to offices, and waiting on hold. Frequent recertifications and complicated digital or physical application portals add to this strain. [1, 2, 3] 
* Psychological Costs: The emotional toll of navigating the system, including the social stigma attached to receiving public assistance, stress over uncertainty, and a feeling of lost autonomy due to intrusive oversight. [1, 3, 5] 
* Human Capital and Resources: An individual's access to resources—such as reliable internet, transportation, free time, and cognitive bandwidth—directly affects their ability to handle bureaucratic demands. People experiencing financial scarcity or working multiple hourly jobs face higher relative costs to comply. [1, 6] 
* Policy and Program Design Choices: Intentional or operational hurdles—such as strict work requirements, asset limits, and rigorous verification steps implemented to curb fraud or error—heighten the overall burden on applicants. [1, 7]
