Completion and graduation rates in education are shaped by a combination of academic, financial, personal, and institutional factors.
## Academic Preparation and Performance

* High school rigor: Taking advanced or challenging courses—especially higher-level math beyond Algebra II—strongly predicts college success and graduation.
* Early milestones: Ninth-grade performance (such as maintaining a GPA above 2.0 and passing core classes) is one of the best indicators of high school completion.
* Study and time management: Strong academic habits, regular attendance, and consistent credit accumulation keep students on track. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] 

## Financial Factors

* Economic stability: Family income, socioeconomic status, and poverty status directly influence persistence.
* Proactive planning: Students who utilize financial aid, scholarships, and budget planning early face fewer disruptions, whereas unexpected financial stress frequently leads to dropping out. [1, 4, 6, 7] 

## Personal and Demographic Background

* First-generation status: Students who are the first in their families to attend college face a significantly higher risk of leaving school, often juggling mental health challenges and family expectations. [6] 
* Life circumstances: Working full-time, attending part-time, single-parent status, or having dependents reduce completion probabilities unless flexible support is provided. [7, 8] 

## Institutional Support and Climate

* Campus engagement: Access to counseling, peer mentoring, academic advising, and welcoming school climates foster a sense of belonging.
* Institutional resources: Schools with robust student support services, active learning strategies, and targeted early-warning intervention programs achieve higher graduation rates. [3, 4, 8, 9, 10]
