Demographic factors, neighborhood conditions, and policing priorities strongly influence officer-initiated police contact within the criminal justice system. [1, 2, 3] 
## Demographic and Social Factors

* Age and Gender: Young adults (particularly ages 18–24) and males experience higher rates of police-initiated contact, such as traffic or street stops, compared to older adults and females. [4, 5] 
* Race and Ethnicity: Studies and national data compiled by organizations like the [Bureau of Justice Statistics](https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/contacts-between-police-and-public-2022) show persistent racial disparities. Black and Hispanic residents experience higher rates of involuntary stops and higher instances of threat or use of nonfatal force relative to white residents. [4, 5] 
* Social Networks and Family: Research indicates that individuals with familial ties to the criminal justice system—such as an arrested parent or an involved sibling—face a higher statistical likelihood of police contact. [3] 

## Neighborhood and Environmental Context

* Disadvantaged Areas: Officers are more likely to conduct proactive, officer-initiated stops in lower-income or economically disadvantaged neighborhoods with higher social instability. [1] 
* Crime Visibility and Policing Strategies: Shifts in departmental priorities, "proactive policing" models, and high-visibility patrols heavily dictate where and how frequently street stops occur. [2, 6] 

## Institutional and Systemic Factors

* School Discipline: Prior disciplinary actions or surveillance within educational settings often serve as a pipeline or contributing factor to early adolescent police contact. [3] 
* System Avoidance: Punitive enforcement loops mean that individuals who have prior records face elevated visibility or recurring scrutiny from law enforcement systems. [6, 7]
