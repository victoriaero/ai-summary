Administrative burden in government benefits is driven by three core types of costs—learning, compliance, and psychological costs—which are shaped by policy design, agency operations, and individual resources. [1, 2] 
## Core Types of Costs

* Learning Costs: The effort required to discover that a program exists, figure out eligibility rules, understand application instructions, and find approved providers. [3] 
* Compliance Costs: The "time taxes" and material expenses spent filling out forms, gathering verification documents, waiting on hold, traveling to offices, and completing renewals. [3, 4] 
* Psychological Costs: The emotional stress, frustration, loss of autonomy, and social stigma experienced during interactions with bureaucratic systems. [3, 4] 

## Key Influencing Factors

* Policy Design and Intent: Rules built into programs—such as strict work requirements, asset limits, or frequent recertification mandates—often intentionally or unintentionally create friction and lower program participation. [5, 6] 
* Administrative Fragmentation and Federalism: Complex coordination across multiple government levels (federal, state, and local) or private third-party contractors creates inconsistent rules, confusing portals, and scattered application steps. [1, 7] 
* Agency Capacity and Communication: Poor government communication, lack of plain-language materials, underfunded agency staffing, and unsupportive organizational structures amplify burdens for applicants. [8] 
* Individual Human and Cognitive Capital: An applicant’s access to resources—such as digital literacy, stable internet, time off work, health status, and cognitive bandwidth—directly affects their capacity to navigate bureaucratic hurdles. [9, 10]
