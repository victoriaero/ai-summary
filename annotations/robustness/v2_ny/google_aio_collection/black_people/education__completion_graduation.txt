Several interconnected financial, systemic, and social factors influence high school and college completion rates for Black students in the United States. [1, 2, 3] 
## Financial Pressures and Work-Life Demands

* Work and Caregiving: A large share of Black postsecondary students balance full-time jobs or family caregiving responsibilities with their studies, which increases the likelihood of stopping out. [4, 5] 
* Debt and Resource Gaps: Financial aid alone is often insufficient. Prohibitive student loan debt and gaps in institutional endowments or financial aid packages disproportionately affect persistence. [1, 2, 6] 
* Institutional Type: Black students are statistically more likely to enroll in two-year community colleges or for-profit institutions—which often feature lower completion rates—partly due to financial constraints and geographic proximity. [3, 7, 8] 

## K-12 Disparities and Academic Preparation

* Opportunity Gaps: Historical and ongoing inequities create K-12 opportunity gaps, including unequal funding, school segregation, and less access to Advanced Placement (AP) courses. [1, 2] 
* Disciplinary Disparities: Black students—particularly Black boys—face disproportionately higher rates of harsh school discipline and suspensions, which disrupts academic momentum and lowers on-time graduation rates. [3, 9, 10] 
* Early Academic Tracking: Systemic tracking and lower baseline academic preparation can affect confidence and readiness upon entering postsecondary education. [1, 6] 

## Campus Climate and Support Systems

* Discrimination and Bias: A notable percentage of Black college students report experiencing frequent or occasional discrimination on campus, negatively impacting their mental health and persistence. [4, 5] 
* Faculty Diversity: Low representation of minority faculty can lead to reduced academic expectations or fewer mentorship opportunities. Conversely, robust faculty mentoring, academic advising, and culturally affirming environments—such as those found at many [Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs)](https://digitalcommons.nl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1635&context=diss)—strongly support degree completion. [1, 11] 
* Institutional Support: Access to emergency funds, inclusive campus cultures, and flexible scheduling heavily influence whether students can remain enrolled through personal or financial crises. [4, 5]
