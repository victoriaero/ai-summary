Person-centered care for Black people in healthcare is significantly shaped by clinician communication, interpersonal and systemic biases, cultural competency, and socioeconomic factors. [1, 2, 3] 
## Clinician-Level Factors

* Active Listening and Communication: According to [Nature Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97536-9), clinician dismissiveness, poor communication, and failure to actively listen heavily degrade the quality of person-centered care. [1, 4] 
* Unconscious Bias and Stereotyping: Racial biases, stereotyping (such as incorrectly assuming drug-seeking behavior), and lack of cultural sensitivity often lead to cold treatment and misdiagnoses. [1, 5] 
* Race Concordance: Research highlights that provider-patient race concordance—such as seeking out Black physicians—often facilitates deeper trust, better relationship-building, and more positive care experiences. [4] 

## Systemic and Structural Factors

* Access and Social Determinants of Health (SDOH): Factors like income inequality, unstable housing, and geographic barriers to medical facilities directly dictate health outcomes and limit continuous, high-quality care. [6, 7] 
* Insurance and Financial Barriers: Non-Hispanic Black individuals face higher rates of being uninsured or underinsured, alongside high out-of-pocket costs for emerging drugs and therapies. [6, 8] 
* Algorithmic Bias: Clinical algorithms and artificial intelligence tools have historically incorporated biased data or race-based adjustments (such as historical kidney function tests) that can worsen treatment disparities. [8] 

## Patient and Community Factors

* Historical and Present-Day Mistrust: Instances of systemic racism and historical medical mistreatment foster a justified institutional mistrust, which can impact patient engagement and adherence. [3, 9] 
* Health Literacy: Complex or high-level medical jargon creates a barrier to understanding health data, treatment plans, and insurance coverage. [6] 
* Cultural and Social Norms: Upbringing, peer influence, and community structures (including masculine gender norms that encourage stoicism) shape how Black individuals—particularly Black men—navigate health-seeking behaviors. [3, 10]
