Several systemic, environmental, and institutional factors influence police-initiated contact for Black people in the criminal justice system. [1, 2, 3] 
## Geographic and Neighborhood Factors

* Concentrated Disadvantage: Research cited by the National Institutes of Health and criminology studies show that police deployment is often heavily concentrated in economically disadvantaged or minority neighborhoods, increasing the frequency of police-resident interactions. [1, 3] 
* High-Volume Policing: According to reports by [The Sentencing Project](https://www.sentencingproject.org/press-releases/new-report-on-racial-disparities-in-policing-and-crime-from-the-sentencing-project/), proactive or "high-volume" policing strategies (such as broken-windows policing or stop-and-frisk) target specific areas, disproportionately raising stop rates for Black and Latinx individuals. [4, 5] 
* Municipal Revenue Practices: Law enforcement agencies in certain jurisdictions have been found to rely on traffic stops and ticketing to raise municipal revenue rather than strictly addressing public safety needs. [4] 

## Social and Environmental Background

* Family and Peer Networks: Criminological studies indicate that early police contact is strongly correlated with variables such as having a parent with an arrest history, siblings involved in delinquency, or exposure to criminal behavior in the surrounding adult network. [1, 6] 
* School Discipline: Disparities in school disciplinary actions (such as suspensions or expulsions) often feed into juvenile justice pipelines, increasing the likelihood of early police engagement for Black youth. [1, 6] 

## Institutional and Implicit Bias

* Implicit and Explicit Associations: Research highlights that law enforcement officers may operate under implicit or explicit biases that associate Blackness with criminality or physical danger, affecting discretionary decisions to initiate stops. [2] 
* Presumption of Criminality: Sociological research notes that Black individuals often navigate a "provisional status" in public or predominantly white spaces where they face a heightened presumption of suspicion from authorities and civilians. [2]
