Historical policies, persistent economic disparities, and ongoing market discrimination heavily shape neighborhood quality and housing access for Black Americans. [1, 2] 
## Historical and Systemic Policies

* Redlining: Federal agencies and private lenders historically drew red lines on maps around Black neighborhoods, marking them as high risk and denying mortgages. [3, 4, 5] 
* Exclusionary Zoning: Local governments used zoning laws to separate industrial spaces from wealthy areas while pushing lower-cost multi-family housing or environmental hazards into Black neighborhoods. [5, 6] 
* Urban Renewal: Past government projects tore down Black-owned homes and thriving business districts to build civic centers or highways, destroying community equity. [6] 

## Ongoing Housing Market Discrimination

* Racial Steering: Real estate agents frequently guide Black homebuyers and renters away from predominantly white neighborhoods and toward segregated or under-resourced areas. [1, 3] 
* Appraisal Bias: Property appraisers routinely undervalue homes in majority-Black neighborhoods compared to identical homes in white areas, limiting homeowner wealth growth. [3, 7, 8] 
* Lending Disparities: Black applicants face higher mortgage denial rates and greater exposure to predatory lending terms. [6] 

## Resource Allocation and Neighborhood Conditions

* Public Infrastructure: Majority-Black neighborhoods often suffer from underfunded schools, fewer full-service grocery stores, and poorly maintained public parks. [1, 9] 
* Environmental Hazards: Industrial pollution and toxic waste sites are disproportionately placed near minority communities. [5, 8] 
* Socioeconomic Mismatch: Middle-income Black families are statistically more likely to live in high-poverty, poorly resourced neighborhoods than low-income white families. [1] 

## Wealth and Homeownership Gaps

* Generational Inheritance: The historic denial of GI Bill benefits and housing loans prevented Black families from passing down generational wealth.
* Home Appreciation: Homes in Black neighborhoods appreciate at lower rates, compounding the broader racial wealth divide. [2, 3, 5, 8]
