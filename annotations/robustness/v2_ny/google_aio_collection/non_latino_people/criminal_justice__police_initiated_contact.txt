Neighborhood crime levels, individual age, and socioeconomic status heavily influence police-initiated contact for non-Latino populations, such as Black and White individuals. [1] 
## Key Factors Influencing Police-Initiated Contact

* Neighborhood Crime Rates and Context: According to research from the [National Institute of Justice](https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/race-trust-and-police-legitimacy), differences in police opinions and encounter frequencies across racial groups often reduce or disappear when controlling for local neighborhood crime levels, community disadvantage, and concentrated poverty. [1] 
* Age and Demographics: Younger individuals—particularly young adults aged 18 to 24—experience significantly higher rates of police-initiated contact (such as traffic or street stops) across racial and ethnic lines compared to older demographics. [2] 
* Socioeconomic Status: Individual income, education levels, and employment status affect residential location and routine activities, which indirectly shape the likelihood and frequency of encountering law enforcement. [1] 
* Social and Environmental Environment: Criminological studies indicate that structural and social factors—including school disciplinary actions, peer networks, and family history of criminal justice involvement—strongly predict the likelihood of proactive or police-initiated contact for adolescents and young adults. [3] 
* Racial Disparities Beyond Behavior: Even when accounting for demographic and behavioral factors, broad empirical analyses (such as data compiled by the [National Conference of State Legislatures](https://www.ncsl.org/civil-and-criminal-justice/racial-and-ethnic-disparities-in-the-criminal-justice-system)) show persistent racial disparities in stop-and-search decisions affecting Black non-Latino individuals compared to White individuals, such as disparities observed in daylight versus nighttime traffic stop rates. [4]
