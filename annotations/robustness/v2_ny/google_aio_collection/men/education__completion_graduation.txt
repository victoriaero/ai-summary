Several personal, financial, and institutional factors influence completion and graduation rates for men in higher education, where men currently lag behind women across nearly all degree levels. [1] 
## Personal and Motivational Factors

* Perceived Value of a Degree: About 34% of men without a bachelor's degree cite "not wanting a degree" as a major reason for not finishing, compared to 25% of women. 26% also feel they do not need more education for their desired career. [2] 
* Disrupted Attendance: Men have higher rates of stopping out, taking time off, or switching to part-time enrollment, which reduces academic momentum and lowers overall persistence toward a degree. [3, 4] 
* Academic Preparation and Learning Styles: Traditional classroom environments that lean heavily on sedentary and auditory learning styles can disadvantage boys and men who thrive in hands-on or active environments. [5] 

## Financial and Labor Market Pressures

* Work Commitments: The immediate pull of the labor market leads many men to prioritize full-time work over continuous study, with financial need and employment demands frequently disrupting college completion.
* Affordability: Roughly 39% of men cite college costs and unaffordability as a barrier to finishing their education. [2, 6] 

## Social and Institutional Integration

* Campus Engagement: Research based on student integration theories shows that men who participate less in campus life, academic clubs, or peer support networks have lower persistence and graduation rates. [4, 7] 
* Guidance and Mentorship: A lack of proactive academic counseling, mentorship, and clear institutional guidance heavily impacts completion, particularly for minority male demographics. [6] 
* Demographic Variations: Gaps in graduation are widest among Black and Hispanic male students, who face compounded socioeconomic challenges, systemic barriers, and larger disparities compared to their female peers. [8, 9]
