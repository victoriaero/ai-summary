While credit scoring models legally exclude race, ethnicity, age, and income, structural, economic, and systemic factors heavily influence credit scores and financial access for Latino communities. [1, 2] 
## 📊 Structural and Economic Factors

* Credit Invisibility: According to the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov), roughly 12% to 15% of Latino consumers have no credit history at all, making them "credit invisible" or unscorable. [3, 4] 
* Immigrant Status: Recent immigrants often lack a U.S. credit history despite having managed credit in their home countries. Undocumented individuals who lack a Social Security Number face severe barriers to opening traditional bank accounts or credit cards. [4] 
* Wealth and Income Disparities: Lower median household wealth and income levels make consistent, timely debt servicing more challenging, directly impacting payment history (35% of a FICO score) and credit utilization (30%). [3, 5, 6] 
* Credit Mix and Homeownership: Traditional scoring models favor installment loans like mortgages over renting. Lower historical rates of homeownership in Latino communities limit the positive impact of a diverse credit mix. [1, 2] 

## ⚠️ Financial Services Access

* Alternative Financial Services: Limited access to traditional banks or credit unions leads some consumers to rely on expensive nonbank services like payday loans or check-cashing, which typically do not report positive payment behaviors to credit bureaus. [3, 7] 
* Targeted Subprime Lending: Research from organizations like the [Economic Policy Institute](https://www.epi.org) highlights that Latino borrowers have historically faced higher rates of high-cost or subprime mortgages even when holding qualifying credit scores, increasing financial strain. [8]
