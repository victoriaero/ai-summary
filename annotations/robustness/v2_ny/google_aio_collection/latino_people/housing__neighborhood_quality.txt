Several socioeconomic, systemic, and cultural factors significantly influence neighborhood quality and housing outcomes for Latino communities. [1, 2] 
## Economic and Wealth Disparities

* Income and Wealth Gaps: Lower median incomes and stark wealth gaps make it harder for Latino households to afford homes in high-opportunity areas. [2, 3] 
* Housing Cost Burdens: A high concentration of Latino families in expensive metropolitan housing markets leads to severe rent or mortgage cost burdens, leaving fewer resources for neighborhood upkeep or savings. [4, 5] 
* Banking Access: Higher rates of being unbanked or underbanked limit access to traditional credit-building tools and mainstream mortgages. [2, 4] 

## Systemic and Market Discrimination

* Steering and Redlining: Discriminatory practices by real estate entities can steer Latino buyers and renters—especially immigrants—toward lower-income, disinvested, or segregated neighborhoods. [4, 6] 
* Housing Speculation: Property speculation and aggressive investor activity frequently concentrate in lower-income Black and Latino neighborhoods, resulting in higher eviction rates and more severe maintenance violations. [7] 
* Lending Obstacles: Higher mortgage denial rates and reliance on alternative, high-cost financing restrict purchasing power and neighborhood mobility. [1, 4] 

## Immigration and Demographic Factors

* Immigration Status and Language: Limited English proficiency and undocumented status create steep barriers when navigating rental agreements, public services, and mortgage underwriting. [1, 8] 
* Household Size: A higher prevalence of multigenerational households often requires larger housing units, increasing vulnerability to overcrowding in tight or expensive housing markets. [1, 9] 

## Infrastructure and Environment

* Public Services and Amenities: Latino communities are disproportionately located in areas with reduced access to safe, well-funded public transportation, reliable infrastructure, and high-quality green or recreational spaces. [10, 11] 
* Environmental Pressures: Disinvested neighborhoods often feature higher exposure to environmental hazards, structural dwelling deficiencies, and flood risks. [1, 9]
