Neighborhood quality for cisgender people in housing is shaped by a multi-dimensional intersection of physical environment, socio-economic factors, infrastructural access, and safety metrics. While cisgender people—individuals whose gender identity aligns with their sex assigned at birth—do not face the specific, systemic gender-identity discrimination or administrative barriers that gender-expansive populations encounter in housing, their perception of neighborhood quality is determined by universal indicators of urban livability and housing stability. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] 
Comprehensive urban planning and environmental psychology frameworks break down these factors into four primary domains: [3, 7] 
## 1. Physical Environment and Aesthetic Upkeep
The visual and sensory condition of a neighborhood heavily weights residential satisfaction and localized home values: [8, 9] 

* Housing and Building Conditions: The age, structural integrity, and general maintenance of surrounding homes establish the neighborhood’s baseline trajectory. Structural blight or property abandonment drastically reduces localized satisfaction. [9, 10, 11] 
* Environmental Health: Low levels of noise pollution, clean air, effective sanitation, and reliable trash collection protect physical and mental health. [2, 12] 
* Green Spaces: Proximity to well-maintained parks, public trees, and recreational open areas consistently acts as a top-tier indicator of high neighborhood quality. [11, 13] 

## 2. Infrastructure, Mobility, and Functional Access
A neighborhood's layout determines how efficiently residents can navigate their daily lives: [3] 

* School District Quality: For cisgender families with children, public school performance scores serve as one of the strongest drivers of long-term home value and neighborhood retention. [9] 
* Transit and Walkability: High street connectivity, pedestrian-safe sidewalk conditions, and reliable access to public transportation heavily influence localized desirability. [11, 13] 
* Commercial Proximity: Convenient access to core economic hubs—such as grocery stores, medical facilities, employment centers, and retail shops—directly impacts urban livability scores. [2, 9]

## 3. Safety and Social Cohesion
Perceptions of safety and community belonging dictate whether a resident stays in a neighborhood long-term: [14, 15] 

* Crime Rates: Low objective crime numbers—and the subjective perception of safety—are critical foundations for neighborhood satisfaction. High crime or perceived vulnerability suppresses property turn-over and neighborhood pride. [9, 14, 15] 
* Social Capital: The existence of active neighborhood associations, community gardens, or local initiatives fosters shared accountability and social cohesion. [9, 13] 
* Demographic Alignment: Residential satisfaction is often higher when the neighborhood's lifestyle match fits the resident's specific stage of life (e.g., quiet, low-traffic areas preferred by retirees versus lively, mixed-use zones preferred by working-age individuals). [9] 

## 4. Economic Profiles and Housing Tenure
Economic stability dictates how vulnerable a neighborhood is to rapid displacement or systemic disinvestment: [6, 16] 

* Affordability Dynamics: High concentrations of severely rent-burdened households (spending >30% of income on housing) can lead to unexpected residential mobility, which fractures neighborhood stability.
* Tenure Status (Owning vs. Renting): Data from the [USAFacts platform](https://usafacts.org/articles/what-do-americans-think-of-their-neighborhoods/) highlights that homeowners structurally express higher rates of satisfaction with their neighborhoods (83%) compared to renters (64%) across nearly every measured category. [17, 18, 19]
