In criminal justice, police-initiated contact for cisgender individuals is primarily influenced by demographic intersections (race and biological sex), situational characteristics, and socioeconomic status. While cisgender people face lower average rates of proactive surveillance, searches, and discretionary arrests compared to transgender individuals, their interactions with law enforcement are heavily shaped by distinct factors: [1, 2, 3, 4] 
## 1. Demographic Intersections (Sex and Race)
While data indicates that cisgender males experience higher baseline rates of police-initiated contact (such as traffic and street stops) than cisgender females, race significantly alters these probabilities: [1] 

* The "Gender Protection" Disparity: Data compiled by the [Prison Policy Initiative](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/blog/2019/05/14/policingwomen/) shows that while white cisgender women experience relatively low rates of proactive stops, Black cisgender women are 17% more likely to face traffic stops than white women.
* Discretionary Escalation: Once stopped, Black cisgender women face arrests at triple the rate of white cisgender women (4.4% vs 1.5%). [5] 

## 2. Situational Characteristics
A comprehensive study published in [Policing: A Journal of Policy and Practice](https://academic.oup.com/policing/article-abstract/doi/10.1093/police/paac064/6696869) analyzed body-worn camera footage and found that a citizen's cisgender status does not directly alter officer behavior. Instead, police-initiated contact and its escalation are dictated by specific situational factors: [2] 

* Level of Resistance: Hostility or non-compliance from the individual strongly dictates proactive police coercion.
* Guardianship: The presence of bystanders, legal advocates, or co-witnesses heavily influences whether an officer initiates or de-escalates an encounter.
* Type of Contact: Proactive traffic enforcement versus responding to calls for service alters officer bias and handling. [2, 6] 

## 3. Socioeconomic and Environmental Factors
For cisgender individuals, the neighborhood context and legal background act as primary drivers for proactive policing: [7, 8] 

* Over-Policed Communities: Living in or traveling through low-income neighborhoods experiencing heavy violent crime control heavily increases the likelihood of proactive stops, regardless of an individual's specific behavior. [8, 9] 
* Social Environment: Research tracking early adolescent police contact indicates that factors like having an arrested parent, school disciplinary history, or a sibling involved in criminal activity are strong predictors for why law enforcement initiates early contact with specific youth. [7] 

## Summary of Comparative Risk
To contextualize police-initiated contact, data from California's Racial and Identity Profiling Act highlights the comparative baseline for individuals perceived as cisgender versus transgender during law enforcement stops: [4] 

| Perceived Identity Group | Percent Searched During a Stop | Risk Level Factors |
|---|---|---|
| Cisgender Men | 14% | Influenced heavily by age (18–24 peak), neighborhood, and behavioral compliance. |
| Cisgender Women | 8% | Lowest baseline risk, but heavily exacerbated by racial disparities (Black and Latina women). |
| Transgender Individuals | 24% | Highly elevated due to systemic gender enforcement, profiling, and transphobic stereotypes. |
