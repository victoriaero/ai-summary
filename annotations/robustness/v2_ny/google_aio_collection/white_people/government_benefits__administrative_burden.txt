White adults accessing public benefits like Medicaid and SNAP experience administrative burdens primarily through learning and compliance costs, which are influenced by system complexity, eligibility rules, and individual resource constraints. [1, 2] 
## Core Factors Influencing Administrative Burden
Administrative burden is divided into three main types of costs: learning, compliance, and psychological. Research on public benefit programs (such as SNAP and Medicaid) highlights how these factors affect White applicants: [3] 

* Learning Costs: These involve the difficulty of finding out about a program, understanding eligibility rules, and figuring out how to apply. White applicants frequently report navigating these initial informational hurdles, which scale with how fragmented or complicated the application rules are. [2, 3] 
* Compliance Costs: These include filling out extensive paperwork, gathering verification documents, waiting in lines or on calls, and completing renewals. Document requirements and work or income reporting rules drive up these practical hurdles. [4, 5] 
* Socioeconomic and Resource Constraints: Factors like limited internet access, flexible work hours, or geographic isolation increase the time and effort needed to comply with bureaucratic demands, regardless of race. [5] 
* Comparative Program Dynamics: Studies (such as those featured by the [Center for Social Development](https://openscholarship.wustl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1978&context=csd_research)) indicate that White adults often report higher baseline learning and compliance burdens in certain public benefit evaluations compared to some minority groups, partly due to differing patterns of geographic distribution, program tier utilization, or fewer reports of direct staff discrimination. [1, 2] 
* Psychological Costs and Stigma: Stress and the social stigma of receiving public aid affect participants, though White applicants report lower rates of perceived racial discrimination from agency staff during these interactions compared to Black or Asian applicants. [1, 2]
