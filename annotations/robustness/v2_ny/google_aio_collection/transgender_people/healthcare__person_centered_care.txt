Person-centered care for transgender people is heavily influenced by interpersonal provider interactions, systemic healthcare training gaps, and financial or geographic accessibility. [1, 2] 
## Healthcare Provider Interactions and Cultural Competence

* Knowledge and Education: Providers who are knowledgeable, experienced, or willing to learn significantly improve patient trust, whereas lacking awareness often forces patients to educate their own doctors. [3, 4] 
* Communication and Respect: Using correct pronouns, chosen names, and inclusive terminology during visits and examinations fosters a welcoming environment. [5, 6] 
* Trauma-Informed Approach: Delivering sensitive, trauma-informed physical exams and avoiding assumptions that mental health conditions always stem from a patient's gender identity are critical. [7, 8] 

## Structural and Systemic Barriers

* Education Gaps: Insufficient formal training in medical and nursing curricula regarding transgender health leads to widespread provider deficits. [2, 7] 
* Environment and Infrastructure: Clinics lacking inclusive intake forms, gender-neutral restrooms, or adapted electronic medical records (EMRs) for sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) data create friction. [7, 9, 10] 
* Availability of Specialists: A severe shortage of local, specialized subspecialty providers often forces patients to travel long distances for transition-related or routine care. [2, 8] 

## Financial and Social Factors

* Affordability and Insurance: Prohibitive costs and frequent insurance coverage denials for essential treatments like hormone therapy or surgeries create heavy financial burdens. [2, 4] 
* Fear of Discrimination: Past stigma, systemic transphobia, or anticipation of mistreatment frequently lead individuals to delay or completely avoid necessary medical care. [2, 11]
