In criminology and criminal justice research, heterosexual individuals generally serve as the baseline comparison group when analyzing how sexual orientation affects law enforcement interactions. Studies show that heterosexual people experience significantly lower rates of involuntary, police-initiated contact than lesbian, gay, bisexual, or transgender (LGBTQ+) individuals. [1, 2] 
When sexual orientation is not a compounding risk factor, police-initiated contact for heterosexual people is primarily driven by traditional sociological, demographic, and behavioral factors: [3, 4] 
## 1. Gender and Sex

* Higher Contact for Males: Heterosexual males face much higher rates of police-initiated encounters (such as traffic stops and street interrogations) than heterosexual females. [5, 6] 
* Gender Stereotyping: Law enforcement patterns frequently operate on gendered assumptions. For example, young men are more frequently stereotyped as potential threats or gang affiliates, increasing their likelihood of being stopped. Conversely, female suspects have historically faced lower frequencies of random searches and arrests due to systemic factors like same-gender search policies. [6] 

## 2. Race and Ethnicity

* Intersectional Disparities: Within the heterosexual population, race heavily dictates the frequency and nature of police contact. Black heterosexual individuals are disproportionately targeted for police-initiated stops compared to their White peers. [3, 7] 
* Outcome Differences: Data from the [Prison Policy Initiative](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/blog/2019/05/14/policingwomen/) notes that Black women are 17% more likely to experience traffic stops and three times more likely to be arrested during those stops than White women. [7] 

## 3. Socioeconomic Disadvantage and Neighborhood

* Over-Policing of Low-Income Areas: Heterosexual individuals living in economically disadvantaged neighborhoods experience heightened police surveillance regardless of their personal behavior. [4] 
* Systemic Exposure: Socioeconomic hardship often correlates with a higher concentration of law enforcement presence, which mechanically increases the likelihood of involuntary stops, questioning, or searches. [4, 8] 

## 4. Behavioral and Social Environment Factors

* Personal Delinquency: Engaging in visible property crimes, drug offenses, or disruptive public behavior remains a primary direct trigger for police-initiated contact. [3, 7] 
* Family and Peer Networks: Research published in [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3868476/) indicates that social environment strongly dictates early life police contact. Individuals are significantly more likely to face police intervention if they have a parent who has been arrested, a sibling involved in criminal activity, or close associations with peers engaging in substance abuse. [3] 
* Institutional Discipline: For youth, a history of school disciplinary actions (such as suspensions) heavily correlates with subsequent police intervention. [3]
