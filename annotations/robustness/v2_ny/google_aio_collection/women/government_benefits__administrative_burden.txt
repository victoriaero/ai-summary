Women experience disproportionate [administrative burden](https://www.acus.gov/document/identifying-and-reducing-burdens-public-administrative-processes) when navigating government benefits due to intersecting gender roles, socioeconomic factors, and policy designs. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] 
## Types of Administrative Costs

* Learning Costs: The time and effort required to find out if a program exists, understand eligibility criteria, and decipher complex government communication. [5, 7] 
* Compliance Costs (Time Taxes): The physical and logistical hurdles of applying or maintaining benefits, such as filling out redundant paperwork, waiting on hold, or traveling for in-person interviews. [8] 
* Psychological Costs: The emotional stress, loss of autonomy, frustration, and social stigma associated with seeking public assistance. [5, 8] 

## Key Influencing Factors

* Gendered Division of Labor: Women routinely shoulder a higher share of unpaid caregiving, household management, and family health responsibilities, leaving less time and cognitive bandwidth to absorb state-imposed compliance tasks. [2, 9] 
* Intersectionality and Social Location: Burden is heavily shaped by overlapping factors like race, class, age, and ability. Women of color and low-income women face harsher surveillance, restrictive program rules, and fewer non-state financial buffers. [6] 
* Socioeconomic Status and Human Capital: Women are disproportionately concentrated in low-wage or hourly jobs, meaning that taking time off work to fulfill compliance requirements (like wait times or in-person visits) carries an acute financial penalty. [8, 10] 
* Program Design and Bureaucratic Friction: Fragmented application processes, frequent recertification demands, strict asset or work requirements, and a lack of digital optimization or data sharing exacerbate the "time tax" on applicants. [4, 8, 11]
