In the criminal justice system, police-initiated contact for individuals without disabilities is driven primarily by a combination of demographic disparities, situational variables, and environmental factors. Unlike interactions involving individuals with disabilities—which are frequently catalyzed by crisis response, medical emergencies, or perceived behavioral non-compliance—contacts within the general, non-disabled population are heavily dictated by proactive enforcement strategies, systemic biases, and lifestyle indicators. [1, 2, 3, 4] 
Data published by the [Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS)](https://bjs.ojp.gov/) and criminological studies outline the primary factors influencing these encounters:
## 1. Demographic Factors

* Race and Ethnicity: Systemic disparities heavily influence who police choose to stop. According to BJS data analyzed by the [Prison Policy Initiative](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/), Black and Hispanic individuals are significantly more likely to experience police-initiated stops (such as traffic and street stops) and face subsequent enforcement actions or use of force compared to white individuals. [1, 5] 
* Gender: Males are disproportionately targeted for police-initiated contact. BJS data consistently shows that men face higher rates of proactive street and traffic stops, whereas women are statistically more likely to initiate contact with the police themselves (e.g., to report a crime). [6, 7] 
* Age: Adolescents and young adults (particularly those aged 18 to 24) experience the highest cumulative rates of proactive police stops. [4, 8] 

## 2. Socioeconomic and Environmental Variables

* Neighborhood Context: The "social-ecological" makeup of a neighborhood plays a massive role in officer discretion. Proactive, police-initiated stops are heavily concentrated in economically disadvantaged or "socially disorganized" areas. Conversely, police contact in higher-income, stable neighborhoods is overwhelmingly "dispatch-initiated" (responding to a specific citizen call) rather than initiated by the officer's own discretion. [9, 10, 11, 12] 
* Community Visibility and Housing Status: Independent of race or age, individuals with high physical visibility in public spaces—such as those experiencing precarious housing or homelessness—face a significantly higher probability of being stopped, questioned, or moved along by officers. [4] 

## 3. Situational and Behavioral Triggers

* Proactive Traffic Enforcement: For the non-disabled general population, traffic violations (speeding, expired registration, faulty equipment) serve as the single most common catalyst for police-initiated contact. [1, 8] 
* Public Substance Use: Visible indicators of illicit drug or heavy alcohol use in public spaces strongly predict officer-initiated stops and subsequent arrests, serving as a primary trigger for street-level policing. [4] 
* Offense Severity: When an officer observes an active misdemeanor or suspicious activity, the legal severity of the perceived offense acts as a direct, objective driver for the initiation of the stop and the likelihood of an arrest. [13]
