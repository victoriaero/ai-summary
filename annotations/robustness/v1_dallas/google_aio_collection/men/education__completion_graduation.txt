Men graduate from college at lower rates than women, with a 4-year completion rate of 43% for men compared to 54% for women. [1] 
## Personal Motivation and Career Views

* Desire for a degree: About 34% of men without a bachelor’s degree state that a major reason they did not finish is simply not wanting a degree, compared to 25% of women.
* Perceived career necessity: Roughly 26% of men cite not needing additional education for their desired career path as a primary deterrent, versus 20% of women. [2] 

## Attendance Patterns and Academic Momentum

* Disrupted attendance: Men have higher rates of part-time enrollment and take more breaks from school, which significantly limits persistence and reduces on-time graduation. [3] 
* Gateway courses: Early completion of foundational math and English courses strongly predicts degree progression and completion for male students. [4] 
* Learning environments: Traditional K-12 and collegiate classrooms often lean heavily toward sedentary and auditory learning styles, which can disadvantage boys and young men who thrive in hands-on environments. [5] 

## Financial Pressures and Employment

* Need to work: Financial responsibilities and the immediate necessity to work to support oneself or family lead many men to delay or halt their education.
* Affordability: Roughly 39% of men cite college costs as a major hurdle to finishing their education. [2, 6] 

## Social Integration and Support Systems

* Campus engagement: Participating in academic clubs, peer mentoring, and campus sports increases the likelihood of graduation; men who lack social integration on campus drop out at higher rates. [3, 7] 
* Guidance and mentorship: A lack of clear academic guidance, positive role models, and institutional support heavily impacts completion rates, particularly for minority male students. [8, 9]
