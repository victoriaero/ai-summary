Traditional masculine norms, systemic provider communication gaps, and structural healthcare designs heavily influence person-centered care (PCC) for men. [1, 2, 3] 
## Societal and Cultural Factors

* Masculine Norms and Stoicism: Adherence to traditional ideals—such as self-reliance, emotional control, and physical toughness—frequently causes men to suppress symptoms, view vulnerability as weakness, and delay seeking preventive care. [4, 5] 
* Stigma Around Mental Health: Societal expectations make it difficult for men to voice psychological distress, increasing the risk of unaddressed depression or anxiety unless care is framed through an action-oriented, transparent lens. [3, 4] 

## Interpersonal and Provider Factors

* Communication Style: Men respond best to clear, goal-oriented, and empathetic communication. Dismissive or overly clinical interactions rupture trust quickly, whereas providers who use collaborative dialogue and active listening foster better engagement. [2, 6] 
* Provider Gender Preferences: Some men prefer male providers for specific physical or professional evaluations, while others value female providers for empathy during sensitive discussions. Accommodating these preferences impacts overall satisfaction and comfort levels. [7] 
* Support Networks: The involvement of partners, family members, or peers heavily influences whether men recognize symptoms early and adhere to long-term treatment plans. [1, 8] 

## Organizational and Health System Factors

* Structural Barriers: Rigid appointment times, long wait times, and traditional clinic atmospheres that feel acute-care focused or unwelcoming discourage routine preventive visits. [1, 5] 
* Flexibility and Access: Integrating digital options like telehealth, streamlined scheduling, and community-based outreach bridges the gap for men balancing heavy work or family-provision commitments. [1, 9, 10]
