Multiple environmental, economic, and structural factors influence neighborhood quality for people with disabilities in housing, shaping their health, safety, and independence. [1, 2] 
## Physical Accessibility and Infrastructure

* Universal Design: Neighborhoods that feature curb cuts, functioning sidewalk ramps, paved pathways, and accessible public transit directly affect a resident's mobility and independence. [3] 
* Housing Upkeep: High rates of physical deterioration, severe upkeep problems, or structural hazards (such as abandoned buildings and window bars) disproportionately degrade the living environment of disabled households. [4, 5] 

## Safety and Social Environment

* Neighborhood Crime: High crime rates correlate strongly with physical and going-outside-the-home disability prevalence, limiting community participation and outdoor safety. [1, 2, 5] 
* Collective Efficacy: Strong social cohesion, neighbor trust, and active community networks improve resident well-being and help disabled individuals remain independent. [1] 

## Economic and Resource Disadvantage

* Poverty and Cost Burdens: Severe housing cost burdens and lower average incomes (exacerbated by reliance on fixed incomes like SSI) restrict housing choices, forcing disabled individuals into resource-disadvantaged areas. [4, 6, 7] 
* Access to Services: Proximity to medical care, community-based long-term services and supports (LTSS), and public assistance programs dictates whether a neighborhood can support daily needs. [5, 8] 

## Systemic and Policy Barriers

* Community Integration: Policies promoting mixed-income, inclusive housing prevent segregated pockets and foster welcoming environments across civic and social life. [3] 
* Fair Housing Enforcement: Resistance to reasonable accommodation and modification requests by property owners restricts housing availability and quality within desirable neighborhoods. [9, 10]
