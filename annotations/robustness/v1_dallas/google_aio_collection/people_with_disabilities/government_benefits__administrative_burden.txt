Factors influencing administrative burden for people with disabilities in government benefits are generally divided into three primary cost categories identified in public policy research (such as Herd and Moynihan's framework), compounded by systemic and structural hurdles: [1] 
## Three Core Types of Administrative Costs

* Learning Costs: The time, effort, and cognitive strain required to find out what programs exist, whether one qualifies, what documentation is needed, and how to apply (e.g., navigating fragmented agency websites, confusing terminology, or multiple overlapping safety-net programs like SSDI, SSI, SNAP, and Medicaid). [1, 2] 
* Compliance Costs: The concrete and financial demands of proving eligibility, which include gathering decades of medical records, paying for doctor notes or legal representation, handling inflexible or repeated in-person appointments, and responding to strict administrative deadlines. [1, 3] 
* Psychological Costs: The emotional toll, stress, loss of personal autonomy from intrusive supervision, and social stigma or frustration experienced when dealing with complex, slow, or punitive systems. [1] 

## Systemic and Structural Factors

* Complex Medical and Proof Requirements: Agencies often require extensive historical medical documentation, continuous re-certifications, and low initial approval rates (roughly one-third for some core disability programs), placing a heavy burden on applicants whose disabilities themselves may make gathering records difficult. [3] 
* Restrictive Financial and Asset Limits: Strict asset caps (such as the traditional $2,000 asset limit for SSI), marriage penalties, and work incentive "cliffs" force recipients to constantly track and report micro-changes in their financial status to avoid losing vital aid. [3] 
* Fragmented "No Wrong Door" Deficits and Service Silos: Lack of a universal application or cross-agency data sharing means applicants must repeat the same information across multiple separate programs (housing, food assistance, healthcare, and paratransit). [3, 4] 
* Geographic and Digital Divides: Rural residents often lack local field offices (such as Social Security Administration offices), forcing reliance on phone or online portals that may be inaccessible or unreliable, whereas physical or language barriers (such as limited English proficiency) drastically lengthen wait times and drop-off rates. [5, 6] 
* Underfunded Agency Staffing: Chronic underfunding of administrative expenses at agencies like the Social Security Administration leads to long processing delays (sometimes averaging nearly a year for hearings) and reduced customer service availability. [4, 7] 

Read more about these challenges through the [Center for American Progress Report](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/how-dehumanizing-administrative-burdens-harm-disabled-people/) or review administrative reduction strategies via the [Administrative Conference of the United States](https://www.acus.gov/document/identifying-and-reducing-burdens-public-administrative-processes).
