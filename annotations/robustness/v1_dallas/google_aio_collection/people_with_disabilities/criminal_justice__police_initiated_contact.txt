Police-initiated contact with individuals who have disabilities is shaped by a complex interplay of behavioral, institutional, and demographic factors. These factors can disproportionately draw individuals with intellectual, developmental, physical, or psychiatric disabilities into the criminal justice system.
Several key factors heavily influence when and how law enforcement initiates contact:
1. Misinterpretation of Behavioral Manifestations
Police culture and training rely heavily on behavioral cues to assess compliance and danger. However, common manifestations of certain disabilities are easily misinterpreted by officers:
• Communication and Eye Contact: Individuals on the autism spectrum or those with cognitive disabilities may avoid eye contact, repeat words (echolalia), or give highly literal answers. Officers frequently misperceive a lack of eye contact as suspiciousness, guilt, or evasion.
• Physical Movements and Motor Cues: Conditions like cerebral palsy, Tourette syndrome, or autism can involve involuntary movements, uncoordinated gaits, or repetitive self-soothing behaviors (stimming). Officers often mistake these physical markers for drug or alcohol intoxication.
• Flight-or-Fight and Sensory Overload: High-stress environments packed with flashing police lights, sirens, and shouting can trigger severe sensory overload. An individual trying to run away or pulling their arms away to avoid physical touch may be viewed as resisting arrest or acting aggressively, prompting police to escalate force.
2. Disproportionate Rates of Victimization
People with disabilities are far more likely to experience crimes than those without disabilities. According to data highlighted by The Arc and the U.S. Bureau of Justice Statistics, individuals with cognitive disabilities face significantly higher rates of violent crime and sexual assault. Consequently, a large portion of police-initiated contact stems from officers responding to scenes where an individual with a disability is a victim or witness. If communication barriers exist, these encounters can inadvertently shift, causing the victim to be wrongly treated as a suspect.
3. Lack of Law Enforcement Policy and Training
Systemic and institutional gaps heavily influence the frequency and outcome of police contacts:
• The "Danger Imperative": A study published in Sociology notes that because police are trained to prioritize their own safety against unpredictable threats, neurodivergent behaviors introduce uncertainty. This uncertainty often leads to proactive police control tactics, making individuals with intellectual or developmental disabilities significantly more likely to be handcuffed or detained during discretionary stops.
• Inadequate Standardized Training: Research highlighted by the RAND Corporation shows a widespread lack of mandatory, standardized training across law enforcement agencies regarding disability awareness, alternative communication, and de-escalation. Without explicit guidelines, officers lack the data and skills to utilize deflection or pre-arrest diversion programs.
4. Vulnerability to Coercion and "Invisible" Disabilities
Many cognitive and developmental disabilities lack visible physical markers. When an impairment is "invisible," an officer may expect a standard response sequence and interpret slow comprehension as deliberate non-compliance. Furthermore, due to a desire for social acceptance or a heightened suggestibility, some individuals with cognitive impairments can be easily manipulated by others into participating in or taking the blame for illegal activities, leading straight to police intervention.
5. Intersectionality and Demographics
Demographic overlap vastly alters the likelihood of police contact. Individuals who have disabilities and belong to marginalized groups—such as people of color, indigenous communities, or low-income neighborhoods—experience much higher rates of police intervention. For instance, youth with conditions like ADHD are stopped and subjected to more intrusive police contact at drastically disparate rates if they are Black or Hispanic compared to their white peers.
