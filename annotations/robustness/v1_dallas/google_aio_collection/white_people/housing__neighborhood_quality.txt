Historical public policies, socioeconomic proxies, and institutional networks heavily influence neighborhood quality and residential choices for White people in housing. [1, 2] 
## Historical and Structural Factors

* Legacy of Public Policy: Decades of government-backed practices, such as redlining and racially restrictive covenants, historically directed investment and resource allocation into predominantly White communities while disinvesting in communities of color. [1, 3, 4] 
* Wealth Accumulation and Returns: Higher historical homeownership rates and property appreciation for White households allow greater access to well-resourced neighborhoods and higher-value housing stock. [5, 6] 

## Socioeconomic Proxies and Perceptions

* Perceived Neighborhood Indicators: Research cited by the [Kinder Institute for Urban Research](https://kinder.rice.edu/urbanedge/residential-segregation-rewards-whites-while-punishing-people-color) indicates that White homebuyers often prioritize low crime rates, high-performing schools, and appreciating property values. [2] 
* The "Racial Proxy" Effect: Sociological studies note that demographic composition is sometimes used subconsciously or overtly as a proxy for neighborhood problems like disinvestment or declining property values, affecting where White residents choose to search for housing. [2, 7] 

## Social Networks and Real Estate Practices

* Homogeneous Networks: Housing searches are frequently mediated through family, friends, and real estate professionals who operate within predominantly White social networks, channeling buyers toward similar neighborhoods. [2] 
* Built Environment Preferences: Preferences for specific neighborhood aesthetics—such as a higher concentration of detached single-family homes, lower proportions of multi-family housing, and greater amounts of green space—strongly align with patterns found in predominantly White census tracts. [8, 9, 10]
