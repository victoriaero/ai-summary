Transgender people face unique administrative burdens in accessing government benefits due to ID incongruence, discretionary bureaucratic enforcement, and intersecting systemic inequalities. [1, 2, 3] 
Administrative burden consists of three core components: learning costs (figuring out eligibility and rules), compliance costs (submitting paperwork and proving status), and psychological costs (stress, stigma, and fear of mistreatment). For transgender applicants navigating programs like Medicaid, SNAP, or unemployment insurance, several specific factors intensify these costs: [4, 5] 
## Key Factors Influencing Administrative Burden

* Identity Document (ID) Incongruence: Mismatches between a person's legal name, gender marker on official identification, and their lived presentation create immediate friction. Applicants frequently must disclose private medical or personal history just to prove their identity matches an older record. [6] 
* Discretionary Bureaucratic Power: High clerk and judicial discretion can lead to arbitrary paperwork rejections, inconsistent enforcement of agency rules, and prolonged processing times. [1] 
* Intersectional and Racial Disparities: Race, class, and housing stability deeply shape these interactions. Studies on legal and administrative processes show that extreme poverty, unstable housing, and criminal legal system contact compound compliance hurdles, disproportionately impacting Black and Latinx transgender individuals. [1, 7] 
* Stigma and Fear of Mistreatment: Interacting with agency caseworkers often triggers high psychological costs. Fear of verbal harassment, discrimination, or outright denial of benefits based on gender identity deters individuals from applying for or retaining vital public assistance. [3, 6, 8] 
* Contradictory Agency Policies: Inconsistencies in sex and gender classification policies across local, state, and federal agencies create confusion regarding what documentation is legally acceptable to secure benefits. [2]
