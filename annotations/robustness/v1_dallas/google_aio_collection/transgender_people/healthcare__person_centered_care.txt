Provider knowledge, inclusive communication, and systemic support significantly influence person-centered care for transgender people in healthcare. [1, 2, 3] 
## Provider Knowledge and Attitude

* Competence and training: A major barrier is the lack of formal education in medical and nursing curricula regarding transgender health and gender-affirming care. When providers lack awareness, patients often have to educate them during consultations. [1, 4] 
* Sensitivity and respect: Using correct names and pronouns, avoiding misgendering, and adopting a non-judgmental attitude foster a culture of trust. [5, 6, 7] 
* Decoupling gender from anatomy: Clinicians should base preventive screenings and physical exams on current organs and anatomy rather than assumptions tied to gender identity. [8, 9, 10] 

## Inclusive Communication and Environment

* Inclusive intake forms: Collecting sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) data respectfully, using gender-neutral language, and allowing pronoun sharing on badges or intake sheets improve patient comfort. [1, 6, 11] 
* Trauma-informed care: Physical examinations can trigger past trauma or discomfort; trauma-guided approaches and transparent communication empower patients and reduce anxiety. [1, 6, 11] 
* Physical infrastructure: Health systems improve experiences by providing all-gender restrooms, private rooms, and adaptable electronic medical records (EMRs). [6] 

## Systemic and Financial Factors

* Insurance and affordability: High out-of-pocket costs and restrictive insurance policies or exclusions for hormone therapy and transition-related procedures create major financial barriers. [4, 10, 12] 
* Geographic access: Shortages of local, knowledgeable subspecialists force many transgender individuals to travel long distances for appropriate care. [4, 9, 13] 
* Informed consent vs. gatekeeping: Moving away from restrictive gatekeeping toward patient-centered, informed consent models supports self-determination and positive health outcomes. [3, 10, 14]
