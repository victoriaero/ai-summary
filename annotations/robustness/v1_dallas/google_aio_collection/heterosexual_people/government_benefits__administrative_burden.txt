Administrative burden for heterosexual people in government benefits is shaped by intersectional factors like income, race, gender, and cognitive or physical resources rather than sexual orientation alone. [1, 2] 
The [Administrative Conference of the United States](https://www.acus.gov/document/identifying-and-reducing-burdens-public-administrative-processes) outlines how bureaucratic interactions impose three main types of costs on individuals: [3] 
## Core Types of Administrative Costs

* Learning Costs: The time and effort required to discover what benefits exist and understand complex eligibility rules. [4] 
* Compliance Costs: The "time taxes" involved in submitting paperwork, documenting eligibility, waiting on hold, or attending in-person appointments. [5] 
* Psychological Costs: The stress, loss of autonomy, and stigma tied to navigating bureaucratic systems or receiving public assistance. [4] 

## Key Influencing Factors

* Socioeconomic Status and Class: Lower-income heterosexual individuals face much steeper compliance and learning costs because the time spent dealing with paperwork directly competes with hourly work or caregiving responsibilities. [5] 
* Human and Cognitive Capital: Factors like scarcity, chronic health problems, and age-related cognitive decline drain the executive functioning required to navigate convoluted bureaucratic rules. [6] 
* Social Location and Intersectional Identity: Research highlighted by the [Russell Sage Foundation](https://www.rsfjournal.org/content/10/4/84) shows that a person's broader social location—including gender, age, and ability—shapes how easily they can access support buffers or manage state surveillance. For example, studies on public assistance indicate that while heterosexual minority groups or white applicants experience different baseline community social networks or information-sharing resources, economic disadvantage consistently magnifies the friction of administrative hurdles.
