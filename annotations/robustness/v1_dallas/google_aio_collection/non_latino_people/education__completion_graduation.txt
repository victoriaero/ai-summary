Educational completion and graduation rates for non-Latino groups—such as White, Asian, Black, and Indigenous students—are influenced by a combination of socioeconomic, institutional, and demographic factors [0.14]. [1, 2] 
## Socioeconomic and Financial Factors

* Household Income and Wealth: Family income directly impacts a student's ability to pay for college tuition, housing, and materials without needing to work long hours, which correlates with higher 6-year graduation rates for White and Asian students compared to lower-income peers. [1, 3] 
* Parental Education Level: First-generation college students across all racial and ethnic backgrounds face higher dropout rates because they often lack familial guidance in navigating academic systems, financial aid, and institutional bureaucracy.
* Working While Enrolled: Full-time employment while studying heavily reduces completion likelihood, forcing many non-traditional or lower-income students to extend their timeline or drop out. [4] 

## Institutional and Academic Preparation

* K-12 School Quality: Access to well-funded primary and secondary schools with advanced placement (AP) courses, rigorous math/science tracks, and effective tutoring heavily dictates college readiness and timely high school graduation. [5] 
* Guidance and Counseling: Availability of active high school counselors who assist with college applications, standardized test preparation (SAT/ACT), and financial aid navigation significantly increases post-secondary matriculation and graduation. [6, 7] 
* Institutional Type and Resources: Graduation rates vary drastically by college type; public 4-year universities, private non-profits, and for-profit institutions offer vastly different levels of academic advising, mental health support, and financial buffering. [2] 

## Demographic and Social Gaps

* Gender Disparities: Across almost all non-Latino racial groups, female students complete high school and graduate from college at noticeably higher rates than male students. [2, 5, 8] 
* Systemic and Racial Disparities: National data from the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES) shows persistent gaps, with Asian and White students achieving higher 6-year bachelor's completion rates (roughly 74% and 68%) compared to Black (46%) and American Indian/Alaska Native (39%) students, driven by varying community resources and systemic barriers.
