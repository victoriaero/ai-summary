Person-centered care (PCC) for non-Latino populations—such as Black, White, or other racial and ethnic groups—is influenced by a combination of patient-level, clinician-level, and systemic factors that shape trust, communication, and treatment adherence. [1, 2, 3] 
## Patient-Level Factors

* Health and Digital Literacy: Differences in an individual's ability to access, process, and understand basic health information or digital health tools directly affect their engagement in shared decision-making. [1] 
* Socioeconomic Status and Social Determinants of Health (SDOH): Income stability, housing, and access to reliable transportation heavily dictate a patient’s capacity to follow longitudinal care plans. [4] 
* Cultural and Spiritual Beliefs: Individual value systems, religious or spiritual perspectives on illness and healing, and the integration of complementary or alternative medicine influence how patients perceive and accept Western medical interventions. [3, 5] 
* Family and Community Involvement: The degree to which patients prefer to include family members in medical briefings and health-related decisions varies across different non-Latino cultural groups. [3, 6] 

## Clinician- and System-Level Factors

* Implicit Bias and Perceived Discrimination: Unconscious biases by providers or perceived discrimination (such as clinicians incorrectly assuming drug-seeking behavior or showing cold treatment) severely damage trust, particularly among Black patients. [1, 7] 
* Cultural Competence and Sensitivity: A lack of provider training in cultural awareness, active listening, and respecting differing frameworks of health and illness creates major communication barriers. [1, 4] 
* Shared Decision-Making Skills: Deficits in clinician communication skills required to negotiate treatment plans respectfully can lead patients to feel unheard or coerced into unwanted medical procedures. [1, 4] 
* Insurance-Based Discrimination: Structural and institutional barriers, including disparities linked to insurance coverage quality, affect how healthcare staff interact with both Black and White patients. [3, 7]
