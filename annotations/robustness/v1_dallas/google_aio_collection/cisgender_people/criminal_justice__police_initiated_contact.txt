In criminological and sociological research, police-initiated contact—such as traffic stops, street stops, and discretionary proactive policing—is driven by a complex mix of systemic, situational, and demographic factors. For cisgender people, while their gender expression generally aligns with societal expectations (shielding them from the specific transphobic profiling or "walking while trans" biases experienced by transgender individuals), their contact with law enforcement is heavily shaped by the intersection of binary gender norms, race, socioeconomic status, and geographical factors. [1, 2, 3, 4] 
According to data from the [Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS)](https://bjs.ojp.gov/) and criminological studies, the primary factors influencing police-initiated contact for cisgender individuals include:
## 1. Demographic Disparities (The Intersections of Gender, Race, and Age)

* The Male Penalty vs. Chivalry Hypothesis: Statistically, cisgender men face significantly higher rates of police-initiated contact (such as traffic and street stops) and harsher downstream criminal justice penalties than cisgender women. Criminologists often attribute this to the "male penalty" (the perception of men as inherently more threatening) and the "chivalry hypothesis," where cisgender women are sometimes viewed by officers as more vulnerable or less dangerous. [4, 5] 
* Race and Gender Intersectionality: The protective effects of being a cisgender woman vary drastically by race. For example, data analyzed by the [Prison Policy Initiative](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/) highlights that Black cisgender women are 17% more likely to be subjected to traffic stops than white cisgender women, and are arrested during those stops at triple the rate. [6] 
* Age: Young cisgender adults—specifically those aged 18 to 24—experience the highest aggregate rates of non-voluntary, police-initiated stops across all demographics. [7] 

## 2. Situational and Structural Context

* Socioeconomic Status and Neighborhood Effects: Proactive policing tactics, such as "hot spots" policing, are heavily concentrated in low-income or socially disadvantaged neighborhoods. Cisgender individuals living in or traveling through these areas experience elevated surveillance and a higher mathematical probability of police-initiated stops regardless of individual behavior. [1, 8] 
* Involvement in Informal or Street-Based Economies: Studies tracking street-based economies, such as survival sex work, demonstrate that structural criminalization heavily dictates police contact. Research shows that patrol officers heavily police cisgender female sex workers, primarily utilizing discretionary "move-along" tactics to manage neighborhood visibility and displacement rather than executing immediate arrests. [9] 

## 3. Institutional Policies and Officer Discretion

* Same-Gender Search Policies: Police internal constraints can paradoxically alter who officers choose to stop and search. For example, in multi-officer units or male-dominated police forces, strict regulations regarding same-gender searches (e.g., requiring a female officer to search a female suspect) can disincentivize male officers from initiating proactive field stops or searches on cisgender women, shifting their discretionary focus more heavily onto cisgender men. [10] 
* Offense Severity and Behavior: In explicit traffic violations or observed property/violent offenses, situational characteristics and offense severity serve as the most direct predictors of a police-initiated stop and subsequent arrest, often overshadowing demographic variations in that specific moment. [11, 12]
