Administrative burden for cisgender people accessing government benefits is driven by three main types of costs—learning, compliance, and psychological—which are shaped by intersecting socioeconomic, racial, and structural factors. [1, 2] 
## Core Categories of Administrative Burden

* Learning Costs: The time and effort required to find out that a program exists, understand eligibility rules, and figure out how to navigate the application system. [2] 
* Compliance Costs: The "time taxes" and monetary costs of fulfilling program rules, such as gathering extensive documentation, waiting on hold, traveling to offices, or attending in-person interviews. [2] 
* Psychological Costs: The emotional toll, social stigma, and perceived loss of personal autonomy experienced when interacting with public assistance programs. [2, 3] 

## Key Influencing Factors

* Racial and Ethnic Disparities: Research shows that administrative experiences vary across racial lines. For instance, studies on programs like SNAP and Medicaid indicate that white applicants sometimes report higher baseline learning and compliance hurdles due to differences in community social capital and generational familiarity with systems, whereas Black and minority applicants report significantly higher rates of unfair treatment and discrimination from agency staff. [4, 5, 6] 
* Socioeconomic Status and Social Capital: Individuals with lower incomes, less access to digital tools, or limited flexible work hours face higher compliance costs. Conversely, collectivist cultural networks or higher community social capital can facilitate information-sharing and lower learning barriers. [6] 
* Gender and Reproductive Labor: Administrative burdens are heavily gendered; women—including cisgender women—often shoulder higher compliance and psychological costs due to disproportionate caregiving responsibilities and interactions with policies regulating reproductive and family health. [7] 
* Disability and Infrastructure: Physical or cognitive disabilities amplify compliance burdens, especially when programs demand complex paperwork, rigid in-person attendance, or lack accessible digital and transportation infrastructure. [8]
