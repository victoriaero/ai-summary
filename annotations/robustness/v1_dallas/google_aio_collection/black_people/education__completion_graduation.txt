A complex mix of systemic, economic, and institutional factors influences educational completion and graduation rates for Black students across K-12 and higher education. [1, 2, 3] 
## Pre-College and K-12 Factors

* School Segregation and Funding: Persistent racial segregation in housing and public schools often channels Black students into underfunded K-12 institutions with higher poverty rates, less experienced teachers, and fewer advanced placement courses. [1, 2, 4, 5] 
* Disciplinary Disparities: Disproportionate and harsher disciplinary practices (such as suspensions and expulsions) push Black students—particularly Black males—out of instructional settings, disrupting academic momentum. [2, 3, 6] 
* Teacher Expectations: Lower academic expectations or racial microaggressions from educators can diminish student confidence and reduce college readiness. [1, 3, 7] 

## Financial and Economic Barriers

* Wealth and Family SES: Unequal parental wealth linked to historical housing discrimination means Black families often have fewer liquid resources to pay for college out-of-pocket. [1] 
* Student Loan Debt: Reliance on loans creates disproportionate financial strain, increasing the likelihood that students will stop out or struggle to complete degrees. [2, 7] 
* External Responsibilities: Black college students are statistically more likely to balance coursework with full-time employment or family caregiving duties, elevating stop-out rates. [8] 

## Higher Education Institutional Environment

* Type of Institution: Disparities exist in where students enroll; Black students are disproportionately represented in under-resourced community colleges, for-profit institutions, or public universities with lower spending per student, which often report lower completion rates than elite or well-funded private institutions. [9, 10] 
* Campus Climate and Support: Low faculty diversity, absence of culturally relevant mentoring, and campus microaggressions negatively affect persistence. Conversely, robust academic advising, peer networks, and supportive environments—such as those found at many Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs)—strongly boost completion rates. [1, 7, 11]
