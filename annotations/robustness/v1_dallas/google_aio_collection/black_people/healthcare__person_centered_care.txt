Several key interpersonal, systemic, and cultural factors influence person-centered care (PCC) for Black people in healthcare.
## Provider Communication and Bias

* Active listening: Clinicians who practice active listening and cultural sensitivity significantly improve the quality of care. [1, 2] 
* Dismissiveness and bias: Unconscious or explicit racial biases, stereotyping, and clinician dismissiveness often lead to poor communication and unmet needs. [1, 3] 
* Shared decision-making: Black patients frequently report being left out of medical choices or coerced into treatments they do not understand, lowering the quality of person-centered care. [2] 

## Workforce Representation and Cultural Concordance

* Diverse providers: Higher representation of Black physicians in the workforce is linked to reduced health and mortality disparities. [4] 
* Cultural congruence: Seeking or having culturally concordant providers helps build trust, eases communication, and fosters a safe environment for Black patients. [3, 4] 

## Historical Mistrust and Systemic Factors

* Historical context: Past exploitation and systemic discrimination—such as the Tuskegee Syphilis Study—created a legacy of medical mistrust that influences how safely and fully Black patients engage with the healthcare system. [5] 
* Social determinants of health (SDOH): Factors like income inequality, housing stability, and lack of transportation heavily shape health outcomes and limit access to continuous, person-centered support. [2, 6] 
* Technological bias: Clinical algorithms and artificial intelligence tools that incorporate biased historical data or race-based adjustments can distort assessments and reduce quality of care. [7]
