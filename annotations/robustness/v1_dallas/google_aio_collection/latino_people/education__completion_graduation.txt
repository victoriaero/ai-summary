Several key socioeconomic, academic, and institutional factors influence high school and college graduation rates for Latino students in the United States. [1, 2] 
## Financial and Economic Pressures

* Family support needs: Many Latino students delay or stop their education because they need to work and financially support their families.
* College affordability: High tuition costs and limited access to financial aid or grants prevent many from finishing degrees.
* Enrollment type: A large majority of Latino students initially enroll in community colleges or open-access institutions, often for financial reasons, which historically feature lower bachelor’s degree completion rates. [1, 3, 4, 5] 

## Academic Preparation and Background

* First-generation status: A high percentage of Latino college students are the first in their families to attend college, meaning they have less familial guidance navigating admissions and campus resources.
* High school preparation: Gaps in rigorous college-prep courses and academic counseling in underfunded high schools limit college readiness.
* Language barriers: Limited English proficiency can create obstacles during schooling and standardized testing. [2, 4, 6] 

## Institutional Support

* Representation and resources: The presence of supportive faculty and targeted student services—such as those recognized by [Excelencia in Education](https://www.edexcelencia.org/latino-college-completion)—help improve retention and graduation. [1, 7, 8] 
* Institutional investment: Colleges that enroll high numbers of Latino students often lack the massive endowments of selective universities, affecting the availability of comprehensive student support. [3, 9]
