Financial affordability is the single largest barrier and influential factor cited by women regarding college completion and non-completion. [1] 
Women currently outpace men in college enrollment and overall graduation rates, earning over 57% of bachelor's degrees and higher shares of advanced degrees. However, specific socioeconomic, financial, and institutional factors heavily shape women's completion and graduation outcomes. [1, 2] 
## Financial and Labor Market Factors

* Affordability: Around 44% of women cite college costs and unaffordability as a primary reason for not completing their degrees or dropping out, outpacing men. [1] 
* Student Debt Burden: Women are more likely to carry student loan debt across public, non-profit, and for-profit sectors, holding a higher median debt load post-graduation ($20,625 vs. $15,000 for men), which can impact persistence. [2, 3] 
* Labor Market Returns: Improvements in women's expected financial returns and career earnings from holding a college degree serve as major drivers for initial enrollment and persistence to graduation. [4] 
* Work Responsibilities: Approximately 36% of non-completers cite the necessity of working while studying as a barrier to finishing their education. [1] 

## Academic and Institutional Support

* First-Year Momentum: Continuous full-time or stable part-time enrollment, high credit success rates, and completing foundational college math or English in the first year strongly predict degree completion. [5] 
* Institutional Type: Women disproportionately enroll in for-profit institutions, which historically show lower overall completion rates compared to public or private non-profit four-year universities. [2, 6] 
* Academic Engagement: Access to proactive campus resources—such as academic advising, peer mentoring, and counseling services—helps mitigate dropout risks. [6] 

## Background and Demographics

* Parental Background: Students with college-educated mothers realize higher utility from higher education and demonstrate significantly higher college completion rates.
* Race and Ethnicity: Minority women (such as Black and Hispanic undergraduates) face distinct barriers, with Hispanic and Black adults overrepresented among those citing severe affordability challenges and risk of non-completion. [1, 4, 7]
