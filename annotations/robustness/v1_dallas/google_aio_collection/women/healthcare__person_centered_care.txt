Person-centered care for women in healthcare is heavily influenced by individual patient factors, provider communication skills, and broader organizational or systemic policies. [1, 2] 
## Individual Patient Factors

* Demographics and Background: A woman's age, socioeconomic status, cultural background, and lifestyle shape her unique health priorities and expectations. [3, 4] 
* Identity and Diversity: Specific needs apply to diverse groups, including transgender women, women with language barriers, or marginalized populations like women in prison or domestic abuse survivors. [4, 5] 
* Past Experiences: Previous negative or traumatic medical encounters strongly affect how safe a woman feels and how openly she communicates with clinicians. [4] 
* Life Stage: Care must extend beyond reproductive and maternal health to encompass lifelong wellness, mental health, and chronic disease management. [5] 

## Provider and Interpersonal Factors

* Communication and Empathy: Active listening, non-judgmental attitudes, and validating a woman's emotional concerns build a strong therapeutic alliance and prevent the dismissal of symptoms. [2, 6] 
* Provider Gender and Preferences: Some women prefer female providers for empathy and intimate care, though technical skill and professional respect remain paramount across all genders. [7] 
* Autonomy and Shared Decision-Making: Empowering women to actively participate in choices regarding their treatment fosters trust and control over their health outcomes. [8, 9] 

## Systemic and Organizational Factors

* Care Models and Continuity: Navigating away from siloed specialists toward long-term primary care relationships ensures a provider understands a patient's full medical history. [5, 8] 
* Resource Availability: Institutional support, affordable insurance coverage, translator services, and reduced consultation fees directly improve accessibility and satisfaction. [1, 7]
