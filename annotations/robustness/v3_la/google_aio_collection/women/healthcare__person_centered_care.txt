Person-centered care for women in healthcare is heavily influenced by individual patient characteristics, provider communication styles, and broader organizational frameworks. [1, 2, 3] 
## Individual Patient Factors

* Demographics and Background: Age, socioeconomic status, cultural background, and lifestyle shape women's health needs, preferences, and expectations. [2] 
* Identity and Experience: Gender identity (such as the needs of trans women), past negative or traumatic medical experiences, and social pressures affect how safe a patient feels and how they interact with providers. [2] 
* Socioeconomic Status: Wealth, employment, literacy, and marital status directly correlate with higher levels of respectful, person-centered maternity and general care. [4, 5] 

## Provider and Clinical Communication

* Relational Quality: Active listening, empathy, a non-judgmental disposition, and avoiding rushed consultations build trust and therapeutic alliances. [3, 6, 7] 
* Autonomy and Involvement: Acknowledging a woman's emotions and involving her (and her family or caregivers, with consent) in informed decision-making enhances satisfaction and dignity. [2, 6, 7] 
* Overcoming Barriers: Utilizing translators or community facilitators helps bridge language gaps for diverse populations. [2] 

## Organizational and System-Level Factors

* Care Models and Continuity: Consistent care models—such as dedicated primary care continuity rather than fragmented specialist visits—allow providers to truly understand a patient’s long-term history and values. [1, 3, 8] 
* Resources and Support: Adequate institutional funding, stakeholder backing, and supportive management policies dictate whether clinicians have the capacity and time to deliver holistic care. [1, 3] 
* Provider Education: Training that addresses gender biases, cultural competency, and relational care skills ensures all providers are equipped to meet diverse healthcare needs. [6, 9]
