Women in the U.S. achieve higher college enrollment and graduation rates than men (with a national six-year graduation rate of roughly 67.9% compared to 61.3% for men), but several key economic, social, and institutional factors influence their completion outcomes. [1, 2, 3] 
## Economic and Financial Factors

* Affordability and Debt: About 44% of women cite college costs as a primary reason for non-completion. Additionally, 71% of women carry student loan debt post-graduation with higher median balances than their male peers, which can increase financial stress. [1, 4] 
* Labor Market Returns: Improvements in women’s expected earnings and career returns with a college degree serve as a major driver for higher enrollment and persistence rates. [3] 
* Work Commitments: Balancing employment while studying is cited by over a third of students as a hurdle to finishing their degrees. [1] 

## Social and Family Background

* Maternal Education: Higher educational attainment in mothers strongly correlates with higher academic expectations and completion rates for daughters.
* Utility and Aspirations: Women often report finding higher personal growth and career utility in attaining degrees, driving continuous engagement. [1, 3, 5] 

## Institutional and Health Support

* Mental Health: Cisgender women report higher rates of anxiety and depression in higher education settings, making access to on-campus counseling and mental health prioritization vital for degree completion.
* Campus Engagement: Utilizing academic advising, mentoring programs, peer connections, and institutional financial planning heavily reduces the risk of dropping out. [4, 6, 7]
