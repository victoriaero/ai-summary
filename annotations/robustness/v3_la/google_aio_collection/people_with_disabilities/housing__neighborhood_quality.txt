Several key physical, social, and economic factors influence neighborhood quality and housing stability for people with disabilities.
## Physical and Built Environment

* Accessibility: The presence of wheelchair ramps, accessible sidewalks, curb cuts, and elevators directly determines whether residents can leave their homes and move safely through the community. [1, 2] 
* Physical Disorder: Visible blight, such as abandoned buildings, trash accumulation, poor street lighting, and structural disrepair, correlates with higher physical and developmental risks. [1, 3, 4] 
* Proximity to Services: Living close to public transit, medical facilities, grocery stores, and community support networks helps reduce isolation and improves daily independence. [5] 

## Social and Safety Factors

* Neighborhood Safety: High crime rates and violence disproportionately impact disabled households, limiting outdoor mobility and increasing psychological distress. [1, 6] 
* Collective Efficacy: Strong social cohesion and mutual trust among neighbors foster supportive environments that improve overall health outcomes and independence. [1] 

## Economic and Systemic Barriers

* Affordability: Severe cost burdens limit housing choices, as many disabled individuals rely on fixed incomes like SSI that fall significantly short of average rental costs. [2, 6, 7] 
* Residential Disadvantage: Systemic barriers often force people with disabilities into under-resourced neighborhoods with fewer amenities, higher natural disaster risks, and older housing stock requiring severe upkeep. [3, 6]
