Person-centered care (PCC) for people with disabilities in healthcare is shaped by a combination of communication factors, provider competencies, environmental accessibility, and systemic or policy structures. [1, 2, 3] 
## Key Factors Influencing Person-Centered Care

* Communication and Information Access:
* Direct patient communication and tailored information (such as easy-read formats, large print, visual aids, or sign language) build active involvement.
   * Overloading patients with verbal information or ignoring alternative communication devices hinders decision-making. [1, 4, 5, 6] 
* Provider Attitudes and Competency:
* Provider inexperience, biased assumptions, or a purely medical/symptom-focused orientation reduce patient autonomy and cause health disparities.
   * Respecting patient autonomy—while balancing safety and informed consent—requires empathy and understanding of non-verbal cues or long-term caregiver insights. [1, 2, 3, 4] 
* Physical and Equipment Accessibility:
* Accessible physical facilities, adjustable examination tables, lift equipment, and alternative diagnostic positioning directly impact whether routine and preventive care can occur. [1, 3] 
* Time and Continuity of Care:
* Extended appointment durations allow enough time to communicate and perform proper examinations without rushing.
   * Seeing the same consistent, familiar healthcare provider over time improves trust, dignity, and accurate tracking of medical history. [1, 3, 5, 7] 
* Involvement of Support Networks:
* Engaging family members, chosen representatives, or legal guardians (while prioritizing the individual's voice to the maximum extent possible) supports meaningful goal-setting and self-determination. [4, 8, 9] 
* Systemic and Policy Frameworks:
* Moving away from rigid, institutional or paternalistic models toward a biopsychosocial and disability-rights framework fosters choice, control, and community participation. [10, 11]
