Administrative burden for people with disabilities in government benefits is driven by three main types of costs: learning, compliance, and psychological costs, which are worsened by complex rules, fragmented systems, and physical or geographic barriers. [1, 2] 
## Types of Administrative Costs

* Learning Costs: Difficulty in finding, understanding, and navigating program rules, eligibility requirements, and application procedures.
* Compliance Costs: The heavy effort needed to satisfy bureaucratic demands, such as gathering decades of medical records, filling out complex paperwork, and attending recurring interviews or recertifications. [3, 4, 5] 
* Psychological Costs: The stress, stigma, low institutional trust, and feelings of dehumanization experienced when interacting with restrictive welfare or safety-net systems. [3, 6, 7] 

## Key Influencing Factors

* System and Medical Verification Hurdles: Programs like SSDI (Social Security Disability Insurance) and SSI (Supplemental Security Income) require extensive historical medical documentation, low approval rates (~33%), and continuous eligibility reviews. [3, 6] 
* Restrictive Asset and Income Limits: Low asset caps (such as $2,000 for single SSI applicants), frequent income/asset reporting rules, and punitive work cliffs force recipients to constantly track and report minute changes to avoid losing benefits. [3, 6] 
* Service Fragmentation and Delivery Channels: Navigating multiple separate programs (like Medicaid, SNAP, and housing vouchers) without integrated "no wrong door" approaches isolates applicants. Additionally, underfunded agency staffing, long phone wait times, and poorly digitized portals increase the strain. [1, 2, 3, 5, 8] 
* Geographic and Social Disparities: Rural residents face distinct disadvantages due to a lack of nearby local agency field offices, poor broadband access, and reliance on over-the-phone or web-based options that may be inaccessible for certain impairments. [9]
