Police-initiated contact for people with disabilities is driven by a complex interplay of behavioral misperceptions, systemic vulnerabilities, intersectional identities, and structural law enforcement practices. Individuals with disabilities—particularly cognitive, intellectual, or developmental disabilities (I/DD)—are disproportionately represented at the front end of the criminal justice system. [1, 2, 3, 4] 
The primary factors influencing these police-initiated contacts can be organized into four core areas:
## 1. Behavioral Misinterpretation and "Command-and-Control" Culture

* Perceived Non-Compliance: Police training heavily emphasizes strict compliance to commands. Behaviors common to certain disabilities—such as avoiding eye contact (common in autism), slow cognitive processing, or a physical inability to move quickly—are frequently misinterpreted by officers as defiance, deception, or hostility. [3, 5, 6, 7] 
* Signs of Intoxication or Threat: Physical attributes or behaviors associated with cerebral palsy, epilepsy, or diabetes can be mistaken for public intoxication or drug impairment. Similarly, natural coping mechanisms like "stimming" (self-stimulatory behavior) or a high-stress "meltdown" can be misconstrued as erratic, dangerous, or aggressive behavior, prompting a tactical intervention rather than a medical or supportive response. [5, 8, 9, 10] 
* Communication Barriers: A lack of immediate access to alternative communication tools, sign language interpreters, or simple-language commands often causes traffic stops or basic welfare checks to rapidly escalate into involuntary detentions or arrests. [5, 6, 10] 

## 2. High Rates of Victimization

* The Vulnerability Gap: People with disabilities are targeted for violent crimes, sexual assault, and robbery at significantly higher rates than the general population. According to data highlighted by [The Arc](https://thearc.org/our-initiatives/criminal-justice/), individuals with cognitive disabilities face the highest risk. [11, 12, 13] 
* Discredited Reporting: Because police officers frequently lack specialized training in interviewing individuals with speech, memory, or cognitive differences, victims with disabilities may not be viewed as credible witnesses. This can lead to circular interactions where the victim is misunderstood or even detained during the investigation of a crime committed against them. [1, 8, 14] 

## 3. Intersectional Demographics

* The Race and Disability "Double Bind": Race heavily compounded by disability dramatically spikes the likelihood of police contact. Research from organizations like the [District of Columbia Developmental Disabilities Council](https://ddc.dc.gov/) indicates that Black individuals with disabilities are far more likely to face police-initiated stops, arrests, and use of force than their white disabled counterparts. [3, 15, 16] 
* Co-occurring Social Risk Factors: High rates of homelessness, poverty, and lack of accessible community healthcare mean that individuals with psychiatric or developmental disabilities are more visible in heavily policed public spaces, increasing routine surveillance contact. [3, 15, 17] 

## 4. Systemic and Organizational Deficiencies

* 
* Inadequate Law Enforcement Training: Fewer than half of U.S. states mandate comprehensive law enforcement training specifically for intellectual or developmental disabilities. Without specific instruction on identification and de-escalation, frontline officers default to physical control or arrest.
* Lack of Crisis Infrastructure: Many jurisdictions lack robust alternative dispatch programs (such as integrated civilian crisis-care or co-responder models), forcing emergency services dispatchers to send armed law enforcement officers to handle what are fundamentally medical or behavioral health crises. [4, 5, 18, 19, 20]
