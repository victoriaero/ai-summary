Person-centered care (PCC) for White people—and patients in general—is shaped by core interpersonal, communication, and systemic factors that prioritize the individual's unique values, preferences, and lifestyle. [1, 2] 
## Communication and Shared Decision-Making

* Active Listening: Clinicians who actively listen and validate a patient’s personal concerns foster higher satisfaction and collaborative treatment plans.
* Shared Control: Allowing patients to participate actively in medical decisions rather than feeling coerced improves adherence and overall care quality. [3] 
* Clear Information Sharing: Providing transparent, easily understood explanations regarding diagnoses and treatment options helps align expectations between the provider and the patient.

## Provider-Patient Concordance and Attitude

* Clinician Empathy: Patient-centered attitudes (such as empathy, respect, and holistic consideration of biopsychosocial needs) directly improve interpersonal connection and history-taking quality during clinical encounters. [1, 4] 
* Interpersonal Skills: Warmth, respect, and the absence of cold or dismissive treatment from both clinical and non-clinical staff heavily influence how safe and valued a patient feels. [5] 

## Socioeconomic and Systemic Factors

* Insurance and Access: Insurance-based coverage and financial stability dictate the choice of facility, time spent with providers, and out-of-pocket burdens, which can restrict or facilitate comprehensive person-centered delivery. [5, 6] 
* Health Literacy: Individual health and digital literacy levels impact how effectively a person can navigate treatment options, understand health promotion tools, and engage in informed shared decision-making. [7] 
* Preferences and Values: Research indicates that while core desires for respectful and attentive care are universal, specific preferences regarding autonomy, long-term planning, and optimism vary based on individual life context and cultural background. [5, 8]
