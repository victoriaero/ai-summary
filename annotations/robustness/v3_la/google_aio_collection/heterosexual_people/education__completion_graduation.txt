When analyzing educational outcomes by sexual orientation, research shows that educational completion and graduation factors for heterosexual individuals are heavily gendered. Broadly, heterosexual women achieve higher graduation rates than female sexual minorities, while heterosexual men show lower educational attainment than male sexual minorities (such as gay or mostly heterosexual men). [1, 2] 
Beyond sexual orientation itself, graduation for straight individuals is shaped by a mix of gender norms, economic constraints, and academic behaviors:
## 1. Gender Identity and Cultural Norms

* The Masculine Identity Gap: For heterosexual men, adherence to traditional masculine identities can negatively influence academic performance. Research indicates a tendency to reject school compliance, leading to higher rates of behavioral problems in school, which accounts for up to 30% of the college graduation gap between men and women. [3] 
* Field of Study (STEM) Dynamics: Heterosexual men are significantly more likely to persist and graduate with STEM degrees compared to sexual minority men. Conversely, heterosexual women have a lower probability of finishing a STEM degree compared to sexual minority women. [4] 

## 2. Differing Motivations and Perceived Returns

* Perceived Necessity: When heterosexual men choose not to finish or pursue a degree, they frequently cite personal choice, not wanting a degree, or feeling that higher education is not necessary for their intended career path. [5, 6] 
* Market Incentives: Heterosexual women have seen rapidly increasing returns on college degrees in both the labor and marriage markets. This economic incentive heavily drives higher enrollment and completion rates among women. [7] 

## 3. Financial and Economic Pressures

* Affordability: Financial constraints are a major hurdle across all demographics. However, women without degrees are statistically more likely to cite a direct lack of affordability as their primary barrier to completion, whereas men more frequently point back to a lack of interest.
* Workforce Demands: The immediate need to work to support themselves or their families frequently derails college completion for both straight men and women. [5, 6, 8] 

## 4. Early Academic and Behavioral Factors
Regardless of orientation, baseline academic factors heavily dictate ultimate graduation success:

* Higher high school GPAs and standardized test scores remain the strongest predictors of finishing a degree.
* Challenges such as a history of grade repetition, behavioral health disorders, or delinquency heavily decrease the likelihood of graduation. [4, 9]
