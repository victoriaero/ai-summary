Person-centered care (PCC) treats patients as equal, individual partners in collaborative decision-making rather than passive recipients of medical services. For individuals without disabilities, the implementation and quality of PCC depend on several key patient, clinician, and system-level factors. [1] 
## 1. Patient-Specific Factors

* Health Literacy and Numeracy: Patients with higher health literacy and numerical comprehension can better digest medical data, evaluate treatment risks, and engage in shared decision-making (SDM). [2, 3] 
* Demographics and Background: Age, gender, educational level, and socio-economic background shape individual preferences, expectations, and active participation in care plans. [3] 
* Behavioral and Cognitive Influences: Factors such as personal motivation, health goals, time perspective (how people connect with their future self), and emotional states (such as anxiety or stress affecting empathy gaps) dictate how patients navigate routines and treatment options. [2] 
* Multi-Morbidity Status: Managing multiple chronic conditions shifts the focus toward comprehensive, goal-concordant care planning that requires high patient self-management support. [4, 5] 

## 2. Clinician-Specific Factors

* Communication and Empathy Skills: A clinician's ability to actively listen, build trust, show empathy, and tailor language directly drives the success of person-centered interactions. [3, 6] 
* Training and Knowledge: Formal education and training in patient-centered philosophies, collaborative communication, and shared decision-making tools help overcome paternalistic habits. [1, 3, 7] 
* Attitudes and Biases: Clinician skepticism, rigid biomedical mindsets, or stereotypical assumptions about patient compliance can hinder true partnership. [2, 7] 

## 3. Health System and Organizational Factors

* Work Environment and Staffing: Supportive nursing and medical work environments, adequate staff-to-patient ratios, and strong team dynamics foster higher levels of person-centered care.
* Time Pressures and Workflow: Rigid time constraints, high work pressure, and complex, fragmented documentation systems restrict the time clinicians can spend on individual goal-setting.
* Leadership and Culture: Strong organizational leadership that explicitly values a person-first culture, interdisciplinary teamwork, and continuous quality improvement sets the tone for daily practice.
* Access and Digital Infrastructure: Continuity of care with a trusted provider, privacy during visits, easy service access, and interoperable digital health tools (such as online medical records and decision aids) empower patients to track and participate in their care.
* Payment and Policy Models: Aligned payment models and healthcare policies that reward health outcomes and coordinated care rather than high-volume task completion remove structural barriers to PCC. [1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11] 

You can learn more about core operational concepts through the [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Person-Centered Care Guide](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/innovation/key-concepts/person-centered-care). [5]
