In the criminal justice system, police-initiated contact (such as traffic stops, street stops, and proactive questioning) for people without disabilities is primarily shaped by a combination of legal, behavioral, demographic, and environmental factors. [1, 2] 
Criminological frameworks—specifically the "focal concerns" of policing (blameworthiness, community protection, and practical constraints)—outline the specific variables that influence why an officer decides to proactively initiate contact with this population: [2] 
## 1. Situational and Legal Factors
The most direct drivers of police-initiated contact are legally actionable situations or behaviors observed by an officer. [2] 

* Offense Seriousness: Proactive stops are highly driven by perceived blameworthiness. Officers are legally obligated and highly motivated to intervene if they witness criminal behavior, traffic violations, or suspicious activity directly. [2] 
* Suspect Demeanor and Compliance: During initial observations, the demeanor of an individual significantly impacts an officer's decision to formalize contact. Displaying hostility, evasiveness, or flight behaviors acts as a behavioral cue that prompts an officer to initiate a stop or conduct a field interview. [2, 3] 
* Substance Use: Observable indicators of drug or alcohol consumption in public spaces frequently prompt police-initiated contact under the mandate of community safety or public order enforcement. [2, 4] 

## 2. Extralegal and Demographic Characteristics
When disability is not a factor, other social and demographic indicators significantly influence the statistical likelihood of experiencing a police-initiated stop. [5] 

* Race and Ethnicity: According to data from the [Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS)](https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/contacts-between-police-and-public-2022) and criminological research, Black and Hispanic individuals without disabilities face significantly higher rates of police-initiated contact (such as street and traffic stops) compared to white individuals. [6, 7, 8] 
* Age and Gender: Criminological data demonstrates that young males (particularly those aged 18 to 24) experience the highest levels of police-initiated contact. Younger age groups and males are statistically more likely to be targeted for proactive enforcement and street monitoring. [9, 10] 

## 3. Environmental and Neighborhood Context
The physical and economic context of an area heavily influences how police distribute proactive monitoring. [11] 

* Neighborhood Socioeconomic Status: Proactive, police-initiated stops are heavily concentrated in low-income, low-stability, or socially disadvantaged neighborhoods. In contrast, police contact in higher-income neighborhoods is more frequently dispatch-initiated (called in by residents) rather than officer-initiated. [4, 11] 
* Targeted and Hot-Spot Policing: Police departments allocate resources based on crime statistics. Individuals walking or driving through designated "high-crime areas" or "hot spots" are subject to heightened systemic surveillance, vastly increasing the likelihood of an officer initiating contact for minor infractions or general questioning. [8, 12] 

## 4. Organizational and Policy Constraints
The operational rules of a specific police department dictate individual officer discretion. [2, 3] 

* Departmental Mandates: High-discretion tactics, quotas, or strict enforcement mandates regarding specific violations (e.g., zero-tolerance policies or aggressive traffic enforcement campaigns) remove individual discretion and dictate when officers must actively initiate stops. [2]
