Standard credit-scoring models—such as FICO® and VantageScore®—do not use race, ethnicity, age, income, or location as direct inputs. For non-Latino individuals (including White and Asian American consumers), credit scores are calculated using the exact same core financial behavior metrics applied to all consumers. [1, 2] 
## The Core Factors That Influence Credit Scores
Major credit scoring algorithms evaluate your credit reports based on these primary elements: [1] 

* Payment History (35% for FICO): Whether you pay your credit cards, installment loans, and mortgages on time. Missed or late payments lower your score. [1] 
* Amounts Owed / Credit Utilization (30% for FICO): How much total revolving debt you carry relative to your total available credit limits (keeping utilization below 30%—and ideally under 10%—is preferred). [1] 
* Length of Credit History (15% for FICO): The age of your oldest account, the age of your newest account, and the average age of all your accounts. A longer track record helps. [1] 
* Credit Mix (10% for FICO): The variety of credit accounts you manage, such as credit cards, retail accounts, installment loans, and mortgages. [1] 
* New Credit (10% for FICO): The number of recent credit applications and newly opened accounts. Too many hard inquiries in a short period can temporarily dip your score. [1] 

## What Is Excluded vs. What Indirectly Affects Scores

* Explicitly Excluded: Official scoring models legally and algorithmically omit race, ethnicity, marital status, gender, religion, national origin, and annual salary. [2] 
* Indirectly Related Economic Factors: While race and ethnicity are not in the algorithm, broader financial conditions—such as generational wealth, access to traditional banking, homeownership status (mortgages build positive credit history while rent traditionally has not), and localized economic resources—frequently correlate with higher average scores for certain demographic groups (with national medians showing White and Asian American consumers averaging higher scores than Black and Hispanic consumers in conventional models). [2, 3, 4] 

You can check your credit reports and scores for free through major financial institutions, credit card apps, or directly via national credit bureaus like Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion. [1]
