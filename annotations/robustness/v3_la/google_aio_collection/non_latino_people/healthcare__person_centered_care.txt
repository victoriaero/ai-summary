Person-centered care (PCC) for non-Latino populations—such as Black, White, and other non-Latino racial or ethnic groups—is shaped by a combination of patient-level, clinician-level, and systemic factors. [1, 2] 
## Patient-Level Factors

* Racial and Ethnic Background: Distinct values, unmet needs, and preferences vary across groups. For example, studies in oncology note that finding meaning in illness is prominent among Black patients, while optimism is more frequently reported by White patients. [2] 
* Health and Digital Literacy: Low health or digital literacy restricts a patient's ability to navigate medical systems, understand complex treatment plans, and engage in digital health portals. [1] 
* Socioeconomic Status: Factors like income, employment, and housing stability heavily influence a patient's capacity to follow medical advice and manage chronic conditions. [3] 

## Clinician-Level Factors

* Cultural Competence and Humility: Providers who lack awareness of diverse cultural beliefs, spiritual practices, or communal family structures may misunderstand a patient's health behaviors. [1, 4, 5] 
* Communication and Shared Decision-Making: Deficits in active listening or poor shared decision-making can make patients feel coerced or unheard. For instance, Black patients frequently report unmet needs regarding provider interactions and treatment decisions. [1, 2, 3] 
* Unconscious Biases: Implicit biases among healthcare professionals can negatively affect the quality of time, empathy, and treatment options offered during clinical encounters. [1] 

## Systemic and Structural Factors

* Trust and Representation: A lack of workforce diversity and minority representation among caregivers can impede trust in dominant healthcare systems. [2, 3] 
* Social Determinants of Health (SDOH): Unequal access to reliable transportation, stable housing, and local clinics impacts overall healthcare utilization and health outcomes. [3, 6]
