Person-centered care (PCC) for Black people in healthcare is heavily influenced by a combination of clinician-level communication behaviors, systemic barriers, and cultural dynamics. [1, 2] 
## Clinician-Level and Interpersonal Factors

* Unconscious Bias and Stereotyping: Implicit biases and racial stereotyping by providers often lead to dismissiveness, lower quality listening, or misattributed behaviors (such as assuming drug-seeking). [3, 4] 
* Active Listening and Cultural Sensitivity: Providers who practice active listening and cultural sensitivity significantly improve trust and interpersonal engagement, whereas cold or dismissive treatment deepens mistrust. [1, 2, 4] 
* Provider Representation (Race Concordance): Having racially concordant providers—such as Black physicians—helps build safety, fosters open communication, and facilitates positive patient-centered encounters. [2, 3] 
* Shared Decision-Making: Patient empowerment and involvement in medical decisions are frequently hindered when clinicians coerce patients or fail to share culturally relevant information. [1, 2] 

## Systemic and Structural Factors

* Socioeconomic Disparities and Insurance: Income inequalities, higher poverty rates, and a lack of health insurance coverage restrict continuous access to quality care and new medical therapies. [5, 6] 
* Geographic and Physical Barriers: Living in underserved areas with a limited supply of nearby medical facilities or doctor's offices limits timely healthcare utilization. [5] 
* Health Literacy: Health educational materials written at high reading levels or a lack of accessible information create functional hurdles to navigating medical treatment. [5] 
* Clinical Algorithms and AI: Digital health tools and medical algorithms that incorporate historical racial biases can perpetuate unequal treatment recommendations. [6] 

## Cultural and Social Factors

* Interpersonal and Historical Mistrust: Past and present systemic inequities foster medical mistrust, which directly affects treatment adherence and patient-provider communication. [7, 8] 
* Community and Gender Norms: Social influences—such as masculine gender norms encouraging stoicism or reliance on family networks—shape how Black individuals (particularly Black men) approach and seek formal medical care. [7, 9]
