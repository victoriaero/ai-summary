In criminal justice research, police-initiated contact for cisgender individuals—such as traffic stops, pedestrian stops, and proactive street enforcement—is primarily driven by an intersection of demographic traits (gender, race, and age), neighborhood socioeconomics, and prior legal system involvement.
Criminological data reveals that while cisgender identity functions as a normative baseline in traditional law enforcement metrics, the likelihood of a police officer initiating an encounter is heavily influenced by the following key factors:
## 1. Gender and Sex Disparities

* Cisgender Men: Across nearly all statistical metrics, cisgender men experience a higher volume of police-initiated contact than cisgender women. According to the [Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS)](https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/contacts-between-police-and-public-2022), males consistently encounter more proactive stops, searches, and uses of force than females. [1, 2, 3] 
* Cisgender Women: Though stopped less frequently overall, cisgender women still account for a substantial percentage of police-initiated contacts—often driven by traffic violations. However, reports by organizations like the [Prison Policy Initiative](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/blog/2019/05/14/policingwomen/) highlight that police use of force against women has historically increased over recent decades. [4, 5] 

## 2. Race and Intersectionality
Race acts as one of the most prominent predictors of police-initiated contact, deeply compounding gender dynamics: [6] 

* Black Cisgender Men: This demographic experiences disproportionately higher rates of intrusive street stops, vehicle searches, and instances of misconduct or force compared to White cisgender men. [1, 7] 
* Black Cisgender Women: Research highlights severe disparities even within female demographics. Black cisgender women are significantly more likely to face traffic stops and are arrested at triple the rate of White cisgender women during those stops. [4] 

## 3. Neighborhood Context and Socioeconomic Status

* Over-Policed Communities: Law enforcement agencies frequently deploy proactive policing strategies in low-income neighborhoods or areas labeled as "high-crime". Consequently, cisgender individuals living in or traveling through these areas face heightened surveillance regardless of their individual behavior. [8, 9, 10] 
* Income Levels: Lower individual or household income significantly correlates with higher rates of involuntary street stops and harsher enforcement outcomes, particularly for Black and minoritized residents. [11, 12] 

## 4. Age and Developmental Factors

* Youth Concentration: Age plays a decisive role in proactive stops. Young adults between the ages of 18 and 24 experience the highest rates of police-initiated contact of any age bracket. [1, 3, 13] 

## 5. Prior Criminal Justice Exposure

* Systemic Snowball Effect: Prior involuntary contact with the legal system acts as a strong predictor for future stops. Criminological studies tracked by the [National Institutes of Health (NIH)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3868476/) indicate that factors such as having a parent or sibling with an arrest history, or personal histories of school disciplinary actions, significantly increase the risk of an adolescent's initial police contact. [14, 15]
