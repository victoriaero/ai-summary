Administrative burden for transgender people accessing government benefits is driven by learning, compliance, and psychological costs tied to identity documentation, system fragmentation, and systemic discrimination. [1, 2, 3] 
## Key Factors Influencing Administrative Burden

* Outdated Identity Documents (ID): Presenting IDs with mismatched names, photos, or gender markers can out transgender applicants, exposing them to harassment, privacy violations, or service denial during routine benefit checks. [2] 
* Intrusive Amendment Processes: Many state and federal agencies require burdensome verification steps—such as court orders or proof of specific medical procedures—to update gender markers on birth certificates or driver’s licenses. [4] 
* Psychological Costs and Stigma: Fear of mistreatment, judgment, or skepticism from caseworkers creates high psychological barriers, including stress, anxiety, and a loss of autonomy that deters enrollment. [5] 
* Policy Fragmentation and Contradictions: Contradictory sex classifications and varying eligibility rules across local, state, and federal agencies increase learning and compliance costs, making programs difficult to navigate. [6, 7] 
* Intersectional Vulnerabilities: Unhoused transgender individuals, people with criminal legal histories, or people of color often face compounded structural barriers when trying to secure basic housing or healthcare safety-net benefits. [2, 8]
