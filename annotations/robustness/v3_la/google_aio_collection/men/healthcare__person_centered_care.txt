Person-centered care (PCC) for men in healthcare is shaped by a combination of psychological, social, cultural, and systemic factors that dictate how men perceive, seek, and experience medical treatment. [1] 
## Masculinity Norms and Health Beliefs

* Traditional masculinity: Adherence to traditional masculine norms—such as self-reliance, emotional stoicism, and fear of appearing vulnerable—frequently prevents men from seeking timely medical care or openly discussing symptoms.
* Stigma around mental health: Men often experience heightened social stigma when addressing mental health or chronic pain, requiring clinicians to create non-judgmental spaces that validate their experiences without reinforcing shame.

## Communication and Provider Dynamics

* Direct and goal-oriented communication: Men often respond more positively to pragmatic, straightforward communication styles from providers that focus on problem-solving, actionable health goals, and collaborative shared decision-making.
* Provider gender preferences: Depending on the nature of the consultation (such as routine exams versus intimate or psychological care), some men express a preference for male or female providers, which can influence their level of trust and comfort during physical assessments. [2] 

## Health Literacy and Education

* Targeted health information: Access to clear, practical health data and decision aids empowers men to take an active role in managing preference-sensitive conditions (such as cardiovascular health or prostate screenings). [3, 4] 
* Navigating the healthcare system: Lower baseline health literacy or lack of early guidance on preventative health maintenance can impede a man's capacity to engage as an equal partner in his care plan. [3, 5] 

## Systemic and Organizational Barriers

* Time constraints: Brief clinical encounter times and fast-paced primary care settings restrict the opportunity to build the deep rapport and trust necessary to uncover unstated health concerns in reluctant patients. [1, 6, 7] 
* Flexible service design: Traditional clinic hours that overlap with standard work schedules act as a structural deterrent, whereas flexible appointment scheduling, telehealth options, and community-based outreach significantly improve male engagement. [4]
