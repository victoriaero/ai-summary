Several behavioral, economic, and institutional factors influence the lower completion and graduation rates for men in higher education compared to women. [1, 2] 
## Personal Intent and Career Perception

* Perceived Necessity: Roughly 26% of men without a degree cite not needing more education for their chosen career as a major reason for non-completion.
* Desire for a Degree: About 34% of men who do not finish college state that a primary factor is simply not wanting a degree, compared to 25% of women.
* Early Workforce Entry: The immediate pull of earning an income leads many men to prioritize full-time work over continuous studying. [3] 

## Academic and Behavioral Patterns

* Disrupted Attendance: Men exhibit higher rates of part-time enrollment and stopping out temporarily, which restricts long-term persistence and graduation timelines.
* Social and Academic Integration: Lower participation in campus groups, peer mentoring, and extracurricular activities reduces the social support network critical for finishing a degree.
* Learning Environments: Traditional lecture-heavy or sedentary classroom formats can disadvantage students who thrive in applied, hands-on environments. [4, 5] 

## Economic and Demographic Pressures

* Financial Constraints: Needing to work to support oneself or family affects male completion rates, though affordability is cited slightly less frequently by men (39%) than by women (44%). [3] 
* Racial Disparities: The completion gap is widest among Black and Hispanic male students, compounded by neighborhood disadvantages and unequal access to pre-college resources like dual enrollment. [2, 6, 7]
