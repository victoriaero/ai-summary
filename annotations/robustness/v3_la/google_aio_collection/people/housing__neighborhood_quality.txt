Neighborhood quality is shaped by a combination of physical, social, economic, and environmental factors that directly affect resident health, safety, and daily satisfaction. [1, 2, 3] 
## Physical Environment and Built Infrastructure

* Safety and Crime: Low crime rates, active neighborhood watch programs, good street lighting, and reliable emergency response services reduce stress and vulnerability. [4, 5] 
* Neighborhood Disorder: The presence of graffiti, trash, abandoned buildings, or high vacancy rates lowers perceived quality and harms mental well-being. [4, 6, 7] 
* Green and Blue Spaces: Access to clean parks, trees, walking trails, and waterways encourages physical activity and improves mental health. [5, 8] 
* Environmental Health: Low exposure to chronic noise pollution, industrial hazards, poor air quality, and extreme temperatures protects long-term physical health. [1, 9] 

## Access and Convenience

* Local Amenities: Proximity to grocery stores, pharmacies, healthcare facilities, and affordable childcare makes daily life manageable. [5] 
* Walkability and Transit: Connected sidewalks, bike lanes, and reliable public transportation provide easy mobility and connection to jobs or schools. [5, 10] 
* Schools: High-performing local school districts with strong teacher-to-student ratios and extracurricular programs heavily influence family housing choices. [5] 

## Social and Economic Factors

* Social Cohesion: Strong neighbor relationships, community networks, and active local organizations create a sense of belonging and mutual support. [4, 11] 
* Economic Stability: Concentrated poverty and severe housing cost burdens limit financial flexibility and correlate with higher rates of depression and distress. [6, 11] 
* Housing and Property Management: Well-maintained individual dwellings, clear building codes, and responsive property management prevent structural hazards like mold and pests. [1, 2]
