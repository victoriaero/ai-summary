Several interconnected structural, cognitive, and environmental factors influence patient safety for people with disabilities in healthcare.
Communication Barriers
• Ineffective information exchange: Misunderstandings happen when clinicians do not tailor communication to individual cognitive or sensory needs.
• Sensory and cognitive gaps: Unaddressed dual sensory loss or intellectual disabilities compromise informed consent and accurate symptom reporting.
• Unreliable tracking systems: Electronic health records often lack consistent "alert flags" or accessible "care passports" outlining a patient's specific communication methods.
Provider Biases and Knowledge Gaps
• Diagnostic overshadowing: Clinicians frequently misattribute new, unrelated medical symptoms entirely to a patient's pre-existing disability.
• Assumptions about quality of life: Inappropriate non-treatment or do-not-resuscitate (DNAR) decisions are sometimes made based on biased societal assumptions.
• Lack of disability training: Providers often lack formal training in disability competence, leading to avoidance, delayed investigations, and omissions of basic care.
Physical and Environmental Obstacles
• Inaccessible facilities: Fixed-height examination rooms, heavy doors, narrow hallways, and missing lifts prevent safe positioning and transfers.
• Inappropriate medical equipment: Lack of accessible scales, height-adjustable tables, or specialized diagnostic tools prevents standard preventive screenings.
• Systemic coordination gaps: Preventive services requiring separate appointments or complex cross-facility coordination show major drop-offs in utilization.
Systemic and Monitoring Failures
• Under-reporting of harm: Incident reporting systems and formal complaints poorly capture omissions of care or hospital-acquired issues (like pressure sores) due to fear of repercussions or lack of tracking.
• Exclusion from design: Healthcare systems rarely integrate multidisciplinary teams or caregiver perspectives proactively into safety protocols.
