Multiple individual, organizational, and cultural factors influence workplace harassment and discrimination against people with disabilities.
Attitudinal and Cultural Factors
• Stigma and Stereotypes: Negative perceptions from coworkers and supervisors—such as the assumption that disabled employees are less productive, incompetent, or a financial burden—foster hostile attitudes.
• Inappropriate "Banter" and Microaggressions: Everyday offensive jokes, intrusive questioning about private medical histories, or mockery regarding a worker's physical or mental impairment often normalize abusive environments.
• Toxic Workplace Culture: Organizations that fail to enforce zero-tolerance policies or neglect subtle bullying (like exclusion from meetings or social events) allow harassment to escalate.
Structural and Operational Factors
• Lack of Knowledge and Training: Employers and managers lacking education regarding disabilities often mishandle accommodation requests or fail to recognize indirect harassment.
• Accommodation Resistance and Sabotage: Viewing reasonable accommodations as an "undue hardship" or actively pushing back against workflow adjustments can lead to coworker resentment and retaliatory hostility.
• Forced Disclosure: Pressuring employees to publicly reveal private medical conditions before they are comfortable doing so compromises privacy and invites discrimination.
Contextual and Legal Factors
• Type of Organization: Smaller organizations may lack dedicated Human Resources infrastructure to properly mediate complaints, whereas larger organizations may have formal compliance structures.
• Nature of the Disability: Visible physical disabilities, neurodivergence, and invisible or mental health conditions (such as depression or anxiety) face distinct societal biases and varying levels of workplace understanding.
• Fear of Retaliation: Employees often hesitate to report harassment due to fears of demotion, reduced hours, or wrongful termination, which perpetuates cycles of abuse.
