Socioeconomic status, school funding distribution, and academic preparation are primary factors influencing educational opportunity and access for non-Latino groups (such as White, Black, and Asian students) in the United States. [1, 2] 
## Socioeconomic Status and Family Income

* Household Wealth: Family income directly impacts access to high-quality preschool, tutoring, and college savings.
* Poverty Concentration: Students from low-income families—regardless of race—frequently attend schools with fewer resources, impacting overall educational attainment.
* College Affordability: Financial constraints and the need to work during school heavily influence whether non-Latino students enroll in four-year universities or community colleges. [1, 3, 4, 5] 

## School Quality and Resources

* Access to Educators: Disparities in funding mean some non-Latino students experience lower access to fully certified, experienced, and high-quality teachers.
* Advanced Coursework: Availability of Advanced Placement (AP) classes, gifted programs, and advanced math and science courses vary widely by district, directly shaping college readiness.
* School Climate: Positive school environments, safety, and up-to-date facilities support higher retention and graduation rates. [1, 2, 5, 6] 

## Academic Preparation and Tracking

* Early Achievement: Gaps in foundational reading and math by the end of elementary school influence tracking into higher-level secondary courses.
* Standardized Testing and Counseling: Access to SAT/ACT preparation courses and college-counseling resources strongly predicts matriculation into four-year degree programs. [2, 5]
