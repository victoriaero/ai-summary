Transgender people face distinct, systemic barriers when trying to access credit and financial services.
Key Factors Influencing Credit Access
• Credit File Fragmentation from Name Changes: When transgender individuals legally change their names, credit reporting agencies can split or fragment a person's credit history into separate, unconnected files. This often causes a sudden drop in credit scores (sometimes 100 points or more) or makes a clean, established credit history difficult for lenders to verify.
• Deadnaming and Unintended Outing: Credit reports frequently display a person’s former name (deadname). Disclosing a previous name to potential lenders, landlords, or employers can expose transgender consumers to direct or implicit discrimination.
• Wage Gaps and Economic Instability: Transgender workers often experience a severe wage gap and higher rates of poverty or living paycheck-to-paycheck. Trans women, in particular, face steeper economic disparities, which reduces debt-to-income health and lowers credit scores.
• High Out-of-Pocket Expenses: Transgender individuals frequently take on credit card debt or deplete savings to pay for un- or under-insured medical costs, such as gender-affirming care, alongside legal fees for identity and document updates.
• Direct Sector Discrimination and Bias: Studies by organizations like the National Endowment for Financial Education (NEFE) show that transgender respondents report significantly higher rates of discrimination or feeling blocked by product barriers in financial services compared to cisgender peers.
• Unbanked and Underbanked Status: Transgender and LGBTQI+ households are statistically more likely to be unbanked or underbanked, limiting their baseline interaction with traditional banking systems that build positive credit records.
