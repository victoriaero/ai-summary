The factors influencing harassment and bullying for transgender people in education operate across a socioecological spectrum, spanning institutional policies, interpersonal relationships, and physical school environments. Research indicates that transgender and non-binary students face significantly higher rates of victimization compared to their cisgender peers.
These primary drivers and mitigating influences shape their safety and well-being in educational environments:
1. Institutional Policies & Administrative Actions
• Presence of Anti-Bullying Policies: Schools with explicit, enumerated anti-LGBTQ+ bullying policies see a noticeable decline in harassment rates. A report by The Trevor Project showed that LGBTQ+ youth in schools with protective policies reported the lowest rates of in-person bullying.
• Administrative Leadership: School administrators heavily dictate whether transphobia is enabled or dismantled. Proactive collaboration with students and protective policy design reduces risk, whereas direct resistance or catering to hostile external stakeholders increases the vulnerability of trans students.
• State and Regional Laws: Broader systemic legal environments play a massive role. Societal or state-level rollbacks on protections and censorship of inclusive education directly correspond with more hostile climates and higher rates of abuse.
2. Gender-Affirming Practices & Curricula
• Name and Pronoun Recognition: Forcing transgender individuals to navigate environments where staff or peers refuse to use their correct names and pronouns (misgendering/deadnaming) acts as a recurring form of institutional harassment.
• Inclusive Curricula: Integrating LGBTQ+ topics into health and standard academic curricula fosters an empathetic school environment. Research shows that inclusive material directly correlates with decreased bias-based and generalized harassment.
3. Physical Infrastructure & Facilities
• Access to Bathrooms and Locker Rooms: Gender-segregated spaces are frequently highlighted as the most physically unsafe locations for transgender students. Being denied access to facilities matching their gender identity—or lacking access to gender-neutral restrooms—sharply increases their exposure to targeted social isolation, exclusion, and physical violence.
4. Staff Awareness and Training
• The "Awareness Gap": A stark disconnect often exists between student experiences and staff perception. Studies have shown that while the vast majority of LGBTQ+ students witness or experience verbal and social bullying, a high percentage of school health professionals and staff report being entirely unaware of it.
• Staff Intervention Skills: When educators lack the specialized training to model respectful behavior or effectively intervene during bullying incidents, students underreport harassment out of a belief that nothing will be done to stop it.
5. Interpersonal and Structural Support Systems
• Peer and Family Ecosystems: Social support from peers and a strong sense of school connectedness act as massive protective buffers against the psychological distress of bullying. Conversely, reinforced prejudice from peers' parents or family rejection compounds the minority stress experienced by the student.
