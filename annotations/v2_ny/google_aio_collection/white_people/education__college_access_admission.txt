AI OVERVIEW TEXT
============================================================

Several key socioeconomic, structural, and institutional factors influence college access and admission for White students in higher education.
Socioeconomic Status and Family Income
• Family Wealth: Higher average family income and accumulated generational wealth among many White households provide greater access to private tutoring, test preparation courses, and well-resourced K-12 school districts.
• College Choice and Selectivity: Family income heavily shapes White students' selection processes and their ability to attend expensive or highly selective institutions.
• Economic Shifts: Declines in overall White undergraduate enrollment in recent years are partly linked to rising college costs, growing availability of jobs that do not require degrees, and shifting perceptions of higher education value.
Academic Metrics and Institutional Policies
• Academic Performance: Admissions decisions heavily prioritize traditional metrics like high school grade point averages (GPA) and standardized test scores, where students from higher-income backgrounds often hold statistical advantages.
• Legacy and Donor Preferences: White applicants, particularly non-first-generation students, benefit disproportionately from institutional practices such as legacy admissions (favoring children of alumni) due to historical enrollment demographics.
• Information and Social Capital: White high school seniors frequently report relying heavily on family networks and personal connections for guidance through the college application process, a form of social capital tied to parental college completion.
Enrollment and Application Dynamics
• Application Rates vs. Enrollment: Data from institutional studies show that while White application shares have declined, White students continue to represent a majority share of enrolled students at many surveyed universities, frequently exceeding their overall application rates.
• Campus Engagement: Studies indicate that White students participate in campus visits and utilize localized college prep resources at higher rates than several underrepresented minority groups, influencing application follow-through.
