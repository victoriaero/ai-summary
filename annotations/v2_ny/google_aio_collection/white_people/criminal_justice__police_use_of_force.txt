Police use of force involving White individuals in the criminal justice system is influenced by several situational, environmental, and institutional factors:
• Encounter Characteristics and Behavior: The primary predictor of police use of force across all racial groups is the behavior of the individual during an interaction. This includes whether a person is actively resisting arrest, assaulting an officer, attempting to flee, or possessing a weapon.
• Neighborhood Crime Rates and Structural Conditions: Force incidents frequently correlate with local crime levels, concentrated disadvantage, and neighborhood violence rather than race alone. High-crime areas experience a higher volume of police-civilian contacts, which statistically increases the overall frequency of use-of-force events.
• Type of Call for Service: Officers responding to violent crimes, domestic disturbances, or armed-suspect calls face higher-stress, volatile environments where use of force is more likely to occur regardless of the subject's demographics.
• Departmental Policies and Training: The presence or absence of clear de-escalation guidelines, use-of-force reporting standards, and early-warning systems shape how officers exercise discretion. Agencies with strict, standardized tracking and comprehensive review processes tend to limit unnecessary force.
• Geographical and Contextual Disparities: The rate of police force varies significantly by region, urban-versus-suburban setting, and local policing culture, reflecting how community density and local ordinance enforcement affect police-citizen contact.
According to research from the National Institute of Justice, situational dynamics—such as encounter quality, resistance levels, and local crime rates—account for a large portion of the variance in how and when police apply force.
