Hiring for people without disabilities is driven primarily by traditional qualifications, human capital factors, and structural workforce norms, contrasting sharply with the attitudinal and physical barriers often faced by disabled job seekers.
Key Factors Influencing Employment for People Without Disabilities
• Education and Training: Higher levels of academic attainment, specialized degrees, and technical certificates serve as primary gateways to employment. Research indicates that a lack of training—rather than disability status itself—is a major bottleneck for entry-level positions.
• Standardized Assessment and Recruitment: Standard application tracks, automated resume-screening software, and traditional in-person or video interviews are designed around conventional neurotypical and able-bodied baselines, making them frictionless for applicants without disabilities.
• Perceived Productivity and Cost Expectations: Employers frequently operate on implicit assumptions that workers without disabilities require zero workplace modifications, special equipment, or schedule flexibilities, reducing perceived onboarding and accommodation friction.
• Social Networks and Cultural Fit: Soft skills, informal small talk, eye contact, and seamless integration into existing workplace culture are heavily prioritized by hiring managers during interviews, favoring candidates who naturally align with unexamined social norms.
• Physical and Digital Accessibility: Worksite layouts, standard transportation options, and digital tools (such as web platforms compliant with general standards) present no navigational or technological hurdles for non-disabled workers.
• Absence of Stigma and Bias: Unlike job seekers with disabilities—who face documented biases, lower expression-of-interest rates in resume experiments, and discriminatory assumptions—applicants without disabilities do not encounter prejudice tied to health or functional status.
