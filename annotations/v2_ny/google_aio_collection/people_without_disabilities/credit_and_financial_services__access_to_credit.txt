Access to credit and financial services for people with disabilities (and general credit access factors) is influenced by several interconnected economic, systemic, and social factors:
• Credit Histories and Scores: Lower credit scores and poor credit histories—often driven by lower employment rates, higher medical expenses, or interrupted income—are primary barriers that lead to loan denials or a reliance on alternative financial services.
• Income and Employment Status: Working-age adults with disabilities experience higher rates of poverty and underemployment, which directly restricts their debt-to-income capacity and perceived creditworthiness.
• Reliance on Alternative Financial Services (AFSs): Because traditional banks may deny or discourage standard credit, many individuals turn to high-cost alternative options like payday loans, pawn shops, or title loans, which can trap borrowers in cycles of debt.
• Lender Misperceptions and Stigma: Misunderstandings, a lack of basic disability etiquette, and subconscious discrimination by loan officers can complicate the application process or discourage people from disclosing their disability.
• Inaccessible Infrastructure and Technology: Digital banking apps, websites lacking screen readers or accessibility tools, and physical barriers at brick-and-mortar branches (like non-accessible ATMs) create friction in managing accounts and applying for credit.
• Legal and Policy Protections Gaps: Unlike certain protected classes under housing or employment laws, federal credit frameworks like the Equal Credit Opportunity Act do not explicitly single out disability as a standalone protected category in every context, though general anti-discrimination rules apply.
• Financial Literacy and Education: Access to specialized financial education tailored to disability income streams (such as SSI or SSDI) plays a large role in helping individuals navigate credit-building tools like secured credit cards or credit-builder loans.
