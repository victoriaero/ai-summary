Criminal justice research indicates that police use of force against individuals without reported disabilities is driven primarily by suspect behavior and situational dynamics, alongside secondary demographic and organizational factors.
Primary Behavioral and Situational Factors
• Suspect Resistance: This is the single strongest and most consistent predictor of police use of force across empirical studies. Aggressive or physical resistance substantially increases the likelihood and severity of force used by officers to gain control.
• Weapon Possession or Display: When a suspect is armed, or appears to be reaching for a weapon, officers are significantly more likely to escalate to higher levels of force, including less-lethal options or firearms.
• Attempting to Flee: Active evasion or running from a lawful stop or arrest frequently triggers physical pursuit and tactical interventions.
• Chemical or Substance Impairment: Being under the influence of alcohol or illicit drugs is a frequent situational correlate that can alter a person’s behavior, reduce compliance, and increase the likelihood of physical struggle.
• Type of Encounter / Context: High-stress dispatches—such as crimes in progress, volatile domestic disputes, or encounters occurring late at night (typically between 8:00 PM and 2:00 AM)—elevate the baseline risk of force.
Demographic Factors
• Suspect Sex and Age: Young adult males (most heavily concentrated between ages 20 and 35) experience police use of force at higher rates than older adults or females, largely reflecting statistical exposure to arrest and street-stop dynamics.
• Race and Ethnicity: Studies consistently show that Black and Hispanic/Latino citizens experience non-lethal police force at higher rates than White citizens, even when controlling for certain situational variables, though findings vary depending on the severity of the force and the specific jurisdiction analyzed.
Officer and Organizational Factors
• Officer Experience and Stress: Individual officer characteristics—such as years on the force, cumulative occupational fatigue, or high-stress physiological responses (like tunnel vision during close encounters)—shape split-second decision-making.
• Departmental Culture and Policies: Agency guidelines, the availability of less-lethal weapons, strict supervisory reporting requirements, and the presence of de-escalation or simulation training influence how frequently and severely officers apply force.
