Police arrest and detention decisions are primarily influenced by legal thresholds like probable cause and reasonable suspicion, alongside situational, officer-level, and neighborhood factors. [1, 2] 
## Legal and Situational Factors

* Probable Cause vs. Reasonable Suspicion: According to the [Office of Justice Programs](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/investigative-detention-constitutional-constraints-police-use-force), investigative detentions require reasonable suspicion, while full arrests require probable cause that a crime was committed. [2] 
* Crime Severity: Serious offenses and felonies sharply increase arrest likelihood because officers have less discretion when handling major harm or victims pressing charges. [3, 4] 
* Suspect Demeanor and Threat: Active resistance, flight attempts, and immediate threats to officer or public safety heavily drive the escalation from a basic stop to formal detention or physical force. [5] 

## Officer and Neighborhood Characteristics

* Officer Discretion: Research highlighted by the [Bureau of Justice Assistance](https://bja.ojp.gov/library/publications/understanding-police-decisions-arrest-impact-situational-officer-and) shows that incident-level conditions account for most variations in arrests, but officer characteristics—such as education level, gender, and individual arrest propensity—also play a measurable statistical role. [1, 6, 7] 
* Neighborhood Context: Studies demonstrate that arrest rates can vary depending on neighborhood demographics and the nature of the precinct, even when controlling for specific situational variables. Data compiled by the [Prison Policy Initiative](https://www.prisonpolicy.org/blog/2020/06/05/policingfacts/) further notes that enforcement actions disproportionately affect marginalized groups, including Black and Latinx individuals, the poor, and people with mental health or substance use disorders. [1, 8, 9]
