Multiple individual, social, and environmental factors influence harassment and bullying in educational settings.
Individual and Personal Risk Factors
• Perceived Differences: Students who appear or are perceived as different—such as differences in appearance, weight, race, religion, sexual orientation, gender identity, or disability—face a significantly higher risk of victimization.
• Behavioral and Emotional Traits: Impulsivity, poor emotional regulation, anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, or social isolation make individuals more vulnerable to being targeted.
• Perpetrator Traits: Those who bully often exhibit traits like a desire for social dominance, a need for control, aggression, low empathy, impulsivity, or a history of being bullied themselves.
Social and Family Dynamics
• Gender Norms: Bullying is often driven by gender dynamics and "gender policing" (regulating behavior to fit traditional stereotypes). Boys experience more frequent physical and verbal aggression, while girls experience higher rates of relational, psychological, and digital bullying.
• Family Environment: Low parental support, poor parental discipline, lack of emotional involvement at home, or exposure to violence within the family increase the likelihood of a student engaging in bullying behavior.
• Peer Influence: Association with aggressive peer groups or friends who support violence heavily predicts bullying behavior.
School and Environmental Factors
• Supervision and Infrastructure: Unstructured, low-supervision areas—such as hallways, cafeterias, playgrounds, and classrooms—exhibit the highest frequencies of traditional bullying.
• School Climate: A negative school climate, lack of clear anti-bullying protocols, or an institutional culture that normalizes exclusion or discrimination fosters ongoing harassment.
• Digital Access: The rise of cyberbullying introduces risk factors such as online anonymity, rapid content spread, disinhibition, and algorithmic polarization that extend harassment beyond school grounds.
