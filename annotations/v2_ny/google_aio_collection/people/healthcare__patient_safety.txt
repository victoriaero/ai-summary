AI OVERVIEW TEXT
============================================================

Patient safety in healthcare depends on a combination of system structures, human behaviors, technology, and patient characteristics. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), these factors interact across multiple levels:
1. System and Organizational Factors
• Workload and Staffing: Chronic staff shortages, high patient-to-nurse ratios, and long work hours increase fatigue and burnout, raising the risk of errors.
• Leadership and Culture: A blameworthy or punitive environment discourages staff from reporting mistakes, whereas a blame-free safety culture encourages learning and open communication.
• Care Coordination: Poor handoffs between shifts or departments, disorganized workflows, and unclear procedures frequently lead to dropped care and safety breaks.
• Resource Availability: Lacking standard medical equipment, clean physical spaces, or proper clinical guidelines weakens day-to-day care.
2. Human Factors and Teamwork
• Communication Breakdowns: Ineffective communication among doctors, nurses, allied staff, and families is one of the leading causes of medical errors.
• Cognitive Bias and Fatigue: Tired, stressed, or overextended healthcare providers experience reduced cognitive performance, slower decision-making, and memory lapses.
• Team Dynamics: Strong interprofessional teamwork and psychological safety (feeling safe to speak up about a concern) directly improve adherence to safety standards.
3. Technological Factors
• Health Information Systems: Poorly designed electronic health records (EHRs), confusing user interfaces, or alert fatigue can cause data entry mistakes or missed warnings.
• Medication and Monitoring Systems: Flaws in automated dispensing cabinets, smart pumps, or patient-monitoring devices create hazards if staff misuse or misconfigure them.
4. Patient-Related Factors
• Health Literacy and Understanding: Patients who struggle to understand their diagnoses, medication schedules, or treatment plans are at higher risk for non-adherence or medication errors.
• Engagement and Participation: A patient's willingness and experience in participating in their own safety activities (such as asking questions or verifying procedures) positively influences outcomes.
• Demographics and Status: Age, employment status, underlying illness severity, and past medical experiences also shape how safely care is experienced and received.
5. External and Policy Factors
• Regulatory Frameworks: The absence of national safety policies or inconsistent enforcement by regulatory bodies leaves gaps in institutional accountability.
• Economic Pressures: Financial constraints and budget cuts force healthcare facilities to stretch resources, sometimes compromising maintenance, staffing, or safety training.
