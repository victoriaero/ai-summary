Abstract
Objectives. We examined disparities among US adults with disabilities and the degree to which health insurance attenuates disparities by race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status (SES).
Methods. We pooled data from the 2001–2007 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey on individuals with disabilities aged 18 to 64 years. We modeled measures of access and use as functions of predisposing, enabling, need, and contextual factors. We then included health insurance and examined the extent to which it reduced observed differences by race, ethnicity, and SES.
Results. We found evidence of disparities in access and use among adults with disabilities. Adjusting for health insurance reduced these disparities most consistently for emergency department use. Uninsured individuals experienced substantially poorer access across most measures, including reporting a usual source of care and experiencing delays in or being unable to obtain care.
Conclusions. Although health insurance is an important enabling resource among adults with disabilities, its effect on reducing differences by race, ethnicity, and SES on health care access and use was limited. Research exploring the effects of factors such as patient–provider interactions is warranted.
Continuing its emphasis on reductions in health care disparities, Healthy People 2020 identifies the elimination of health disparities as 1 of 4 overarching goals.1 Congress has charged the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) with tracking disparities in health care access and quality among racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic groups, as well as for priority populations. In April 2012, AHRQ released its ninth report, finding that, although there was evidence of improvements in quality of care, health care access and quality are suboptimal, particularly for individuals who are racial/ethnic minorities or of lower socioeconomic status (SES).2
An estimated 1 in 5 noninstitutionalized individuals in the United States experiences a disability.3 Although individuals with disabilities have been identified as a priority population by Congress,2 examining disparities among those with disabilities has garnered less attention. There is evidence that individuals with disabilities receive fewer preventive and treatment services than individuals without disabilities.4–7 Individuals with disabilities are more likely to report cost as a barrier to care,4,8 to report higher out-of-pocket costs,9 and to report unmet need for medical care.8
Individuals with disabilities are disproportionately racial/ethnic minority and of lower SES. Rates of disability are higher for Blacks (22.2%) than for Hispanics (17.8%) and Whites (17.4%).3 Using data from the American Community Survey, Erickson and Lee10 found prevalence of disability to decline with increasing income. Disability is also associated with educational attainment. Substantially more people without a disability (30.8%) had a bachelor’s degree or higher than people with a disability (12.5%). Limited work has focused on disparities by race and SES on health care access and use among individuals with disabilities.11
Health insurance provides an essential link to health services and outcomes,12,13 attenuating but not eliminating disparities by race and SES in the general population.14 Data from the 2010 American Community Survey indicate that, nationally, 82.1% of working-age individuals with disabilities had health insurance, compared with 78.2% among working-age individuals without disabilities.15 Nationally, 17.9% of working-age adults with disabilities were uninsured, but there was substantial state variability. Adults with disabilities are much more likely to have public, relative to private, coverage—50.5% public and 43% private coverage nationally in 2010 (some individuals have both public and private coverage). Research has rarely examined the extent to which health insurance coverage reduces potential disparities by race, ethnicity, and SES among individuals with disabilities. Thus, we extended the research on disparities among individuals with disabilities by examining whether minority and lower-SES adults with disabilities experience disparate access to and use of health care services.
METHODS
Drawing from Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Care Access,16,17 we examined the following 4 hypotheses:
- Minority individuals with disabilities will experience poorer potential access (e.g., having a usual source of care) than White individuals. Minority individuals with disabilities will be more likely to experience potential access barriers (e.g., delaying care) than White individuals.
- Individuals with disabilities and of lower SES will experience poorer potential access (e.g., having a usual source of care) than individuals of higher SES. Individuals with disabilities and of lower SES will be more likely to experience potential access barriers (e.g., delaying care) than individuals of higher SES.
- Individuals with disabilities who are uninsured will experience poorer potential access (e.g., having a usual source of care) than individuals with health insurance. Individuals with disabilities who are uninsured will be more likely to experience potential access barriers (e.g., delaying care) than individuals with health insurance.
- The effects of race, ethnicity, and SES on health care access and use will be significantly reduced by health insurance coverage. Specifically, models that include health insurance will have significantly smaller effects for race, ethnicity, and SES than models that do not include health insurance variables.
We used the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS). The MEPS uses the National Health Interview Survey as its sampling frame, yielding a nationally representative survey of noninstitutionalized adults and children in the United States. A multistage probability design is used, with an oversampling of certain subpopulations to yield more precise estimates. Consistent with Andersen’s model, we collected data on a number of population predisposing (e.g., race and ethnicity), enabling (e.g., health insurance), and need (e.g., presence of a disability) variables, as well as health care access and use. We pooled 5 MEPS panels for years 2001 through 2007 to attain an adequate sample size of adults with disabilities aged 18 to 64 years (n = 10 122). Because of the complex nature of the MEPS and the editing prerelease that is done to minimize item nonresponse, we limited our analyses to complete cases and did not attempt to impute values for the modest numbers (1%–2%) of missing values for control variables. We did include a missing category for the education variables because of its size and role as a key study variable.18
Variables
Dependent variables.
We used 3 access measures (having a specific source of ongoing care; having a source of ongoing care that is a “place” [e.g., clinic], relative to a provider, with a place deemed as suboptimal19; and delaying or being unable to obtain needed medical or dental care or prescription drugs) and 1 service utilization measure (use of the emergency department [ED]). We drew the access measures from AHRQ National Disparities Report access measures.2 The ED is the safety net of the safety net, providing care to those who lack insurance or are unable to access providers in the community.20 We examined ED use as an indicator of potential provider access issues. We drew our usual source of care measure from a MEPS question on whether the respondent has a usual place or person they use for care. Further questions in the MEPS elucidate whether that source is an individual person as opposed to a clinic or facility; we used this to create our measures of a person or a place as a usual source of care. MEPS asks a series of questions on whether the respondent was unable to or experienced difficulty or delay in accessing different types of medical goods and services (i.e., medical care, prescription drugs, and dental care). We combined these measures to create a summary binary measure that reflects any difficulty, delay, or being unable to obtain care. Information on ED utilization is collected by MEPS. We used a simply binary measure of any ED utilization (≥ 1 visits to the ED) in the reference period.
Independent variables.
Our analyses focused on race and ethnicity, SES, and health insurance. We used MEPS self-reported race and ethnicity variables to create mutually exclusive categories of non-Hispanic Whites, non-Hispanic Blacks, and Hispanic ethnicity. The SES variables included measures of self-reported educational attainment (< high school, high school, some college, undergraduate degree, and > undergraduate degree) and income (self-reported in the MEPS as a ratio of family income to the federal poverty threshold [FPL], adjusted for family size). Categories of health insurance included private, Medicare, Medicaid, dually insured (Medicare and private or Medicare and Medicaid), and uninsured. To create our insurance variables, we used variables in the MEPS that reported whether a respondent had a particular type of health insurance (e.g., Medicaid) at any time in the referenced calendar year. Persons were coded as uninsured if they responded negatively to all of the questions regarding insurance types (our tabulations replicated those included in the MEPS data). Following Andersen’s model, race, ethnicity, and education predispose one to use services, and income and health insurance enable the use of services.
Additional predisposing variables included age, gender, and marital status. We measured age as a continuous variable gathered in the first year of MEPS participation. We used self-reported data on marital status to create categories of married, widowed, divorced or separated, never married, and living with a partner.
Our measures of “need” are derived from 6 disability measures, drawn from the International Classification of Functioning, and include sensory, physical, cognitive, functional, mental health, and work-related disabilities.21 The MEPS includes questions that elicit information about physical and sensory limitations (e.g., difficulty walking up a flight of stairs, difficulty standing, difficulty seeing even with glasses), cognitive limitations (e.g., confusion or memory loss that interferes with daily activities), difficulties with activities of daily living (e.g., dressing, bathing), instrumental activities of daily living (e.g., managing money), and difficulties working (e.g., working is difficult because of physical problems). Mental health status was assessed with the mental health summary score of the Short-Form 12 and we used a Mental Component Summary threshold of 29 or below as an indicator of mental illness. Following Altman and Bernstein,22 we combined these measures to create measures of “basic” and “complex” activity limitations. We created mutually exclusive measures of basic only (i.e., sensory, physical, cognitive, and mental health), complex only (i.e., functional and work-related), or both basic and complex activity limitations. To capture contextual environmental influences, we included whether a person resided in a metropolitan statistical area and geographic region. We converted all categorical measures to a set of binary variables for our regressions, with White, less than high school, below the FPL, private insurance coverage, married, basic activity limitation, and Northeast as reference groups.
Data Analyses
We employed the class of Stata survey procedures version 12 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX) designed for use with complex surveys to account for the sample design features, including stratification and clustering. The MEPS includes sample weights that account for probabilities of selection, nonresponse, and poststratification. In addition, the MEPS provides the design variables needed to obtain population-weighted estimates of counts and proportions, and correct standard errors for estimates. We adjusted the weights to account for our use of pooled years of data by simply dividing the annual weights by the number of years pooled as suggested by AHRQ (http://meps.ahrq.gov/mepsweb/data_stats/download_data/pufs/h36/h36u07doc.shtml).
We descriptively compared the characteristics of people with disabilities by health insurance coverage. To examine hypotheses 1 through 3, we then estimated multivariate logistic regression models and examined the significance of our variables of interest. To examine hypothesis 4, we first estimated models with race, ethnicity, education, and income as well as the additional predisposing, need, and contextual factors, examining whether effects related to minority status and SES were evident after we accounted for covariates. In a second model, we included health insurance. We used post hoc tests of significance for each of the coefficients on race, ethnicity, education, and income significant in the models without health insurance to examine hypothesis 4 regarding the influence of health insurance on our variables of interest. We tested whether the significant coefficients were either reduced in size or were no longer significant after we adjusted for health insurance.
We use postestimation tests (the SUEST command in Stata) to test cross-model hypotheses regarding the coefficient estimates in each model (e.g., the estimated effect of being Hispanic in one model versus the other model that includes Hispanic as well as health insurance variables). Such tests evaluate the null hypothesis that the coefficient estimate in the one model is not significantly different from the coefficient estimate in the other model; a significant test result indicates that the 2 coefficient estimates are significantly different from each other. Following Perneger,23 we did not correct for multiple comparisons but provided additional information related to the level of significance (i.e., P ≤ .05; P ≤ .01; P ≤ .001) for each post hoc test.
To facilitate analysis and interpretation, we used our estimates to calculate relative risks (RRs) for our logit models.24 Age was the only continuous variable. We evaluated the effects of age set at 1 standard deviation below and above the mean value for age. We evaluated all binary variables when set to zero and then 1. In each case, we set the regressor of interest to the value for all cases, and calculated predicted probabilities with that value for the regressor of interest and original (case-level) values for all other variables. We calculated predicted probabilities for each observation. Then we calculated an RR for each observation as the predicted probability of the outcome when a regressor was set to 1 divided by the predicted probability of the outcome when the regressor was set to zero. We report mean RRs. Standard errors and confidence intervals for RRs are not directly available from Stata. We used bootstrapping procedures in Stata to sample our original sample by using equal probability sampling with replacement to estimate 95% confidence intervals for our RRs. We use 1000 replications for our bootstrapping procedures and report the bias-corrected empirical confidence intervals. We discuss findings that are statistically significant at P ≤ .05.
RESULTS
White adults with disabilities were more likely to have private insurance coverage, relative to Black and Hispanic adults with disabilities (Table 1). Black adults were more likely to have Medicare or Medicaid coverage, whereas Hispanic adults were more likely to have Medicaid coverage or to be uninsured. Adults with less than a high-school degree or who were high-school graduates were more likely to have Medicaid coverage or to be uninsured, whereas private insurance coverage was more likely among those with a college degree or higher. We observed similar patterns for income. Those lacking health insurance were more likely to report an access problem relative to those adults with insurance (Table 2). Emergency department use was highest among adults with disabilities insured by Medicaid or with dual coverage.

Race and Ethnicity
In models unadjusted for health insurance, Hispanic adults were less likely than White adults to report a usual source of care (RR = 0.919; 95% CI = 0.888, 0.948). Black (RR = 1.288; 95% CI = 1.105, 1.478) and Hispanic (RR = 1.325; 95% CI = 1.123, 1.537) adults were more likely to report a suboptimal usual source of care. Black adults had a higher RR of ED use (RR = 1.122; 95% CI = 1.024; 1.237), and Hispanic adults had a lower RR of ED use (RR = 0.795; 95% CI = 0.703, 0.893), relative to White adults. Both Black (RR = 0.779; 95% CI = 0.703, 0.867) and Hispanic (RR = 0.723; 95% CI = 0.645, 0.808) adults with disabilities were less likely to report delaying or being unable to receive needed medical care, relative to White adults with disabilities. After we adjusted for health insurance, Hispanics continued to have a lower RR of reporting a usual source of care (RR = 0.935; 95% CI = 0.906, 0.965) and Blacks (RR = 1.300; 95% CI = 1.119, 1.494) and Hispanics (RR = 1.284; 95% CI = 1.079, 1.497) had a higher RR of reporting a suboptimal usual source of care compared with Whites. The RR of ED use was higher among Black adults (RR = 1.107; 95% CI = 1.007, 1.219) and lower among Hispanic adults (RR = 0.796; 95% CI = 0.705, 0.897) relative to their White counterparts. Both Black (RR = 0.796; 95% CI = 0.716, 0.881) and Hispanic (RR = 0.688; 95% CI = 0.612, 0.772) adults continued to have a lower RR of reporting delays or being unable to obtain needed care, compared with White adults with disabilities.
To evaluate hypothesis 4, we tested whether adjusting for health insurance significantly changed the effects of race and ethnicity. Among Black adults, adjusting for health insurance coverage significantly reduced the RR of ED use compared with White adults with disabilities. The RR of reporting a delay or being unable to obtain needed care relative to Whites moved closer to 1 (from RR = 0.779; 95% CI = 0.703, 0.867 to RR = 0.796; 95% CI = 0.716, 0.881) and this change was significant. Among Hispanic adults, adjusting for health insurance significantly increased the RR of having a usual source of care, reduced the RR of having a suboptimal usual of care, and reduced the RR of reporting an access problem, compared with White adults with disabilities. In each case, the effect of health insurance was to reduce the effects of race and ethnicity, bringing the RR closer to 1, with the exception of the change for Hispanic adults in reporting an access problem.
Education
In models unadjusted for health insurance, we observed education effects most consistently across levels of education for reporting a usual source of care, a suboptimal usual source of care, and ED use. For example, individuals with a high-school degree had an RR of 0.835 (95% CI = 0.722, 0.965) for reporting a suboptimal usual source of care compared with individuals without a high-school degree. This RR was 0.575 (95% CI = 0.432, 0.742) among adults with a bachelor’s degree and 0.499 (95% CI = 0.311, 0.734) among adults with greater than a bachelor’s degree, compared with adults without a high-school degree. Education was not significantly associated with reporting an access problem. In models adjusted for health insurance, effects were most consistent for reporting a suboptimal usual source of care and ED use, and were in the hypothesized direction. To illustrate, adults with a high-school degree had an RR of 0.844 (95% CI = 0.728, 0.976) for reporting a suboptimal usual source of care, compared with adults without a high-school degree. This RR was 0.599 (95% CI = 0.450, 0.777) among adults with a bachelor’s degree and 0.518 (95% CI = 0.323, 0.756) among adults with greater than a bachelor’s degree.
The change in the effect of education after we accounted for health insurance was most consistently significant for ED use. The addition of health insurance also reduced the effects of education on reporting a usual source of care for individuals with some college education as well as postgraduate education relative to individuals without a high-school degree. As adjusting for health insurance brought the RR associated with education closer to 1, it attenuated the effects of education on ED use.
Income
We observed income effects consistently, and in the expected direction, across measures. For example, adults with incomes between 100% and 199% of the FPL had an RR of 0.895 (95% CI = 0.808, 0.990) of using the ED relative to individuals with incomes below the FPL; this RR was 0.827 (95% CI = 0.725, 0.936) for those with incomes at 200% to 299% of FPL; 0.692 (95% CI = 0.591, 0.807) for those with incomes at 300% to 399% of FPL; and 0.690 (95% CI = 0.608, 0.786) for those with incomes at 400% of FPL and higher. In models adjusted for health insurance, we continued to observe these patterns, most consistently for reporting unmet need and ED use.
Adjustment for health insurance reduced the significance of the effects of income most clearly for ED use, as the P value for the change in coefficients was less than .001 for each level of income. Health insurance significantly reduced the effect of income on reporting a usual source of care, but only at the highest 2 income levels. Similar to education, adjusting for health insurance attenuated the effects of income, bringing the RR closer to 1.
Health Insurance
Health insurance also significantly affected individuals with disabilities’ access to and use of services. Relative to individuals with private insurance, those with dual coverage had a higher RR of having a usual source of care (RR = 1.060; 95% CI = 1.020, 1.091). Individuals with dual coverage also had a lower RR of a suboptimal usual source of care (RR = 0.743; 95% CI = 0.585, 0.931), compared with those with private insurance. However, individuals with dual coverage had a higher RR of ED use (RR = 1.347; 95% CI = 1.167, 1.551). Individuals covered by Medicaid had a higher RR of ED use (RR = 1.350; 95% CI = 1.186, 1.524), compared with adults with disabilities insured privately, as did adults insured by Medicare (RR = 1.230; 95% CI = 1.018, 1.433).
Individuals with disabilities who were uninsured had a significantly lower RR of having a usual source of care (RR = 0.760; 95% CI = 0.731, 0.795). If they had a usual source of care, they had a higher RR of it being a suboptimal usual source of care (RR = 1.797; 95% CI = 1.511, 2.113). Those lacking health insurance also had a higher RR of reporting being unable to obtain needed care (RR = 1.579; 95% CI = 1.43, 1.729). Those without health insurance did not differ significantly from those with private insurance in the RR of ED use.
DISCUSSION
We found evidence of disparities in access and use of services by race, ethnicity, education, and income among adults with disabilities. As an enabling variable, health insurance was related to access and use of services. The effect of health insurance on reducing disparities, our fourth hypothesis, was limited largely to ED use. We summarize these findings and discuss related policy and research implications.
Our first 2 hypotheses focused on disparities by race, ethnicity, education, and income. We observed disparities for 2 measures of access related to a usual source of care, a measure of potential access in Andersen’s model. Hispanic adults had a significantly lower RR of reporting a usual source of care relative to White adults. We observed some education effects, as well as income effects, and in the expected direction. Both Black and Hispanic adults had a higher RR of reporting a place as a usual source of care, relative to White adults with disabilities. The RR of reporting a suboptimal source of care declined significantly as income increased.
A usual source of care, or a provider that one consistently sees, was associated with a range of health benefits.25–27 Continuity of health care services is enhanced when one has a usual source of care, which is associated with greater use of preventive and ambulatory care services, reduced ED use, and greater satisfaction. When that usual source of care is a primary care provider, there is evidence of improved trust as well as good communication between patients and their providers.28 Individuals who have trust in their providers are more likely to seek care, adhere to treatment recommendations, and return for follow-up.29 Improved trust and communication are also related to improved health outcomes.30–32 Lack of a usual source of care, or having a suboptimal usual source of care (e.g., a clinic) likely places individuals with disabilities at increased risk for complications from chronic disease.
The source(s) of the race, ethnicity, and SES differences in reporting a usual source of care and in having an optimal usual source of care among adults with disabilities is important to determine. Interactions with providers may influence individuals’ willingness to continue seeing a particular provider. Specific to disability, there is evidence that provider attitudes toward individuals with disabilities may influence their ability or willingness to establish a relationship with a provider.33 Iezzoni et al.34 found that people with disabilities covered through Medicare more often perceived that “the doctor acts as though he/she was doing you a favor by talking to you” relative to individuals with no difficulties. Developing interventions to improve interactions with providers may be warranted.35 Diversifying the provider workforce may also facilitate improved interactions.36
We found the RR of reporting delaying or being unable to obtain needed care lower among minorities with disabilities, relative to Whites, an effect counter to what was hypothesized. As income increased, the RR of reporting being unable to obtain needed care decreased. It is unclear why minority adults had a lower RR of reporting being unable to obtain needed care, although the finding is consistent with what is found in the general population in AHRQ’s national disparities reports.2 Among Black adults with disabilities, it may be that needed care is sought through the ED, as their RR of ED use is higher. There may also be predisposing differences in certain factors that may shape the perceived need for care, such as beliefs about the efficacy of care or cultural influences on health care seeking37 that influence this finding.
Black adults with disabilities had a higher RR and Hispanic adults with disabilities had a lower RR of ED use, compared with their White counterparts. Income was consistently associated with ED use among adults with disabilities, with the RR of use decreasing as income increased. Education demonstrated a similar relation as income. Nationally, ED visits have increased substantially. Between 1997 and 2007, ED visits increased by almost double the rate that would be expected from population growth.20(p664) Adults insured by Medicaid contributed significantly to the observed increase in visit rates. We also observed increased visit rates for Blacks and Hispanics, with Blacks’ visit rate almost double that of any other racial or ethnic minority. Our findings, coupled with the finding that Black adults with disabilities were more likely to have a suboptimal usual source of care, suggest that access to primary care providers may be problematic.
Our third hypothesis focused on the effects of health insurance on access and use of services. In Andersen’s model, health insurance is an enabling variable, directly affecting access and use of services. In our sample of adults with disabilities, 14.1% lacked health insurance. Individuals with disabilities who lack health insurance experienced significantly and substantively poorer access to care. We found access to a usual source of care was problematic, as individuals without health insurance had a lower RR of reporting a usual source of care, compared with individuals with private insurance. This finding of poorer access is consistent with what is found in the general population.2 Individuals without insurance who had a usual source of care had a significantly higher RR of that being a suboptimal source of care. Adults with disabilities who lacked insurance also had a higher RR of reporting delaying or being unable to obtain needed care. Policy efforts to expand health insurance coverage may, over the next several years, increase access to and use of services for adults with disabilities who lack health insurance.
In our sample, 8.99% of adults with disabilities had dual coverage. Individuals with dual coverage had a higher RR of reporting a usual source of care and a lower RR of reporting a place as a usual source of care. As dual coverage captures the effect of having more extensive coverage, these effects could be expected. However, dual coverage was also related to a higher RR of ED use, which may reflect greater need for care not captured in our measures of disability.
Among adults with disabilities in our sample, 22.1% were insured by Medicaid. Individuals insured by Medicaid had a higher RR of ED use; this is consistent with the findings of Tang et al.20 There is evidence to suggest that providers are less willing to provide care to individuals insured through Medicaid, because of typically lower reimbursement levels.38 Further examination of factors contributing to higher ED use among individuals with disabilities insured by Medicaid is important, because of the long-standing role of Medicaid as a source of coverage for low-income people with disabilities. Adults with disabilities insured by Medicare also had a higher RR for ED use. As eligibility for Medicaid and Medicare among nonelderly adults with disabilities is linked to eligibility for federal disability programs, our Medicaid and Medicare effects may again capture unmeasured greater need for care relative to those with private insurance.
Our final hypothesis focused on the effect of health insurance in reducing disparities among adults with disabilities. We found the most consistent effect on ED use, where health insurance significantly reduced the effects of race, education, and income. Substantive effects were modest for race and education, but somewhat larger for income. Health insurance also significantly reduced the effects of ethnicity for 2 measures related to a usual source of care, but increased the effect on ethnicity on reporting unmet need for care. As some work found health insurance to attenuate disparities in the general population,14 further examination of its influence on access and use among adults with disabilities, relative to factors such as physical accessibility and provider perceptions, is warranted.
Limitations
It is important to note study limitations. Our categories of health insurance are often used in analyses of the influence of health insurance on access and use. Yet certain categories of coverage, including private insurance and Medicaid, are heterogeneous. Private insurance offerings will vary in important ways related to the breadth and depth of coverage, cost-sharing provisions, and other characteristics. Medicaid benefits vary by state, because of the structure of mandatory and optional services that provide states flexibility in their Medicaid offerings. Services available to those lacking insurance may vary from area to area.
A second potential limitation relates to our modeling of health insurance, in that we did not employ modeling to address concerns such as endogeneity.13 Much of the research related to health insurance in the general population compares the uninsured to privately insured, separately discussing why Medicaid effects may appear counterintuitive.12 Work focused on Medicare primarily addresses the effects of coverage for older adults as they transition from no insurance to Medicare.13 Importantly, among working-age individuals with disabilities, receipt of insurance through Medicare and Medicaid is a function of having a disability and meeting eligibility criteria for Supplemental Security Income, which links them to Medicaid in most states, or Social Security Disability Insurance, which links them to Medicare following a 2-year waiting period. Relations among disability, disability program participation, and health insurance warrant continued examination.
It is also important to note that we constructed our data to be cross-sectional. Thus, we can observe associations among factors but cannot conclude causal relations. Although we measured key dimensions of Andersen’s model such as income and health insurance, certain factors, such as perceptions of the efficacy of health care, are not assessed in MEPS.
Conclusions
In summary, a significant share of individuals in the United States experiences a disability, with disability experienced disproportionately by racial/ethnic minorities and individuals of lower SES. Among adults with disabilities, we found significant differences in access and use of health care by race, ethnicity, and SES. Health insurance attenuated disparities related to ED use but had a limited impact on disparities related to our measures of potential access, such as reporting a usual source of care. Adults with disabilities who lacked health insurance experienced poorer access across measures, with the exception of ED use. Because of the role of health insurance coverage in facilitating access to care, policy efforts to expand health insurance coverage among individuals with disabilities are supported by our findings. Further examination of factors related to access and use, particularly those that may have a more significant impact on individuals with disabilities, such as physical accessibility and provider perceptions, is also important.
