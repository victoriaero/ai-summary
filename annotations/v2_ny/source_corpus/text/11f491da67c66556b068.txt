Abstract
The current work builds on past research by examining the extent to which demographic, attitudinal, and situational factors 
predict public perceptions of police use of force. Specifically, we move beyond general perceptions of force into specific 
perceptions of threat and responsibility related to both the officer and civilian. In a between-subjects experimental design, 
participants (N = 724) listened to a traffic stop vignette where a civilian engages in varying levels of noncompliance (less 
noncompliance v. more noncompliance) and the officer uses force. Participants completed measures of threat, responsibil­
ity, and reasonableness, as well as demographics and attitudes towards police legitimacy and authoritarianism. Participant 
age, education level, and political orientation significantly impacted judgments. Yet, the impact of demographics was 
diminished when police legitimacy and authoritarian attitudes were accounted for. The situational influence of civilian 
action also predicted perceptions, with attitudinal influences remaining significant even when controlling for civilian 
noncompliance. Individuals’ education level and police legitimacy attitudes predicted their perceptions of initial threat to 
both the officer and civilian prior to any officer force or civilian noncompliance. Implications and future directions are 
discussed.

In attempts to further understand police-civilian encoun­
ters, researchers have begun to examine how perceiver 
demographics (e.g., Navarro and Hansen 2023) and attitudes

encounter-specific factors like the level of civilian aggres­

of police use of force. The existing literature (discussed 
in detail below) regarding the role of demographics (e.g., 
political affiliation, age, and sex) and attitudes (e.g., police 
legitimacy) has primarily focused on how the perceiver’s 
demographics or attitudes predict perceptions of whether 
the force used by officers was reasonable (compared to 
excessive). Understanding how individual differences, such 
as demographics or attitudes, impact use of force judg­
ments may benefit attorneys in the jury selection process for 
alleged excessive use of force cases. Research suggests that 
jurors’ case-related attitudes (e.g., Chapman and Bornstein 
1996; Estrada-Reynolds et al. 2015; Sivasubramaniam et al. 
2020) and demographics (albeit to a lesser extent; Lieber­
man and Sales 2007) impact their legal judgments. Beyond 
the courtroom, a knowledge of how demographic charac­
teristics impact public perceptions of police and police use 
of force has the potential to improve police training and 
improve communication between officers and civilians in 
various communities. For instance, knowing how different 
demographics perceive the police and police-civilian inter­
actions may improve the cultural sensitivity training some 
police agencies provide, for which no current nationally 
centralized training exists (Moon et al. 2018).
While reasonable force judgments are important and 
directly related to how jurors may decide relevant legal 
cases, a more nuanced examination into how these fac­
tors predict perceptions of other facets of police use of 
force encounters, such as perceptions of threat, is essential. 
This is particularly true given that scholars have recently 
called for a greater emphasis on understanding threat within 
police-civilian encounters (Bennell et al. 2021). Given that 
research has shown a civilian’s behavior can impact how 
threatening the situation is perceived to be (e.g., McClure 
et al. 2023), we sought to examine how a perceiver’s demo­
graphics and attitudes, along with the situational influence 
of a civilian’s behavior, affect the public’s perceptions of 
threat in a police-civilian use of force encounter. In the cur­
rent work, we experimentally manipulated the civilian’s 
action in a police use of force encounter. We also measured 
participants’ perceptions of police legitimacy, authoritari­
anism, and demographic information to examine how each 
predicted their perceptions of threat in a police-civilian 
interaction.
Demographic Predictors of Perceptions of 
Police Use of Force
When examining public perceptions across a variety of 
criminal justice issues, an individual’s demographics can 
play an important role in predicting their judgments. A 
handful of demographics previously examined within per­
ceptions of policing include, but are not limited to, race, sex, 
age, political orientation, education, and socioeconomic 
status. When examining perceptions of police use of force 
encounters specifically, the role of these demographics is 
far from conclusive. Some previous research finds demo­
graphics like sex and age play an important role in predict­
ing perceptions of use of force interactions (e.g., Simon et 
al. 2021; Trahan and Russell 2017), and other studies find 
no impact, or opposite effects (e.g., Mourtgos and Adams 
2020; Weitzer and Tuch 2006). In this section, we briefly 
review how a perceiver’s race, sex, age, political orienta­
tion, education level, and income affect their perceptions of 
police and use of force incidents.
Race
Race and ethnicity are commonly studied factors in police 
use of force (e.g., Arthur and Case 1994; Brown and Bene­
dict 2002; Dierenfeldt et al. 2024; Mourtgos and Adams 
2020; Roche and Roux 2017). Previous research consis­
tently indicates that racial minorities have more negative 
views of police use of force compared to White individu­
als (e.g., Boudreau et al. 2019; Jefferis et al. 2011; Johnson

and Kuhns 2009; Peffley and Hurwitz 2010; Strickler and 
Lawson 2020; Wu 2014). For instance, Boudreau and col­
leagues (2019) examined public perceptions of police use of 
deadly force and how these perceptions impact how individ­
uals assign blame for these incidents across various related 
actors. Results indicated that these judgments varied by 
race, such that Black respondents, compared to non-Black 
respondents, attributed significantly more blame to police 
during these deadly force incidents when respondents were 
given the choice of assigning blame to the police, the vic­
tim, or local or state/federal officials. Increased disapproval 
of police use of force is not limited to comparing White and 
Black individuals, with research showing Latinos and Asian 
Americans also express more unfavorable views of police 
use of force compared to White Americans (e.g., Berk-
Seligson 1990; Carter 1983; Sun & Wu, 2015). This trend is 
arguably unsurprising given the history of police mistreat­
ment of racial minorities in the United States (e.g., Hadden 
2021; Hurwitz and Peffley 2005; Johnson and Kuhns 2009).
Sex
Contrasting race, previous research on sex differences in 
police use of force attitudes finds inconsistent results. Some 
research suggests men generally express higher approval of 
police use of force (across reasonable and excessive force 
scenarios) compared to women (Arthur and Case 1994; Bar­
kan and Cohn 1998; Johnson and Kuhns 2009; Mourtgos 
and Adams 2020; Ratcliffe et al. 2015; Simon et al. 2021; 
Thompson and Lee 2004). However, other research has 
found women are more in favor of police use of excessive 
force than men, and suggest no sex differences when the 
force used was reasonable (Cao et al. 1996; Gerber & Jack­
son, 2017; Lai and Zhao 2010; Navarro and Hansen 2023; 
Reisig and Correia 1997; Weitzer and Tuch 2002). Other 
studies reported no relationship between sex and satisfac­
tion with police more generally (Haberman et al. 2016; 
Weitzer and Tuch 2006).
Age
Age is another demographic factor that can influence per­
ceptions of police use of force, with research indicating 
somewhat mixed results. When considering general atti­
tudes towards police, older individuals tend to report greater 
satisfaction with and less suspicion of police than younger 
adults (e.g., Brown and Coulter 1983; Cao et al. 1996; Cher­
mak et al. 2001). When looking at police use of force judg­
ments specifically, research suggests older individuals tend 
to be more approving of police use of force than younger 
individuals (e.g., Trahan and Russell 2017), while other 
research suggests no age differences (e.g., Carter and Corra 
2016; Mourtgos and Adams 2020; Simon et al. 2021). One 
potential explanation for these contradictory results is that 
the impact of age may be dependent on just how excessive 
the force used by police was. Older adults tend to be less 
in favor of police use of excessive force and more in favor 
of reasonable force compared to younger adults (Gerber & 
Jackson, 2016; Navarro and Hansen 2023).
Political Orientation
When considering the impact of an individuals’ political 
orientation, previous research indicates those with more lib­
eral (compared to conservative) political views tend to hold 
more unfavorable opinions of police (e.g., Cao and Stack 
2005; Cao and Zhao 2005; Tyler et al. 1997) and their use 
of force (e.g., Braga et al. 2014; Mourtgos and Adams 2020; 
Trahan and Russell 2017). Research also shows liberal-lean­
ing individuals tend to favor punishing officers who have 
committed a false-positive error (i.e., shooting an unarmed 
person) to a greater extent than more conservative individu­
als (Patil 2018). This relationship between political orienta­
tion and views of police is not unique to the U.S.; research 
from the United Kingdom also finds an association between 
political conservatism and increased positive perceptions of 
police use of force (Bradford et al. 2017). Notably, recent 
work by Navarro and Hansen (2023) suggests a potentially 
more nuanced view of liberal individuals’ perceptions. Lib­
eral individuals’ views of police use of force appear to be 
more dependent on the threat level present in the use of 
force scenario, when compared to conservative individuals. 
Conservatives tended to approve of police use of force when 
force was used when there was a violent threat to others or 
the officer, and conservatives maintained strong approval for 
force across situations that lacked a clear threat of violence, 
aligning with previous research (e.g., Patil 2018; Tyler et al. 
1997). In contrast, liberals’ judgments varied as a function 
of the threatening situation, such that liberals’ support for 
the use of force decreased as threat levels became less clear.
Education
The empirical evidence on the impact of education on pub­
lic use of force judgments is mixed. Research shows that 
individuals with higher levels of education tend to express 
greater approval for police use of force (e.g., Arthur and 
Case 1994; Halim and Stiles 2001; Silver and Pickett 2015; 
Simon et al. 2021; Trahan and Russell 2017), whereas 
individuals with less formal education tend to hold more 
negative perceptions of the police, particularly if they have 
personally been exposed to police violence (Jackson et al. 
2021). However, some studies suggest a more complex 
relationship, indicating education’s impact might depend

Attitudinal Predictors of Perceptions of 
Police Use of Force
In addition to individuals’ demographic characteristics, 
some attitudinal measures have also been found to affect 
public perceptions of police use of force incidents. Contrary 
to the handful of demographics discussed above, certain 
attitudes predict individuals’ judgments more consistently: 
police legitimacy beliefs and authoritarian views.
Attitudes Toward Police Legitimacy
A key individual difference found to be important when 
assessing people’s perceptions of police-civilian interac­
tions is the extent to which they believe the police are a 
legitimate authority in society. Police legitimacy comprises 
multiple dimensions, including procedural fairness, dis­
tributive fairness, lawfulness, and effectiveness (Tankebe 
2013). Legitimacy directly influences citizens’ willingness 
to cooperate with police and indirectly affects cooperation 
through perceived obligations to obey (Tankebe 2013; Tyler 
and Fagan 2008). Procedural justice, characterized by citi­
zen participation, perceived neutrality of the authority in 
their decision-making, respect by the authority, and trust­
worthiness of the authority, is a key factor in shaping public 
perceptions of police legitimacy (Tyler 2004; Mazerolle et 
al. 2013). Research indicates that experiencing procedural 
justice during personal encounters with police enhances 
legitimacy, regardless of outcome favorability (Tyler and 
Fagan 2008).
Based on Tyler’s (1990, 2006) work on why people obey 
the law, Reynolds and colleagues (2018) developed a scale 
to assess people’s attitudes toward police legitimacy. The 
original scale, or its validated short form (Reynolds 2023), 
has been used in many studies exploring perceptions of 
police-civilian interactions, and it consistently predicts indi­
viduals’ decisions (e.g., Ewanation and Maeder 2023; Smith 
and Nuñez 2024). People with greater beliefs that the police 
are a legitimate authority tend to perceive police use of 
force incidents as more justified (e.g., Reynolds et al. 2018), 
on situation-specific factors. Navarro and Hansen (2023) 
found that the impact of education on use of force judg­
ments may depend on the officer’s specific actions and level 
of force, with higher levels of education being associated 
with greater approval of police use of excessive, but not rea­
sonable, force. Lastly, some studies suggest either no sig­
nificant relationship between education and perceptions of 
police use of force (Johnson and Kuhns 2009) or an inverse 
relationship (as education increases, approval decreases; 
Braga et al. 2014; Mourtgos and Adams 2020). For instance, 
people with baccalaureate degrees or higher (compared to 
those with lower educational degrees) have been found to 
have more negative general assessments of police officers’ 
actions (Braga et al. 2014).
Income
Income is another factor considered within the context 
of public perceptions of police and use of force. Overall, 
empirical evidence supports the notion that individuals 
with higher income tend to be more approving of police 
and police use of force (e.g., Brown & Cutler, 1983; Cao 
et al. 1996; Carter and Corra 2016; Mourtgos and Adams 
2020; Parker et al. 1995; Simon et al. 2021). For instance, 
Braga and colleagues (2014) assessed individuals’ income 
levels and general attitudes toward police. Respondents 
with incomes over $40,000 had significantly more positive 
attitudes toward police than those with less than $40,000. 
Jackson and colleagues (2021) reported similar correlations 
across police legitimacy/trust, effectiveness, and satisfac­
tion. This trend is supported by a 2016 national survey that 
found 79% of white Americans with incomes over $60,000 
held more favorable attitudes toward police compared to 
56% of white Americans with incomes of less than $30,000 
(Ekins 2016). This relationship is seen with perceptions of 
police use of force as well, with numerous studies indicat­
ing that individuals with higher income are more approv­
ing of officers using force (e.g., Mourtgos and Adams 2020; 
Simon et al. 2021).
Summary of Demographic Predictors
Previous research regarding individual demographic factors 
of race, political orientation, and income on individuals’ per­
ceptions of police and police use of force has shown largely 
consistent results. Findings suggest that individuals who are 
racial minorities, hold liberal political views, or have lower 
incomes tend to have more negative perceptions of policing 
and use of force. In contrast, the influence of an individual’s 
sex, education level, and age on these judgments has been 
generally inconsistent. There are several potential explana­

Situational Predictors of Perceptions of 
Police Use of Force
Previous research suggests that various situational factors 
predict whether, and the extent to which, an officer uses force 
in a police-civilian encounter. Some of these situational fac­
tors include but are not limited to suspect resistance, suspect 
intoxication, presence of a weapon, location of the encoun­
ter, number of citizen bystanders, crime seriousness, and 
number of other officers present at the scene (e.g., Engel et 
al. 2000; Kavanagh, 1997; Moyer 1981; Scharf and Binder 
1983; Wolf et al. 2009). However, how the public perceives 
these different situational predictors of an officer’s use of 
force is less studied. For instance, Simpson (2017) found 
that the presence of a police uniform (compared to plain 
clothes) increased participants’ perceptions of an officer as 
aggressive, and foot patrol and bicycle officers were rated 
more positively (e.g., approachable, respectful) than vehicle 
patrol officers.
One notable situational factor explored across both 
police use of force decisions and public perceptions of force 
is the level of suspect resistance, which appears to be the 
most reliable predictor of whether an officer will use force, 
or the level of force used (e.g., Boivin 2016; Engel et al. 
2000; Garner and Maxwell 1996; Kavanagh 1997; McLean 
et al. 2022; Wolf et al., 2009). Overall, as the level of sus­
pect resistance increases, officer use of force also increases, 
which aligns with departmental policies on use of force 
(Stoughton et al. 2020). Recent work also indicates that 
public perceptions of police use of force are impacted by a 
civilian’s actions. McClure and colleagues (2023) examined 
how public perceptions of officer-civilian interactions may 
be affected by civilians’ non-verbal actions (e.g., clenched 
fists, standing close to the officer) and found that civilians’ 
non-verbal indicators of threat increased participants’ per­
ceptions that the encounter would turn violent. This provides 
initial evidence that civilians’ situation-specific actions can 
impact public perceptions of police-civilian encounters.
Additional recent work exploring the impact of situation-
specific circumstances on perceptions of use of force inci­
dents suggests a significant impact of civilian action as well. 
Vardsveen and Wiener (2022) used a mock civil trial context 
to manipulate civilian threat as either ambiguous (suspect 
pulled out an unidentified object) or high (suspect pulled 
out a knife). Participants thought the officer’s use of lethal 
force was more reasonable when suspect threat was high 
compared to low (Vardsveen and Wiener 2022). In conjunc­
tion with civilian threat, additional findings emphasize that 
individuals attend to civilian resistance when judging use of 
force encounters as well. Individuals perceive an officer’s 
use of non-lethal force against a civilian as more reasonable 
when the civilian engages in assaultive behavior towards 
officer’s use of fore actions as more acceptable (Celestin 
and Kruschke 2019), be less likely to say an officer should 
be prosecuted for murder (Burd et al. 2025), be less likely 
to convict an officer who used force (e.g., Ewanation et al. 
2022), and be less likely to suggest punishment for an offi­
cer who used force (Celestin and Kruschke 2019; Flick and 
Schweitzer 2025). Further, individuals with particularly low 
legitimacy beliefs rarely view lethal police UOF as accept­
able, even when a civilian uses lethal levels of force against 
an officer (Celestin and Kruschke 2019).
Authoritarianism
Authoritarian individuals tend to be considered submis­
sive to authority, hostile towards those who do not follow 
social norms, and guided by traditional values and social 
conventions (Altemeyer 2004; Passini 2017). Recent 
research challenges the traditional conceptualization of 
right-wing authoritarianism by Altemeyer (2004), propos­
ing a three-factor model comprising authoritarian submis­
sion, authoritarian aggression, and conventionalism instead. 
The Aggression-Submission-Conventionalism (ASC) scale, 
developed by Dunwoody and Funke (2016), is a three-factor 
measure of authoritarianism that improves upon Altemey­
er’s Right-Wing Authoritarianism scale (Altemeyer 2004) 
and has been shown to better capture authoritarianism than 
one-dimensional measures (Funke 2005; Dunwoody and 
Funke 2016).
Importantly, authoritarianism has been shown to be 
related to police legitimacy and perceptions of use of force. 
People with higher authoritarian views are more likely to 
view the police as legitimate (Reynolds et al. 2018), sug­
gesting the measures are related but distinct constructs. 
Individuals high in authoritarianism have also been found 
to view forceful actions by police more positively (e.g., 
Watson and Stevenson 2022) and to view police excessive 
use of force as justified (e.g., Milani 2020), even when con­
trolling for police legitimacy attitudes (Gerber and Jackson 
2017). Those with higher, compared to lower, authoritarian 
beliefs have also been shown to believe police officers who 
use force should receive less professional and legal punish­
ment (Flick and Schweitzer 2025). They also have more 
positive perceptions of the officer and more negative per­
ceptions of the civilian involved in the use of force encoun­
ter (Flick 2023). Lastly, increased authoritarian beliefs are 
associated with increased perceptions of societal threat (e.g., 
crime rates, economic threat; Doty et al., 1991; Roccato et 
al. 2014). Given previous research on perceptions of police 
use of force and the importance threat perceptions play in 
authoritarian worldviews, it seems possible that authoritar­
ian beliefs may be particularly predictive of individuals’ 
perceptions of threat in a police use of force encounter.

Method
Participants
Participants were recruited online through Prolific and 
included 724 community members over the age of 18 who 
passed all attention checks and reside in the United States. 
Participation took approximately 15 min, and participants 
were compensated $2.05 USD. A post-hoc sensitivity analy­
sis using the pwr package (Champely 2020) in R 4.3.1 (R 
Core Team 2023) indicated that we had 0.80 power for a 
minimum detectable effect size (f2) of 0.02, suggesting that 
the study was sufficiently powered to detect small effects 
(Cohen 1988).
Materials
The university’s institutional review board approved all 
study materials and measures. Materials and data are

Current Study
Given recent emphases on perceptions of threat in use of 
force encounters (e.g., Bennell et al. 2021; Krajewski et al., 
2024; McClure et al. 2023), the current study expands on 
previous research by examining the extent to which demo­
graphic and attitudinal factors predict perceptions of both 
threat to the officer and threat to the civilian in a non-lethal 
police use of force encounter. We also explore whether these 
demographic and attitudinal factors predict public percep­
tions of the threat at the beginning of an encounter (the ini­
tial threat) and the end of the encounter, while controlling 
for the situational-specific influence of civilian behavior. 
Lastly, although we were primarily interested in expanding 
on previous work by examining perceptions of threat spe­
cifically, we also measured perceptions of officer and civil­
ian responsibility and reasonableness of force to examine 
alignment with previous work.

pants’ trait-level attitudes towards police legitimacy were 
measured using a validated (Provenza 2021) short form 
of the Attitudes towards Police Legitimacy Scale (APLS; 
Reynolds et al. 2018). The scale contains 17 items that 
were summed to create a total ( α = 0.99) with higher 
scores indicating more positive attitudes towards the police 
(M = 69.52, SD = 30.00, range: 17–119). We chose the APLS 
instead of other assessments of police legitimacy because 
we were interested in the extent to which people perceive 
the police to be legitimate, not whether the police are, in 
fact, legitimate.
Authoritarianism  Participants’ 
trait-level 
authoritarian 
beliefs were assessed via the Aggression-Submission-Con­
ventionalism (ASC) Scale (Dunwoody and Funke 2016). 
The ASC scale is a validated 18-item measure of authori­
tarianism derived from the three dimensions of the seminal 
Right Wing Authoritarianism scale (Altemeyer 1981, 1988) 
that includes the three original dimensions of authoritarian 
aggression, authoritarian submission, and conventionalism. 
ASC scale has shown greater divergent validity with politi­
cal orientation by including items that are not as strongly 
linked to right-wing attitudes and behaviors (Dunwoody 
and Funke 2016; Torres-Vega et al. 2021). An example item

handcuffing the civilian.
Civilian Action Manipulation
Across all conditions, the officer asked the civilian to exit the 
vehicle, but the civilian refused. The officer then attempted 
to open the driver’s door, but the civilian attempts to keep 
the car door shut. The officer then extracts the civilian from 
the vehicle and attempts to arrest the civilian for obstruc­
tion and delay. Once out of the vehicle, the civilian engages 
in certain noncompliant actions based on the condition. In 
the less noncompliance condition, the civilian attempts to 
flee the scene by running away. In the more noncompliance 
condition, the civilian attempted to flee, but prior to doing 
so, they lunged towards the officer with clenched fists and 
struck them on the shoulder. A pilot study (described in the 
OSM) confirmed that individuals perceived the civilian’s 
actions in the less noncompliant condition to be less force­
ful, threatening, and physically harmful to the officer. 1

Procedure
The study was conducted via Qualtrics. In the informed 
consent, participants were instructed they would hear about 
a brief police UOF scenario and would be asked to answer 
questions about the UOF incident, and complete general 
attitude measures and demographics. Participants were ran­
domly assigned to one of two conditions that varied in civil­
ian action (less noncompliance v. more noncompliance). 
After listening to the first paragraph of the scenario (which 
was consistent across conditions), participants completed 
the initial measures of threat. Next, participants listened to 
the remainder of the scenario description. Following this, 
participants completed their perceptions of reasonable 
force, which were followed by their perceptions of overall 
threat and responsibility in a randomized order. Participants 
then completed the two attitude measures (APLS and ASC) 
in a randomized order. Lastly, participants completed demo­
graphic information, were thanked for their time, and pro­
vided their code for payment.
Results
For each dependent variable, we report hierarchical regres­
sion models where the first step includes only participant 
demographic variables (i.e., sex, race3, age, political orien­
tation, income, and education), the second step includes par­
ticipant demographics and attitudes (i.e., APLS and ASC), 
and the third step includes participants demographics, 
attitudes, and our situational factor of civilian action (less 
noncompliance v. more noncompliance)4. Key findings are 
communicated in-text, and full model statistics are reported 
in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. All analyses were performed using 
the statistical program R (R Core Team, 2024) and its base 
packages. The following additional packages were used to 
restructure and examine the data and create tables: dplyr 
(Wickham et al., 2023), lsr (Navarro 2015), psych (Revelle 
2025), sjPlot (Lüdecke 2024), and sur (Harel 2020).

Dependent Measures
Initial Threat to the Officer and Civilian  Participants’ 
perceptions of the extent to which the officer and the civil­
ian were threatened at the beginning of the encounter prior 
to any civilian noncompliance or officer force was measured 
via one item each. The item was measured on a 9-point scale 
from 1 = strongly disagree to 9 = strongly agree and stated, 
“The officer [civilian] feared for his safety”. Perceptions of 
initial threat to the officer (M = 2.57, SD = 1.97) and threat 
to the civilian (M = 2.85, SD = 2.02) were measured directly 
after the first paragraph of the vignette, which described the 
civilian pulling over to the side of the road after seeing the 
officer’s police lights in his rearview mirror (see OSM for 
full description).
Overall Threat to the Officer and Civilian  Participants’ per­

threatened following exposure to the entirety of the use

9-point scale from 1 = not at all to 9 = completely. The item 
stated, “To what extent do you believe the officer [civilian] 
felt threatened in the situation?” (Mofficer = 5.30, SDofficer 
= 2.50; Mcivilian = 5.44, SDcivilian = 2.50).

In addition to political orientation, participants’ age and 
education level significantly impacted multiple use of force-
related judgments. Older individuals tended to perceive 
more overall threat to the officer, less overall threat to the 
civilian (even when controlling for APLS and ASC), per­
ceived the officer as less responsible, the civilian was more 
responsible, and thought the officer’s force was more rea­
sonable. Further, an individuals’ education level somewhat 
consistently predicted their perceptions. Individuals with 
more formal education perceived both the officer and civil­
ian as more initially threatened (even when controlling for 
police legitimacy attitudes and authoritarian views). How­
ever, participant educational level did not impact percep­
tions of overall threat. It is possible that individuals with 
more formal education have greater knowledge of the threat 
dynamics and potential negative consequences that might 
arise from when a police and civilian make contact, and thus 
perceived both the officer and civilian to be more initially 
threatened in the encounter. However, after exposure to the 
full vignette, the influence of situation-specific information 
(e.g., the civilian actions) was of greater importance to their 
threat perceptions.
Interestingly though, when considering responsibility 
outcomes, individuals with more formal education per­
ceived the officer as more responsible and the civilian as 
less responsible, even when controlling for participant atti­
tudes and the situational influence of civilian action; yet, 
this did not translate to reasonableness judgments. This 
effect is notable, given it was the only demographic char­
acteristic that remained significant across all outcomes 
when controlling for attitudes or a civilians’ actions in the 
encounter. The strong impact of education on use of force 
related judgments aligns with previous work by Braga and 
colleagues (2014) which found that education was the only 
control variable that predicted attitudes, such that those with 
more formal education were more disapproving of an offi­
cer’s force. Future research should further explore the rela­
tionship between perceiver education level and perceptions 
of responsibility in use of force encounters.
Previous research on the impact of age and education on 
public perceptions of police and police use of force has been 
mixed. Our findings suggest that both age and education 
have independent influences on these perceptions, which 
aligns with some work (e.g., Trahan and Russell 2017) but 
contrasts other work that has found no significant differ­
ences based on age (e.g., Mourtgos and Adams 2020; Simon 
et al. 2021) or education (e.g., Johnson and Kuhns 2009). As 
force encounters is important for predicting legal decision-
making, individuals’ likelihood to engage in lawful activ­
ity, and police training. When considering the influence 
of demographics, our findings contrast past work that has 
found a consistent impact of race (e.g., Boudreau et al. 
2019) and income level (e.g., Carter and Corra 2016) on 
police use of force judgments. Thus, findings do not sup­
port H1a-e or H3a-e. One potential explanation for why race 
did not predict individuals’ judgments in the current work is 
the racial composition of our sample. Approximately 75% 
of our sample identified as White, which limited the extent 
of meaningful comparisons we could examine across racial 
groups. We discuss this point further in our limitations sec­
tion. Previous research has also indicated that individuals 
with higher incomes tend to hold more positive perceptions 
of police use of force. Yet, we found no significant impact 
of income on perceptions in our current work. It is possible 
that the impact of income affects more general judgments of 
police (e.g., Braga et al. 2014) or overall approval of force 
(e.g., Mourtgos and Adams 2020) but does not translate to 
more specific perceptions of threat, responsibility, or rea­
sonableness, which were assessed in the current work.
Our results suggest that an individuals’ age, education, 
and political orientation are the most consistent predictors 
of their perceptions of threat, responsibility, and reason­
ableness of force. Supporting H2a-e, participants who were 
more conservative, compared to liberal, perceived the offi­
cer as more threatened (initially and overall), less respon­
sible for the incident, and perceived the officer’s force as 
more reasonable. More conservative individuals also per­
ceived the civilian as less threatened (initially and overall) 
and as more responsible for the incident. These findings are 
aligned with previous work suggesting conservative indi­
viduals have more positive perceptions of police and their 
use of force (e.g., Braga et al. 2014; Cao and Stack 2005; 
Trahan and Russell 2017) and extend this notion to percep­
tions of threat specifically in an officer-civilian encounter, as 
well to perceptions of civilians in these encounters.
Notably, however, the impact of political orientation on 
public judgments was eliminated after accounting for indi­
viduals’ police legitimacy attitudes and authoritarian views. 
This suggests that individuals’ attitudes (i.e., APLS and 
ASC) are more important in predicting their perceptions 
of threat, responsibility and reasonableness in police use 
of force incidents, when compared to their political orien­
tation. It is possible that APLS and ASC attitudes encom­
pass an individuals’ broader political ideological belief 
than political orientation alone, as suggested by Gerber and 
Jackson (2017), and thus once accounted for, the impact 
of political orientation is diminished. Further, the greater 
influence of attitudinal measures compared to demographic 
characteristics on individuals’ legally relevant judgments

participants’ police legitimacy and authoritarian attitudes are 
accounted for (except for the impact of education on responsi­

on their perceptions of threat, responsibility, and reasonable­
ness in use of force encounters, even when controlling for 
a civilian’s specific noncompliant actions in that encounter. 
Therefore, it appears particularly important for scholars and 
criminal justice professionals to assess these attitudes.
Implications
The findings of the present research suggest that attorneys 
in police excessive use of force cases should measure or ask 
questions regarding potential jurors’ attitudes towards police 
legitimacy or authoritarianism during voir dire. Alterna­
tively, it may be wise for judges to provide jurors with addi­
tional instructions that discuss how individuals’ pre-existing 
attitudes regarding police can bias their interpretations of 
police-civilian interactions, particularly perceptions of how 
much threat the officer faced in the encounter. If jurors are 
aware of the possibility that their personal biases can impact 
seemingly objective observations of police-civilian interac­
tions, such as how much the officer was threatened, jurors 
may have a higher likelihood of being able to reduce the 
role of these biases in their legal decisions. Recent work 
on the impact of jury instructions aimed at drawing jurors’ 
attention to personal biases (e.g., implicit racial bias) sug­
gests jurors exposed to such instructions are more likely to 
discuss the issue of their own or others’ bias in their delib­
erations (Lynch et al. 2022).
Aside from a legal decision-making standpoint, these find­
ings may also be used to educate officers on civilian judg­
ments, perhaps through trainings. If police are aware that 
education level increased, participants’ perceptions of the 
officer become more negative and perceptions of the civil­
ian more positive. These findings contrast with various stud­
ies that indicate more formal education is associated with 
more positive perceptions of police (e.g., Simon et al. 2021; 
Trahan and Russell 2017) but align with others that suggest 
the inverse (e.g., Braga et al. 2014; Mourtgos and Adams 
2020). It is possible that the impact of education on percep­
tions depends on whether more general or situation-specific 
judgments are assessed. In other words, some past work has 
assessed use of force judgments in a general, hypothetical 
sense (e.g., “a policeman striking a citizen”; Mourtgos and 
Adams 2020; Trahan and Russell 2017), while the current 
study asked about a specific use of force that included addi­
tional contextual information. Future research should exam­
ine this possibility to better understand the impact of formal 
education on perceptions of police-civilian interactions.
It is also important to note that participant sex (male vs. 
female) was a significant predictor of perceptions of civil­
ian responsibility and reasonable force judgments. Male 
participants thought the civilian was more responsible and 
the officer’s force was more reasonable when controlling for 
participants’ APLS and ASC scores. This trend aligns with 
previous work suggesting men have more positive percep­
tions of police use of force than women (e.g., Johnson and 
Kuhns 2009; Ratcliffe et al. 2015; Simon et al. 2021). Yet, the 
findings are noteworthy such that they suggest other percep­
tions beyond those related to perceptions of threat may vary 
by sex and ultimately impact reasonable force judgments. In 
other words, some scholars (e.g., Bennell et al. 2021) suggest 
threat perceptions are particularly important in judgments of 
whether force is reasonable. Yet, these findings suggest indi­
viduals’ threat perceptions do not vary but sex, but sex still 
impacted their overall perceptions of whether force used was 
reasonable. Future research should explore this to determine 
additional related judgments that may vary by sex, and if these 
effects are dependent on the sex of the officer and civilian.
Beyond demographics, participants’ attitudes towards 
police legitimacy and authoritarianism had a consistent influ­
ence of all judgments within our study (except for authori­
tarian views predicting initial threat), supporting H5a-e and 
H6a-e. The notion that those with more positive attitudes 
towards police legitimacy and more authoritarian views had 
more positive perceptions of the officer, more negative per­
ceptions of the civilian, and were more supportive of the 
officer’s force aligns with previous work (e.g., Celestin and 
Kruschke 2019; Gerber and Jackson 2017). Further, as men­
tioned, these attitudes appear to be more influential to judg­
ments than participants’ demographic characteristics.

Limitations and Future Directions
The current work has several notable limitations. Although 
based on a real-life traffic stop, we used an audio-recorded 
vignette with an accompanying written transcript in our 
study, which may not elicit the same perceptions as viewing 
a police-civilian encounter occurring in real-time or viewing 
a video recording of the encounter. Future researchers should 
examine whether these factors predict public perceptions of 
threat when watching a police use of force incident. Another 
limitation is our use a self-report methodology, particularly 
when assessing attitudes and perceptions of a publicly charged 
topic. Our participant responses may have been influenced by 
social desirability bias or participants’ subjective interpreta­
tions especially given the potentially sensitive nature of the 
judgements assessed (Robinson and Clore 2002). Future 
work should consider the use of social desirability scales, 
such as the Marlowe-Crowne Social Desirability Scale 
(Crowne and Marlowe 1960), to explore the potential impact 
social desirability concerns had on our findings. Lastly, our 
work is limited by our primarily White participant sample.
