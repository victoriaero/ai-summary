Abstract
Objectives. To compare health insurance coverage and access to care by sex and sexual minority status during the COVID-19 pandemic and assess whether lack of insurance hindered access to care by sexual minority status.
Methods. Using Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System data (January 2021–February 2022), we examined differences by sex and sexual orientation among 158 722 adults aged 18 to 64 years living in 34 states. Outcomes were health insurance coverage type and 3 access to care measures.
Results. Sexual minority women were significantly more likely to be uninsured than were heterosexual women, and lack of insurance widened the magnitude of disparity by sexual minority status in all measures of access. Compared with heterosexual men with health insurance, sexual minority men with health insurance were significantly more likely to report being unable to afford necessary care.
Conclusions. During the pandemic, 1 in 8 sexual minority adults living in 34 study states were uninsured. Among sexual minority women, lack of insurance widened inequities in access to care. There were inequities among sexual minority men with health insurance.
Public Health Implications. Sexual minority adults may be disproportionately affected by the unwinding of the COVID-19 public health emergency and may require tailored efforts to mitigate insurance coverage loss. (Am J Public Health. 2024;114(1):118–128. https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2023.307446)
Sexual minorities (i.e., people who identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, or other nonheterosexual identities) have a greater need for, yet fewer opportunities to access, comprehensive health services than do their heterosexual peers.1 In the United States, well-documented health disparities among sexual minorities (e.g., higher rates of mental health disorders, HIV)1 are the products of social and structural stigma,2 but the harms of these conditions may be reduced or rendered manageable with consistent access to appropriate health services.3 Sexual minority adults have historically faced higher rates of lack of insurance and worse access to care because of structural inequities (e.g., homophobia, barriers to marriage and employer-sponsored insurance).4,5 Sexual minorities are also more likely to report trouble finding a provider who is informed, affirming, and sensitive to the unique needs of sexual minority patients.6
However, previous work suggests that among men, disparities in self-rated health by sexual minority status are narrower in states with more comprehensive legal protections.7 More broadly, the health equity promotion model—a framework that builds on minority stress theory and the psychological mediation framework—highlights the role of structural and environmental context on mental and physical health for sexual minorities, and there is evidence in the United States and internationally of the association between structural stigma and health inequities among sexual minorities.1,7–9
Lack of insurance and access to care among sexual minority adults improved after implementation of the 2010 Affordable Care Act (ACA; e.g., individual mandate, Medicaid expansion, nondiscrimination policies of Section 1557)10 and the legalization of same-sex marriage via Obergefell v Hodges in 2015.5,10,11 Provisions of the ACA (i.e., Section 2713) also eliminated patient cost sharing for recommended preventive services, such as preexposure prophylaxis to prevent HIV, greatly reducing financial barriers to appropriate care for sexual minority adults at higher risk for HIV. By some estimates, the rate of lack of insurance among sexual and gender minorities (SGM; which includes transgender and gender nonconforming populations) have steadily increased over time from their low of approximately 8% in 2016.12 Trending increases in lack of insurance parallels that of heterosexual and cisgender populations, likely reflecting the multipronged efforts or the President Donald Trump administration to undermine the ACA.13
Meanwhile, the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated inequities in socioeconomic status, employment, and access to care among marginalized groups in the United States.14,15 There were concerns that given structural inequities, sexual minority adults would bear disproportionate economic burdens (e.g., employment loss) and pandemic-related barriers to care (e.g., health insurance coverage loss).16 Survey-based studies found disparities in economic outcomes, with more SGM respondents reporting job loss and food insecurity in the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic in the United States.17,18 However, no studies to our knowledge have examined health insurance enrollment outcomes for sexual minority adults during the pandemic.15–18
We extended previous research by examining detailed health insurance coverage and access to care by sexual minority status using large-scale and population-based data. Specifically, we examined differences in health insurance coverage type (using newly available multistate data) and access to care by self-reported sex and sexual orientation among adults aged 18 to 64 years. We then assessed differences in access to care by self-reported sex, sexual minority status, and lack of insurance status to further explore whether and how health insurance coverage might hinder access to care by sexual minority status. Lastly, in an exploratory analysis, we examined variation in outcomes by state lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, or queer (LGBTQ) policy environment.
METHODS
We used data from the 2021 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), which collected more than 270 000 adult interviews between January 2021 and February 2022. BRFSS is a nationally representative telephone survey administered annually by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and is used to examine health status and access to care for adults in the United States. In addition to the core questionnaire, states can include optional modules each year.16
Beginning in 2014, BRFSS included an optional module for states to collect data on a respondent’s sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI). States’ inclusion of this module has increased over time, and in 2021 it was used by 34 states (Appendix Table A, available as a supplement to the online version of this article at http://www.ajph.org). Building on previous definitions, we defined an individual as belonging to a sexual minority as one who self-identified as “gay,” “lesbian or gay,” “bisexual,” or “something else” (Appendix Table B, available as a supplement to the online version of this article at http://www.ajph.org).19 Because most individuals aged 65 years and older have Medicare coverage, we excluded them from the main analysis.
Outcomes
Historically, BRFSS has asked respondents whether they had any health insurance coverage (yes/no), and states had the option of asking respondents their primary health insurance type. However, starting in 2021, the main BRFSS questionnaire asked the specific source of health insurance coverage for all respondents. Building on previous work comparing health insurance coverage type, we created 6 health insurance coverage categories: employer-sponsored insurance, marketplace, Medicare (including Medicare and Medigap), Medicaid and Children's Health Insurance Program, other sources of coverage (e.g., Tricare/CHAMPUS, Indian Health Services, state-sponsored health plan, other government plan), and uninsured with no coverage (Appendix Table B).20 We then assessed differences in 3 measures in the BRFSS access to care module: having a personal doctor, having a checkup in the past 2 years, and reporting inability to afford needed care in the past 12 months (Appendix Table B). These survey questions have previously been used to assess differences by sexual orientation.21
Covariates and Statistical Analysis
We estimated descriptive statistics with the Pearson χ2 test to compare distributions by sexual minority status. We then used linear probability regression models to examine differences in our outcomes by sexual orientation (sexual minority vs heterosexual). Because of documented differences in health status and access to care by sex and sexual orientation, we ran all analyses separately for men and women.1,14,22 Specifically, BRFSS asks individuals to self-report their sex (“Are you male or female?”), which we used in our main analyses (Appendix Table B).
For health insurance type outcomes, we adjusted for age, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, household income (measured as a percentage of the federal poverty level according to the US Census),23 marital status, fair or poor self-reported health status, urban versus rural residence, number of chronic conditions (0, 1, 2, or more), and state policies (e.g., Medicaid expansion and LGBTQ policy environments, as we describe later). For the 3 access to care outcomes, we added indicators for unemployment and lack of insurance to our models. Our main findings included estimates adjusted for the variables specified previously, consistent with our a priori conceptualization (for more detail, we provide stepwise regression estimates in Appendix Tables C and D, available as a supplement to the online version of this article at http://www.ajph.org).
To measure household income as a percentage of federal poverty level, we used a previous method based on the midpoint of household income responses and household sizes.23 We linked BRFSS data with publicly available state data on Medicaid expansion status and LGBTQ state policy environment, which we measured as a state-level index score of policies related to SGM equality (e.g., related to relationship and parental recognition, nondiscrimination, religious exemptions, SGM youths, health care, criminal justice, and gender identity document changes).24 The Movement Advancement Project, an independent nonprofit think tank that examines changes in state-level LGBTQ laws and policies, developed the index, which groups states into 1 of 5 categories: negative, low, fair, medium, and high.24,25 We used data from June 2021, the middle of our study period, to categorize states’ LGBTQ policy environments (Appendix Table A).
To assess whether access to care differed by sex, sexual minority status, and health insurance status, we added a 2-way interaction between sexual minority status and lack of insurance (sexual minority × lack of insurance) to our model. The magnitude, direction, and statistical significance of the interaction term indicated whether there were different associations by sexual minority status and lack of insurance to care access. In an exploratory analysis evaluating whether access to care differed by a state’s LGBTQ policy environment, we employed similar methods (i.e., adding a 2-way interaction between sexual minority × state LGBTQ policy environment to our model), in which states with negative LGBTQ policy environments were the reference group.
Sensitivity Analyses
We tested the robustness of our main analysis by conducting multiple sensitivity analyses. First, we determined differences for other types of health insurance. Second, we determined sample characteristics, unadjusted differences, and adjusted differences by different sexual orientation categories (e.g., separately for gay or lesbian, bisexual, and individuals reporting something else). Third, we excluded adults for whom we imputed household income from our analysis. Fourth, we used a logistic regression model.
We performed all analyses using Stata version 18 (Statacorp, College Park, TX).
RESULTS
Our study sample included 12 576 sexual minority (7652 women, 4924 men) and 146 146 heterosexual (74 369 women, 71 777 men) adults aged 18 to 64 years living in 34 states (Table 1). Sexual minorities were more likely to be younger, be unmarried, report household income below the federal poverty level, and describe their health as fair or poor (instead of excellent, very good, or good) compared with their heterosexual peers.

Health Insurance Coverage Type
In unadjusted analyses, sexual minority women (42.3%) were nearly 12 percentage points (PPs) less likely to report employer-sponsored coverage than were heterosexual women (54.1%; P < .001; Table 2; Appendix Table C, available as a supplement to the online version of this article at http://www.ajph.org). Sexual minority women were significantly more likely to report Medicaid coverage (17.3% vs 11.0%; P < .001), other health insurance (9.8% vs 8.3%; P = .005), or being uninsured (13.1% vs 8.6%; P < .001) than were heterosexual women (Table 2). Sexual minority men (46.0%) were significantly less likely to report employer-sponsored insurance compared with heterosexual men (55.9%; P < .001; Table 2; Appendix Table D, available as a supplement to the online version of this article at http://www.ajph.org). Sexual minority men were significantly more likely to report Medicaid coverage (9.1% vs 5.8%; P < .001) or other health insurance (11.1% vs 9.4%; P = .03) than were heterosexual men.

Access to Care
In adjusted analyses, sexual minority women reported worse access to care on all measures relative to heterosexual women: they were less likely to report having a personal doctor (−3.4 PP; 95% confidence interval [CI] = −5.1, −1.7; P < .001) or report a checkup in the past 2 years (−2.6 PP; 95% CI = −4.2, −0.9; P = .002). Sexual minority women were also significantly more likely to report being unable to afford medical care than were heterosexual women (6.8 PP; 95% CI = 5.0, 8.5; P < .001). Although sexual minority men were significantly more likely to report having a personal doctor (3.6 PP; 95% CI = 1.4, 5.9; P = .002), they were also significantly more likely to report not being able to afford medical care (5.0 PP; 95% CI = 2.9, 7.1; P < .001) in adjusted models.
Access to Care by Lack of Insurance
Barriers to care were significantly larger for uninsured sexual minority women than insured sexual minority women (as indicated by the P values in Table 3). The magnitude of disparity between sexual minority and heterosexual women who reported a personal doctor was significantly greater for those who were uninsured (−12.0 PP; 95% CI = −19.0, −5.0; P < .001) compared with those with insurance (−2.1 PP; 95% CI = −3.7, −0.5; P = .007 for the interaction between sexual orientation and lack of insurance). Similarly, lack of insurance widened the magnitude of disparity for reporting a checkup in the past 2 years between sexual minority and heterosexual women (−15.9 PP; 95% CI = −27.3, −4.5 for uninsured vs −0.9 PP; 95% CI = −1.8, −0.0 for insured individuals; P = .01 for interaction).

Differences by State Policy
For women, disparities in the ability to afford medical care between sexual minority and heterosexual women were significantly narrower in states with high (i.e., more inclusive) policy environments than in states with negative LGBTQ policy environments. For men, disparities in the ability to afford medical care between sexual minority and heterosexual men were significantly narrower in states with more inclusive LGBTQ policy environments (low, fair, medium, or high) when compared with those with negative LGBTQ policy environments (Table 4).

Sensitivity Analyses
Stepwise regression estimates suggest that after accounting for age, race/ethnicity, marital status, and educational attainment, the distinctions between sexual minority and heterosexual adults became less prominent (Appendix Tables C and D). We determined variation across all health insurance coverage types (Appendix Table E, available as a supplement to the online version of this article at http://www.ajph.org) and disaggregated estimates by sexual orientation (Appendix Tables F–I, available as a supplement to the online version of this article at http://www.ajph.org). Our findings were relatively unaffected by other model specifications (Appendix Table J, available as a supplement to the online version of this article at http://www.ajph.org).
DISCUSSION
Using data from 34 states, we found wide variation in health insurance coverage type and access to care by sex and sexual minority status between January 2021 and February 2022. Compared with heterosexual peers, sexual minority women were significantly more likely to be uninsured, and both sexual minority men and women were significantly less likely to report employer-sponsored coverage. Sexual minority women reported less access to care on all metrics than did heterosexual women. Sexual minority men were more likely to report having a personal doctor but more likely to report being unable to afford medical care compared with heterosexual men. Analyses stratified by sex indicated that access to care differed by sexual minority status and lack of insurance for certain outcomes. For both men and women, residence in states with the most inclusive LGBTQ policy environments narrowed disparities in ability to afford necessary medical care by sexual minority status when compared with states with negative LGBTQ policy environments.
Our results are consistent with previous studies that have identified inequities in other access to care measures (e.g., no usual source of care) by sex and sexual minority status, and our results support evidence that sexual minority adults are more likely to delay medical care because of cost than are heterosexual adults.6,11,19,21,22 A previous study assessed differences by sexual minority and lack of insurance status using 2014–2017 BRFSS data.21 Compared with these previous estimates—which did not stratify by sex—our findings identified wider inequities in ability to afford medical care by sexual minority status during the pandemic and that there was variation by sex and sexual minority status.21 These findings suggest that insurance status is an important driver of access to care for sexual minority populations, with substantial disparities in access for those without insurance.
Strategies that promote insurance coverage (e.g., targeted enrollment assistance) may need to be implemented, and policies that threaten insurance coverage (e.g., Medicaid unwinding) should be carefully monitored. Previous work has assessed the association between state LGBTQ policy environment and self-reported health status, and our exploratory analyses extend this work by assessing its association with access to care.2,7
Our study has several implications for health equity policy and practice. We identified new disparities in health insurance coverage and potential underinsurance (as measured by financial barriers to care despite having health insurance) by sexual minority status. Recent evidence suggests that, despite substantially fewer people lacking insurance beginning in 2014, lack of insurance rates have gradually increased for adults since their lowest point in 2016.12 Although lack of insurance rates remain lower than rates before the ACA, about 1 in 8 nonelderly sexual minority adult remained uninsured in 2021 compared with 1 in 10 heterosexual adults.12
For sexual minority women, lack of insurance widened existing inequities in having a personal doctor and having a checkup in the previous 2 years, illustrating the unique disparities faced by sexual minority women (e.g., structural-level inequalities related to sexism). However, we found significant differences in ability to afford medical care between sexual minority and heterosexual men among those with health insurance, suggesting that some sexual minority adults may be underinsured. These findings are supported by a recent study suggesting that sexual minority adults are more likely to report money-saving behaviors regarding medications (e.g., skipping medication doses, delaying prescription refills).26 Future work should examine the role of health insurance benefit design (e.g., high-deductible plans) on inequities in access to care for sexual minorities.
Our study provides new evidence about inequities by sexual minority status during the COVID-19 pandemic. Historically, sexual minority adults have borne the burden of structural inequities, including higher rates of economic hardship, such as poverty and experiencing homelessness.27 Consequently, there were concerns that sexual minority adults would be disproportionately affected by the pandemic.15 Some studies have examined health and health care inequities faced by SGM adults in the pandemic: early in the pandemic, more than 13% of SGM adults reported health insurance loss.28 Apart from a few states (e.g., California and Pennsylvania), there was limited systematic SOGI data collection to understand the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. In July 2021, the US Census Bureau began ascertaining SOGI information in the Household Pulse Survey and demonstrated that SGM adults experienced substantially higher rates of financial precarity, including food insecurity and loss of employment and income, than did non-SGM adults.17,18 Additional examination of the long-term impacts of the pandemic on sexual minority adults—including health insurance loss, delays in care, and mental health outcomes—remains critical.14
Beginning in March 2020, through COVID-19 emergency declarations and congressional legislation, states were required to provide continuous enrollment for individuals enrolled in Medicaid. However, Medicaid continuous coverage protections ended on March 31, 2023, and states were permitted to resume Medicaid disenrollments.29,30 Sexual minority adults—who reported higher rates of Medicaid coverage than heterosexual adults—may be disproportionately affected by the Medicaid continuous enrollment provision ending. Tailored outreach and enrollment assistance to sexual minority adults may be needed to mitigate lapses in health insurance coverage, retain coverage for individuals who remain Medicaid eligible, and transition individuals who are no longer Medicaid eligible to other forms of health insurance coverage.
More broadly, our findings underscore the importance of developing policies that protect and improve the well-being of all vulnerable populations—including SGM populations. Our study used data from 34 states that opted to collect SOGI data. Comprehensive data collection that follows best practices for requiring SOGI data collection is a critical first step for understanding the needs of SGM communities and using reliable SOGI-based information to develop targeted state and federal policies.31 Inequities in access to care are largely the product of discrimination and other structural barriers to SGM health equity. Nondiscrimination protections for SGM adults may play a crucial role in mitigating inequities in access to care. For example, the August 2022 proposed revision to Section 1557 of the ACA reinstates the prohibition of discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity on health insurance benefit design and insurer marketing practices.32
Lastly, public policies may play a critical role in reducing health inequities. Should the 10 states that have not yet expanded Medicaid do so, it could reduce insurance coverage inequities for low-income sexual minority adults. Social policies that codify equality by sexual orientation or gender identity may particularly increase financial security and mental well-being.8 For instance, the December 2022 passage of the Respect for Marriage Act is a positive step forward.33 Additional policies, such as passage of the federal Equality Act—which would prohibit discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity in education, housing, public accommodations, employment, and other federally funded programs—could further advance equity.34 Efforts to lessen unemployment, in particular, might facilitate the availability of employer-sponsored insurance and decrease income-related delays in care. In the meantime, more evidence is required to understand how structural discrimination creates or ameliorates disparities in health insurance coverage and access to care for SGM population.
Limitations
Our study has several limitations. First, the BRFSS response rate was 44%, which is lower than other federal surveys such as the American Community Survey.35 Second, because the SOGI module is optional, it includes only 34 states, so the sample may not be nationally representative. However, the SOGI module was used by more states in 2021 than any previous wave of BRFSS and contains the most recent data available. Relatedly, this study is not generalizable to sexual minorities in other countries. Third, it is possible that our approach to measuring household income is imprecise, but we followed previously validated methods.24 Fourth, health insurance coverage type was self-reported and may have been subject to recall and misreporting biases. Respondents in our sample may have had multiple forms of health insurance coverage, but BRFSS requires respondents to identity their primary source of coverage.
Fifth, although previous work suggests that state policy environments affect health outcomes for sexual minority adults, it is possible that the significant relationship between state LGBTQ policy environments and affordability of care is spurious or possibly attributable to other state-level characteristics.2,7 Future work should directly test the hypothesis of this potential relationship. Relatedly, state LGBTQ policy environments can change over time. Therefore, our results may differ using different points in time. Sixth, we were unable to further examine mechanisms driving disparities in access to care. For our outcome examining ability to afford care, we were unable to disentangle whether certain types of care (e.g., mental health care, prescription medications) were particularly challenging.
Conclusions
We identified inequities in health insurance coverage and access to care by sex and sexual minority status during the COVID-19 pandemic. To prevent disparities from widening following the decline of the COVID-19 public health emergency, tailored outreach and enrollment assistance to sexual minority adults may be needed.
