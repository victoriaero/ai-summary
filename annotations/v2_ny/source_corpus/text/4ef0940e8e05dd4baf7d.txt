The United States has the largest immigrant population of any country in the world with about 45 million people born outside of the country (Budiman et al., 2020). Hispanic immigrants make up more than 44% of all immigrants, followed by non-Hispanic Asian (27%), non-Hispanic White (18%), and non-Hispanic Black (9%) immigrants. Immigrant and native populations in the United States use public benefits at different rates and levels. The overall participation in a broad range of safety net programs is higher among immigrant-headed households than nonimmigrant-headed households, and the difference is primarily attributable to their higher use of noncash programs such as Medicaid and food stamps. In 2012, for example, immigrant households had 19 and 18 percentage point higher participation rates, respectively, in these two programs, whereas the use of cash supports (e.g., Supplemental Security Income [SSI], Temporary Assistance for Needy Families) was only marginally higher for immigrants (Camarota, 2015). Looking at trends over the 1995–2018 period and focusing on low-educated households with children, another study finds that the overall use of public benefits is consistently higher for immigrants, roughly varying between 5 percentage points in late 1990s to over 10 percentage points in more recent years (Huang, 2021). Excluding healthcare benefits (e.g., Medicaid and State Children’s Health Insurance Program), however, low-educated immigrant households with children had generally lower use of public benefits that their nonimmigrant peers since late 1990s, with a decline in difference around the time of the Great Recession and another increase in more recent years. Although important, these numbers do not account for compositional differences between immigrants and nonimmigrants in the United States, in particular as it relates to their socioeconomic and health profiles, among others (e.g. Bitler and Hoynes, 2013; Borjas, 2011). Recent research aimed at addressing this shortcoming finds that if immigrants had the same sociodemographic profile as nonimmigrants, that is, assuming no difference in their age, racial/ethnic and gender composition, relationship status, and educational attainment, among others, their participation in public benefit programs would have been sharply lower and well below that of US-born individuals (Huang, et al., 2021).
A critical factor shaping the use of public benefits by immigrants are policies and regulations governing their eligibility for various programs and possible consequences that their use may have for immigrants’ current and future immigration status in the United States. For example, in recent years, research has documented that an increasing share of immigrants in the United States has been avoiding applying for and enrolling in various public benefit programs out of fear that it may affect their immigration status (Bernstein et al., 2019), even in the face of increased need for support during the COVID-19 pandemic (Bernstein et al., 2021). This research has linked the observed trend with the impact of the expansion of the “public charge” rule, first proposed in 2018, implemented in early 2020, and halted a year later. Under this policy, noncash public benefits, such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP; i.e., food stamps) or Medicaid, could be considered by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services when determining whether applications for permanent residency or temporary visas should be granted or denied. Although the rule itself was limited in scope and lasted a relatively limited period, data suggest that it may have had a lasting impact on discouraging immigrants and their families from applying for various benefits that they qualify for, and the chilling effects were observed even among those who already were naturalized citizens or legal permanent residents. Prior changes to policies affecting immigrants’ use of public benefits, such as the welfare reform implemented in 1996 and changes to the definition of public charge in 1999, seem to have also had a noticeable impact on the use of public benefits by immigrants and their families (e.g., Fix and Passel, 1999).
Beyond policies determining immigrants’ eligibility for various public supports, there are other personal and structural factors that lead to differences in immigrants’ receipt of public transfers relative to nonimmigrants. As immigrants are unfamiliar with the social support system in the United States and may have a more limited social network, they could be less likely to apply for public benefits that their US-born peers. Furthermore, those with limited English language proficiency could face barriers in access not faced by native language speakers. Finally, to the extent that immigrants may be self-selected on good health, this could result in their lower use of public supports, especially those directly linked with poor health.
Although differences in the use of public benefits between US-born and immigrant populations have been examined extensively, there has been very little focus on older adults. There are reasons to believe, however, that the experience of older immigrants may be at least somewhat unique. On one hand, many of them have spent a large part of their lives in the United States and are more likely to be naturalized or to have otherwise regulated their immigration status. On the other hand, many older immigrants may have been exposed to years of working in hazardous environments and physically demanding jobs, which would have likely eroded any possible health advantage they may have enjoyed at the time of immigration. Indeed, an earlier study that examined older immigrants found that following the welfare reform in the 1990s, they did not experience a decline in welfare use, unlike other groups of immigrants (Fix and Passel, 1999).
However, it is unclear whether subsequent policy reforms, and especially those enacted in recent years, would lead to different outcomes for older immigrants than younger immigrants. Furthermore, recent policy changes seem to have disproportionately adversely affected non- European, and especially Latin American origin, immigrants (Lowrey and Van Hook, 2022).
Trends in Public Transfer Receipt for the US Population by Nativity
In the context of these trends and the arguably unique circumstances that may be facing older immigrants, we assessed whether older immigrants in the United States exhibit differences in the receipt of public supports relative to native-born US older adults consistent with that observed for the broader population of immigrants, whether and how these differences changed over the past two decades, and whether there are differences in the results for Hispanic immigrants relative to non-Hispanic immigrants. To do so, we analyzed data from the 1998–2020 waves of the Health and Retirement Study, a nationally representative biennial longitudinal survey of the US population over the age of 50.
Results shown in Figure 1 suggest that older immigrants are much less likely than their US-born peers to receive Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) benefits, and they are somewhat less likely to receive various cash transfers. However, older immigrants have traditionally been either more likely (among Hispanics) or similarly likely (among non-Hispanics) to receive food stamps, that is, SNAP benefits. Over time, however, there has been a clear trend toward older immigrants being increasingly less likely to receive public support relative to US-born older adults, with the trend already noticeable during the 2010–2016 Health and Retirement Study survey waves and accelerating during the 2018–2020 survey waves, roughly corresponding to the Obama and Trump administrations, respectively. The magnitude of the observed differences is largest for SSDI benefits, followed by food stamps and, albeit substantially less so, various cash transfers. The differences tend to be larger for Hispanics than non-Hispanics, and they have also been subject to a more dramatic drop-off in the likelihood of receipt of various benefits relative to those born in the United States.
Discussion
Immigration is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon involving economic, demographic, political, and cultural aspects, among others. Focusing on its economic aspects, the country of immigration undoubtedly receives important economic benefits in the form of additional workforce, human capital and know-how, additional tax payments, and government safety-net program contributions. Yet it inevitably also has increased outlays for public benefits that support the residents of the country of immigration in times of need.
In this article, we aimed to describe the trends in the receipt of public support for older immigrants in the United States and document any potential systemic change that may have happened over the previous two decades. In contrast to findings for general population, we found that older immigrants mostly receive fewer benefits than nonimmigrants, even without accounting for differences in their socioeconomic and health profiles. For older non-Hispanic immigrants, this is true regardless of the type of public benefit over the whole observed period (i.e., since 1998), whereas for Hispanics, one noticeable exception is the use of food stamps, which was higher for immigrants than nonimmigrants prior to 2018. Similar to trends observed for younger immigrants, we find a long-term trend of declining use of public benefits by older immigrants relative to nonimmigrants, with arguably an accelerated pace during the Trump administration. The magnitude of this decline is much more pronounced for Hispanic than non- Hispanic immigrants.
These results extend our knowledge about the recent developments in public benefits use by older immigrants in the United States. They have important policy implications as the debate continues about possible future efforts at immigration reforms and, more broadly, the role of immigrants in our economy and society. The study findings suggest that, as they age, older immigrants are not a disproportionate burden on the welfare system of our country. Nonetheless, much more work remains to be done to assess fully the economic costs and benefits of immigration over the life course.
