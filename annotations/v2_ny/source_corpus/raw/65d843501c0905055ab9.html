Access & Affordability

Why are fewer white students attending college?

Higher education officials have been gearing up for demographic changes that will lead to lower enrollment at less-selective colleges, and the number of white college students appears to be “plummeting the fastest,” according to federal data, experts, and analysis by The Chronicle of Higher Education. Between 2012 to 2022, enrollment of white undergraduate students has fallen more than that of any other racial group—findings that have surprised many colleges and demographers. 

​​“White enrollments were declining even before the pandemic across all sectors,” Doug Shapiro, executive director of the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center tells the Chronicle. “We didn’t really notice it until the last couple of years. When the pandemic started in 2020, we were focused on race and ethnicity and the large declines in Black and Hispanic enrollments,” particularly at two-year community colleges. 

Now, few higher education institutions seem prepared to recruit more white students while remedying racial inequities for students of color, who may soon make up the majority of their student population, The Chronicle says. 

A surprise in the data

Colleges already are facing other factors that may dampen college attendance. Experts predict that, in the next year or two, many campuses will face an enrollment cliff: a substantial decline in enrollment of traditional-age college students, as the college age population shrinks for five to 10 years. 

The COVID-19 pandemic also dealt a serious blow to overall enrollment. Additionally, the Supreme Court’s ruling ending race-conscious admissions and legal challenges to race-based scholarships and diversity, equity, and inclusion efforts have left education officials struggling to find legal ways of boosting college enrollment among students of color.

Some selective colleges have seen declines in enrollment among Black and Latine students since the end of race-conscious admissions. However, for less-selective institutions (colleges that accept at least 75% of their applicants, do not consider the race of their applicants, and enroll more than half the nation’s students), the most significant enrollment declines have been among white students. The data has seemed counterintuitive: white college students tend to come from wealthier families that can afford a college education, compared to students of color, who face various obstacles to enrollment, including economic disparities, academic attainment gaps in K-12 education, and bias in the classroom.

What is causing this decline?

Rising college costs and decent-paying jobs that don’t require a college degree have been significant factors in drawing white students away from higher education, even as research shows that a college education is a good investment. Rising skepticism also has played a role: a November 2023 Chronicle survey found that 56% of non-white respondents said colleges did an “excellent” or “very good” job educating students, while only 31% of white respondents said the same. White respondents were also twice as likely to say colleges did a “not so good” or poor job. 

Those forces have contributed to a decade-long enrollment decline among white students. Between 2012 and 2022, white student enrollment dropped 22%, or from 9 million to 7 million students across all higher education sectors, slightly outpacing Black student enrollment, which fell by 19%. In comparison, Asian student enrollment increased by 2%, and Latine student enrollment rose by 31%. 

Looking at the undergraduate enrollment share by race and ethnicity, white student enrollment fell from 47% of undergraduate students in 2018 to 41% in 2023, according to data from the Clearinghouse Research Center. In Fall 2023, there was a slight increase in overall undergraduate enrollment, but around 80% came from Black, Latine, and Asian students, while white student enrollment fell 2%, says Shapiro.    

Among high school students younger than 18 who are enrolled in college courses through early college or dual enrollment programs, white enrollment has fallen by 6% since 2018, just as overall enrollment among that age group has surged by 23%, the Chronicle says. Although experts have noted that these programs are less accessible to students from underrepresented communities, much of the enrollment increase from 2018 to 2023 has been due to growth in Black, Latine, and Asian student participation. The difference in enrollment trends may also reflect the fact that while some white adults, especially men, are more likely to get a good job without a college degree, adults of color have had to get more education to get the same jobs, experts tell The Chronicle.

The impact

In recent months, these enrollment declines have forced some majority-white colleges to lay off employees; close some departments; or in extreme cases, to shut down campuses altogether. Colleges and universities significantly impacted by white student enrollment declines will have to learn why those families are not sending their kids to college and then make college an attractive option for them, experts tell the Chronicle. Those efforts may include recruiting more students from small towns and rural areas and launching new programs in popular job-related fields, even as they work to recruit and retain students of color.
