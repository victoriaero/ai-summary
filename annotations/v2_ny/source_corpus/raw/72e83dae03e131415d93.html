Abstract

Background

LGBTQ + communities in the United States disproportionately experience policing practices that involve high officer discretion, contributing to wide-ranging health disparities. These interactions, often involving low-level offenses or perceived violations, introduce significant subjectivity that can reinforce racial, gender, and sexual bias. While the broader health effects of criminal-legal involvement have been studied, the specific public health consequences of these discretionary enforcement practices remain underexplored, particularly among LGBTQ + populations.​.

Methods

Guided by Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR, we first submitted an Open Science Framework protocol, then we searched six databases for peer-reviewed empirical studies examining the health impacts of police discretion on LGBTQ + populations in the contiguous United States. Because the term “discretionary arrest” is rarely used in published literature, studies were included if discretion was described in participant narratives or author analysis. Studies were analyzed through a holistic health lens encompassing physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual well-being.

Results

Four empirical studies met inclusion criteria. Thematic synthesis revealed eight cross-cutting themes: (1) health impacts from policing, (2) racialized enforcement patterns, (3) discretion in arrest outcomes, (4) criminalization of gender expression and identity, (5) sexual risk and surveillance, (6) social withdrawal and isolation as coping, (7) mistrust in public systems, and (8) intersectional marginalization. Black, Latine, transgender, and gender-expansive individuals were most frequently targeted and harmed.​.

Conclusions

Officer discretion in the decision to arrest functions as a structural determinant of health and a key target for policy and intervention. Findings underscore the urgent need for community-led, trauma-informed, LGBTQ+-affirming interventions. With only four studies meeting inclusion, interpretations should remain cautious and context-specific. Community-academic partnerships can prioritize harm reduction and guide structurally grounded health-justice interventions that curb discretionary arrest and improve access to LGBTQ+affirming care.

In 2007, the Texas state legislature gave agencies greater discretion in the decision to arrest, allowing them to cite-and-release on certain misdemeanor charges (Texas Code of Criminal Procedure, Chap. 14, Article 14.06, Subsections ((Texas a-d)). Cite-and-release policies allow police officers to issue a citation along with a summons to appear in court as an alternative to arrest (Meuris, 2023). In June 2021, officials in Tarrant County, Texas, the third-largest county in the state, announced their decision to begin implementing Cite-and-Release. While this policy was widely praised by local police chiefs as a way of reducing the burden of arrest on both citizens and officers (Fort Worth Business Press Staff, 2021), similar policies have been unevenly adopted across the state and there is limited data on their use (Carreon, 2023). This leaves community stakeholders with limited guidance for adopting, implementing, or evaluating cite-and-release policies or other local interventions to reduce discretionary arrests.

This review emerged as part of an NIH-funded project aimed at reducing discretionary arrests for three highly policed populations in Tarrant County, Texas (Black, Latine/immigrant, and LGBTQ+). Drawing on the Texas Cite & Release law as a means of operationalizing “discretionary arrests,” this project sought to develop a structural intervention at the point of community-police contact to reduce entry into the community criminal-legal system. Early in this community organization-led structural intervention project, community stakeholders and co-investigators raised concerns about developing a single intervention strategy for Black, Latine/immigrant, and LGBTQ + communities. They emphasized that a one-size-fits-all approach risked overlooking significant differences: What if the health outcomes associated with discretionary enforcement vary significantly by group? What if the evidence base is stronger for one population than another? These discussions prompted the team to conduct a focused scoping review to map the literature specific to each target population and apply findings to inform structural intervention outcomes, indicators, and strategies. While the termination of federal funding precluded the development of the structural intervention originally planned, the scoping review provides a foundational evidence base that can inform structurally grounded health equity efforts across jurisdictions.

Extending beyond the initial scope of this project, the purpose of this scoping review is to provide community stakeholders with an evidence base for advocating for the adoption, implementation, or evaluation of policies that promote or protect the health of LGBTQ + communities in discretionary arrests. This review does not attempt to assign causal interpretations to police discretion, recognizing the challenges of disentangling the impact of other aspects of arrest encounters (e.g., the use of force) or subsequent interactions within the criminal-legal system. This scoping review is limited in scope to health impacts associated with “discretionary arrests” to preserve relevance for community stakeholders and their sphere of influence, which includes local interventions such as cite-and-release policies or diversion programs. Other policies that seek to influence discretionary arrests tend to exist at the state level, such as reclassification of offenses, decriminalization, generally involve a change in state policies and are highly unlikely in very “law-and-order” states. Policies that influence prosecutorial discretion, while they may be local, are more “downstream” in criminal legal system involvement, so policies that promote “cite-and-release” enforcement or diversion programs are more focused on preventing entry into the criminal legal system.

Background

With a population of over 2.1 million and significant racial/ethnic diversity (U.S. Census Bureau, United 2020), Tarrant County exemplifies the structural conditions—legal ambiguity, limited protections, and weak accountability—that shape discretionary policing. The 2009 police raid of the Rainbow Lounge, a Fort Worth gay bar, remains a painful emblem of these dynamics (Starling, 2023) and the intersecting identities within communities that face disproportionate rates of arrest and incarceration (Morrison, 2019); Prison Policy Initiative, (Prison n.d.)).

Historically, LGBTQ + communities have been criminalized and subjected to discriminatory policing. The Stonewall Uprising of 1969, led by Marsha P. Johnson and Silvia Rivera—two transgender and gender non-conforming people of color, one Black, and one Latina—marked a pivotal moment of resistance to violence and discrimination at the hands of police, giving rise to what we now know as the Pride Movement (Varga et al., 2019; Tandon & Rao, 2021; Ashley & Sanchinel, 2023). Marsha P. Johnson died under suspicious circumstances in 1992, and Rivera passed away from liver cancer in 2002. Their life spans—46 and 50 years, respectively—contrast starkly with the national life expectancies at those times (75.8 in 1992 and 77 in 2002), illustrating the compounding vulnerabilities faced by marginalized trans leaders within broader contexts of violence and neglect (Dattani et al., 2023; Osorio, 2017). These outcomes cannot be fully understood through biomedical outcomes alone; they are shaped by the criminalization of identity, institutional violence, and the ongoing structural conditions that produce premature death and public health inequities of Black, Indigenous, and people of color LGBTQ + communities (Trinh et al., 2017; Wirtz et al., 2020). Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer/questioning+ (LGBTQ+) communities in the United States (U.S.) are at elevated risk of being targeted by police and facing discriminatory treatment, including elevated rates of arrest (Schwartz et al., 2022).

LGBTQ communities are more likely than their non-LGBTQ + counterparts (21% vs. 14.6%) to experience police-initiated contact such as stop, search, and arrest (Grasso et al., 2024). Anti-LGBTQ discrimination is associated with a wide range of disparities along the continuum of criminal-legal involvement including unequal enforcement, sentencing disparities, violence, and mortality (Ritchie & Jones-Brown, 2017; Girardi, 2022). Beyond police-initiated contact, disparities span the full continuum of criminal-legal involvement—arrest, incarceration, probation, and parole—reflecting both differential exposure and differential treatment across systems (Meyer et al., 2017; Prison Policy Initiative, n.d.). Importantly, sex-specific patterns are pronounced: gaps between sexual-minority and heterosexual women in criminal-legal involvement are often larger than the gaps among men, underscoring the need to disaggregate by sex and gender identity (Meyer et al., 2017).

These disparities are not only products of moment-of-contact bias, but also of life-course structural disadvantages—for example, family rejection leading to being unhoused, a factor that elevates police exposure—and higher victimization, both of which increase criminal-legal contact for LGBTQ populations (Flores et al., 2020). Vogler’s Queer Pathways Framework synthesizes these dynamics, showing how cumulative marginalization channels LGBTQ people into greater criminal-legal entanglement over time (Vogler, 2025). For example, transgender people are at seven times greater risk of experiencing unjustified police contact, violence, and harassment even when compared with their cisgender LGBQ + counterparts, (Stenersen et al., 2022). This risk is magnified when paired with other intersections such as race and socioeconomic status (Stenersen et al., 2022). Sexual minorities were stopped more often and at younger ages than heterosexual peers (Schwartz et al., 2022). These encounters contribute to a cascade of physical, psychological, social, and economic harms (Gaynor & Blessett, 2022; Reimer et al., 2021; Cox & Malkin, 2023), all of which reinforce institutional mistrust (Shields, 2021) and deepen marginalization (Gaynor & Blessett, 2022).

Among the most harmful yet underexamined practices are arrests that rely on officer discretion. Scoping reviews are uniquely suited to address emerging and complex research questions (San et al., 2017). In this case, such an approach enables a systematic synthesis of a fragmented evidence base, capturing empirical data across disciplines and study designs. By centering these multidimensional impacts, the review contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how discretionary policing operates as a structural determinant of health.

Despite these patterns, data collection on sexual orientation and gender identity and expression (SOGIE) remains woefully incomplete across policing systems, making it difficult to assess the true scope of disparities. While the broader health impacts of criminal-legal involvement have been documented (Malika et al., 2022), most empirical studies treat arrest as a binary variable, present or not, rather than distinguishing among various discretionary enforcement practices. This gap obscures how subjective enforcement decisions may uniquely function as a mechanism of marginalization (Huff, 2021). Given the conceptual ambiguity surrounding arrests involving officer discretion and the limited, multidisciplinary nature of existing studies, a scoping review offers an appropriate strategy for mapping the current evidence.

Rationale

While the health impacts of arrests and incarceration have been studied (Malika et al., 2022; Hereth, 2023), less is known about the health impacts of arrest decisions that rely on officer discretion, particularly on LGBTQ + communities. Although the vast majority of arrests in the U.S. involve officer discretion (Del Pozo et al., 2021; Pozo et al., 2024); Neusteter & O’Toole, 2019), the term ‘discretionary arrest,’ those arrests based on officer judgment rather than legal mandate, is rarely used in scholarly literature; however, research on officer discretion is robust and foundational to understanding the unequal enforcement patterns explored here (Huff, 2021; Chenane et al., 2020). In this work we use the term discretionary arrest intentionally to foreground the subjective nature of these enforcement decisions, which often reinforce bias, enable discriminatory policing, and undermine both public health and public safety (Huff, 2021). While the broader public health impacts of criminal-legal involvement have been studied, the specific health impacts of officer discretion, particularly as it affects LGBTQ + individuals, remain underexplored and under-theorized. We distinguish discretion (latitude permitted by statute or policy) from discrimination (biased or unequal application of law). Discretion can enable discrimination, but the terms are not synonymous; this review focuses on health impacts associated with the arrest decision when it is non-mandated and contingent on officer judgment.

Because most arrests in the U.S. fall into this category, it is essential to understand the dynamics of discretionary enforcement and their impacts on the well-being of sexual and gender minoritized communities (Restar, 2023). This scoping review will consolidate all known empirical information so that researchers may better understand the impacts of this type of arrest. Two complementary framings help clarify potential mechanisms. First, an endowment effect: LGBTQ people experience higher rates of criminal-legal contact, and contact is broadly harmful to health; therefore, disparities in exposure compound health inequities (Schwartz et al., 2022; Parker et al., 2020). Second, a coefficient effect: the marginal health harm per unit of contact may be greater for LGBTQ people due to increased mistreatment during encounters, fewer buffering supports, and higher baseline vulnerability for certain outcomes (e.g., HIV, anxiety/depression) (Flores et al., 2020; Parker et al., 2020). These framings situate discretionary arrest within a wider ecology of risk and motivate a focus on upstream enforcement decisions.

Objective

This scoping review aimed to systematically identify and synthesize the empirical evidence on the health impacts of arrest decisions made at the discretion of police officers, rather than mandated by law, on LGBTQ + communities in the contiguous U.S. Discretionary arrests reflect officer subjectivity and are a key mechanism by which bias in policing may be enacted, yet they remain underexamined in public health literature. By mapping empirical findings related to LGBTQ + individuals, this review provides foundational insight for community-academic partnerships seeking to reduce health harms associated with police interactions in marginalized communities through structurally responsive interventions.

Materials and methods

Design

This scoping review uses the methodological framework put forth by Arksey & O’Malley (2005) as well as the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist for protocol and manuscript preparation (Tricco et al., 2018) [see Additional file 1]. A scoping review protocol was submitted to the Open Science Framework (OSF) and can be found at 10.17605/OSF.IO/RXHA4. The OSF protocol was registered prior to database searching and guided subsequent screening, extraction, and reporting.

Framework

We used a Population and Context (PC) framework for this study where our Population was people who identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, questioning, or any other SOGIE that fell under the LGBTQ + umbrella term including but not limited to pansexual, asexual, and/or Two-Spirit of any age within the contiguous U.S. Studies that included one or more participants who belonged to the LGBTQ + community were included.

Our context was discretionary arrests, defined as police decisions to arrest that are not strictly mandated by statute or policy, but instead allow for officer interpretation, preference, or situational judgment. We included any study in which such discretion was evident—whether described by participants or inferred from author analysis—regardless of whether the term “discretionary arrest” was used. For example, studies where participants reported being arrested by one officer but not by another for the same offense were eligible. We excluded articles that treated arrest as a binary variable (e.g., arrested/not arrested) without sufficient context to assess discretion, as well as studies that explicitly stated they excluded discretionary arrest from analysis. This ensured that our sample reflected discretionary enforcement practices that disproportionally impact sexual and gender minority communities.

To distinguish arrest discretion from upstream stops and downstream charging discretion, we only coded discretion when the arrest decision itself was described as non-mandated (by statute/policy) and contingent on officer judgment; discretion at other decision points is noted contextually but not treated as the exposure of interest. For clarity, our exposure is the arrest decision itself when non-mandated; discretion at upstream stops or downstream charging/sentencing is noted contextually but not treated as the primary exposure. By contrast, non-discretionary arrests include situations where statute or policy requires custody (e.g., active arrest warrants or statutorily mandated arrests for specified offenses), leaving no lawful option to cite-and-release.

Criteria for study selection

Health impacts

This scoping review is grounded in a holistic definition of health aligned with a social determinant of health lens, which conceptualizes well-being as encompassing physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual dimensions (Steele, 2020; Hatzenbuehler & Flores, 2024). This framing informed the inclusion of outcomes such as anxiety, hypervigilance, spiritual harm, medical mistrust, and perceived dehumanization—health harms often reported among LGBTQ + individuals with intersecting marginalized identities (Parker et al., 2020; Adedoyin et al., 2019; Cunneen & Tauri, 2019; Medina & Mahowald, 2023; Schuler et al., 2021).

Empirical studies

Only empirical studies published about the contiguous U.S. were included, as territories like Puerto Rico have different policing policies. We excluded dissertations and grey literature that has not undergone the peer review process even if it met all other inclusion criteria. Systematic and other scoping reviews were also excluded.

Search strategies

With the assistance of a university research librarian who specializes in scoping and systematic reviews, we developed a search strategy to include a wide range of keywords and subject headings for this review’s population, the LGBTQ + community, including terms that may now be considered outdated and/or offensive along with keywords and subject headings for the review’s context, discretionary arrest. [see Additional file 2]. In addition to the original term development for the search strategy, the research librarian consulted search hedges related to the population and included terms as appropriate (Rey & Holland, 2021; Emory University Libraries, 2024). This ensured coverage of studies across decades beginning in 1970, one year after the Stonewall Uprising. The following databases were used in the primary search: Academic Search Complete (EBSCO), APA PsycINFO (EBSCO), Criminal Justice Abstracts with Full Text (EBSCO), Legal Collection (EBSCO), LGBTQ + Life (EBSCO), Medline (Ovid) [see Additional File 3 for search strategies within each database]. Figure 1 depicts a PRISMA diagram of the complete process. No hand searched articles were included. Articles were imported into Covidence on July 24, 2024.

Data collection and analysis

Different levels of screening were conducted. First, two independent reviewers performed a title and abstract review using Covidence software (Veritas Health Innovation, (Veritas 2024)). Conflicts were resolved by a third independent reviewer. Secondly, a full-text review was performed by all three reviewers independently to ensure both rigor and all the above criteria were met. Any disagreement was managed by discussion until there was consensus. After consensus was met, these studies were then extracted into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet where study characteristics were input by reviewers and other members of the research team. The data charting form drew on prior work of the lead author (Wood et al., 2023) and was modified through consensus of the research team. Study characteristics included title, authors, abstract, publication date, publication journal, whether it is primary or secondary data, study design, sampling methods, sampling population, sample demographics, operationalization of discretionary arrest (yes/no), discretionary arrest is referred to or mentioned (introduction/methods/results/discussion/conclusion/limitations), how discretionary arrest is referred to or used, health impacts, and the discussion themes. Health impacts were coded using an inductive approach that captured a wide range of physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual outcomes (e.g., anxiety, hypervigilance, trauma-related symptoms, medical mistrust, and barriers to care). Reviewers identified and categorized health-related findings during data extraction to ensure alignment with the study’s public health focus.

Results

The authors identified a total of four empirical studies that were included in the final analysis. A summary of the included studies is provided in Table 1. None of the included studies used the term ‘discretionary arrest’ explicitly, but all met inclusion criteria by describing officer discretion in enforcement decisions. Of these, n = 2 were qualitative, n = 1 was mixed methods, and n = 1 was quantitative. The total number of participants in these four articles was 685 people. The sample was racially diverse, and all four articles stated the racial/ethnic demographics of their participants with 148 being Black (both Latine and non-Latine Black), 186 being non-Latine White, 243 being Latine of any race, 53 being mixed or biracial, 29 being Asian or Pacific Islander, and 9 identified as some other race and 17 were unknown. The ages of the participants ranged from 13 to 32 + years.

Theme 1: health impacts from policing

Across all four studies, participants described police encounters as triggering acute and chronic health impacts. These health effects extended beyond the immediate physical risks of arrest and incarceration, shaping emotional, psychological, and spiritual well-being in deeply interconnected ways. LGBTQ + people of color, particularly transgender women and gender-expansive individuals, consistently described feeling dehumanized and targeted, even in the absence of formal charges.

In Hereth (2023), nearly every participant recounted experiences of misgendering, verbal abuse, and perceived assumptions of sex work. One woman, for example, shared that officers referred to her as “it” during a search. Others described how repeated harassment normalized a baseline level of hypervigilance and anxiety that shaped all aspects of their public life. Participants described internalizing the belief that they were “not seen as fully human,” a belief that became a psychological baseline shaping their daily interactions.

Similarly, Robinson (2020) documented how police encounters intensified psychological distress among LGBTQ + youth experiencing homelessness. Participants described daily efforts to avoid contact with police knowing that such interactions, whether or not they resulted in arrest, could result in humiliation, assault, or being placed in a gender-inappropriate holding cell. The emotional labor of managing these risks added to feelings of despair and disconnection.

Theme 2: racialized enforcement patterns

All four articles documented racialized patterns in police decision-making and enforcement. Even though the term “racial profiling” was not always used explicitly, participant narratives clearly illustrated how Black and Latine LGBTQ + individuals were disproportionately targeted. In Parker et al. (2018), Black men who have sex with men (BMSM) described being stopped for behaviors that went unpunished when exhibited by their White peers. One participant recalled, “They [White men] got tickets. I got cuffs.” In Ompad et al. (2015), quantitative findings suggested that arrest histories had opposite associations depending on race: White/API young men who have sex with men (YMSM) with arrest histories were statistically more likely to report unprotected sex, suggesting risky behavior; meanwhile, Black/Latine YMSM with arrest histories were less likely to report such behaviors, suggesting that they may have been arrested based on appearance, presence in public space, or other non-behavioral cues.

Robinson (2020) pushed this analysis further, arguing that policing of LGBTQ + people of color often functioned to uphold White heteronormativity. The study described how Black and brown LGBTQ + youth were incarcerated at higher rates, often on prostitution-related charges that rested on officer suspicion rather than evidence. One youth was jailed for being a lesbian, another for being a transgender woman, both placed in cis male facilities, both misgendered, and both criminalized for their gender presentation.

Theme 3: officer discretion in arrest outcomes

Although none of the studies used the term “discretionary arrest,” all documented patterns of officer discretion that influenced whether participants were arrested, warned, or released. Discretion in arrest outcomes was a recurring theme, particularly in qualitative narratives that contrasted the same offense yielding different legal consequences based on race, gender identity, or perceived sexuality. Hereth (2023) provided multiple examples in which transgender women were arrested while their cisgender companions committing the same act were not. One participant described being with a group of friends when all were stopped, but only she—being visibly trans—was taken into custody. An unhoused participant described being alternately arrested or left alone by officers, depending on their perception of her identity.

In Ompad et al. (2015), arrest was operationalized as a variable correlated with sexual risk behavior, but the racial breakdown complicated the interpretation. White/API men with arrest histories exhibited higher rates of unprotected sex, potentially indicating behavior-based arrests. However, Black and Latine men with similar histories were actually less likely to engage in risk behaviors, suggesting that arrests were more likely due to discriminatory enforcement rather than actual conduct. This pattern, the same action is punished differently depending on who performs it, is the core of discretionary arrest’s public health impact. It delegitimizes legal norms, undermines trust in public systems, and creates unpredictable environments that foster anxiety and distress.

Theme 4: criminalization of gender expression and identity

The most visceral and pervasive form of discretionary policing documented in these studies involved the criminalization of gender expression, particularly for transgender and gender-expansive individuals. Hereth (2023) and Robinson (2020) both highlighted the concept of “walking while trans,” in which simply existing in public space while visibly trans triggered police stops, harassment, and arrest.

In Robinson’s study, transgender youth of color shared that they were routinely profiled as sex workers, even when engaged in everyday activities. One interviewee was placed in a men’s jail despite identifying as a woman, while another was arrested on prostitution charges based on clothing and location alone. These encounters exemplify how discretionary arrests are used to enforce gender conformity and punish transgression, particularly when race, poverty, and non-cis-heteronormative identity intersect.

Hereth (2023) similarly emphasized how gender markers on IDs were weaponized. Participants shared that even if they passed as their gender, officers would harass or detain them as soon as their ID revealed a mismatch. As one participant put it, “the ID didn’t protect me, it exposed me.” In Texas, this finding carries specific weight given the recent changes to the Department of Public Safety’s policy, which now bars most changes to gender markers on state IDs (Transgender Education Network, 2024). Such policies, combined with discretionary enforcement, functionally criminalize trans existence and increase vulnerability to state violence.

Theme 5: sexual risk and surveillance

Both Parker et al. (2018) and Ompad (2015) documented how zero-tolerance policing and heightened surveillance shaped sexual health behaviors, creating disincentives to seek care and increasing HIV risk. Parker et al. (2018) showed how discretionary enforcement practices, such as using condom possession as evidence of sex work, created powerful deterrents for HIV prevention. BMSW described skipping clinic visits or avoiding carrying condoms to reduce the risk of being stopped by police. One participant explained that being stopped for “looking gay” discouraged him from accessing prevention services. These practices, in turn, increased HIV vulnerability.

Similarly, Ompad et al. (2015) found that arrest history among Black/Latine YMSM was associated with lower likelihood of engaging in unprotected anal sex, but not because of improved health behaviors. Instead, participants described heightened scrutiny and fear of criminalization, which may have led to avoidance of public sexual health services and diminished agency in sexual decision-making. These racial disparities suggest that arrest experiences operate through different psychological and structural pathways depending on race and social positioning. Discretionary arrest thus acts not only as a public health barrier, but as a mechanism that amplifies sexual risk and surveillance, particularly for queer men and transgender women of color navigating highly policed environments.

Theme 6: social withdrawal and isolation as coping

Participants in Hereth (2023) and Robinson (2020) described withdrawing from public life as a survival strategy in response to the threat of discretionary arrest and constant surveillance. Transgender women and gender-expansive individuals in Hereth’s study repeatedly expressed that simply existing in public spaces triggered anxiety and hypervigilance. “Walking while trans,” a phrase used by multiple participants, captured the reality that daily movements were policed, scrutinized, and potentially criminalized.

A Latina participant elaborated that she and other trans Latinas “limit their time in public spaces because of the added fear of being targeted due to their immigration status.” Similarly, another participant described how being unhoused meant she could be arrested or left alone based solely on the officer’s discretion that day. Such behavioral adaptations—shrinking themselves, staying home, and limiting visibility—were common survival strategies. While these responses reduced immediate risks, they also led to disconnection from affirming services and increased isolation, reinforcing structural exclusion.

Theme 7: mistrust in public systems

Experiences with discretionary arrest and targeted policing shaped deep mistrust in both the criminal-legal and healthcare systems. Parker et al. (2018) highlighted that frequent police contact, especially for minor or perceived infractions, eroded trust in institutional actors across the board. One participant shared that police interactions were often not about justice but about being made an “example,” reinforcing the idea that public systems existed to control rather than protect them.

This mistrust extended to healthcare systems. Participants in Hereth (2023) and Parker et al. (2018) reported avoiding hospitals and clinics out of fear that their gender identity, race, or immigration status would lead to mistreatment or surveillance. The threat of being misgendered, having one’s documentation questioned, or being reported to authorities discouraged them from accessing even essential care. As one participant put it, “you never know what they’ll do once they see your ID.” This erosion of trust had tangible health consequences: delayed care, untreated trauma, and chronic fear in routine interactions.

Theme 8: intersectional marginalization

All four studies demonstrated how discretionary arrest functions at the intersection of multiple systems of oppression, where race, gender identity, sexuality, immigration status, and class collide to produce cumulative harm. Participants in Robinson (2020) described being policed not just for their actions, but for their embodiment of identities that challenged White, cisgender, and heteronormative norms. One participant was incarcerated in a men’s jail cell because she was a lesbian; another, a transgender woman, was misgendered and placed in a male unit, exemplifying how institutions actively reinforced gender conformity through punitive measures.

In Hereth (2023), a trans Latina participant who was also undocumented described feeling triply targeted: as a woman, as trans, and as someone without legal immigration status. She articulated that the fear of being arrested was not only about jail, it was about being disappeared by systems that viewed her as simultaneously criminal, disposable, and invisible. In Parker et al. (2018) and Ompad et al. (2015), Black and Latine men who have sex with men reported similar compounded vulnerabilities. Officers used non-behavioral cues, such as clothing, posture, or location, to justify stops and arrests. Police discretion targeted not actions, but identities, bodies marked as deviant through race, gender, and sexuality. Across all studies, the theme of intersectional marginalization emerged not as background context but as a core mechanism through which discretionary policing operated.

Discussion

The results of this scoping review document how subjective enforcement practices disproportionately harm LGBTQ + communities through structural mechanisms that produce health disparities. A consistent, deeply racialized pattern emerges in how LGBTQ + communities, particularly Black, Latine, transgender, and gender-expansive individuals, experience police contact and its associated health harms. The eight themes identified across the four included studies demonstrate interactions with systems of oppression that generate anxiety, social isolation, HIV vulnerability, and deep mistrust in both public safety and healthcare systems. For instance, mistrust in public systems and spiritual harm suggest the need for trauma-informed, LGBTQ+-affirming care across the lifespan (Craig et al., 2021; Keuroghlian et al., 2018). Racialized enforcement and the criminalization of gender expression highlight the urgency of policy reforms that curtail officer discretion, such as standardizing cite-and-release for eligible offenses (Hirschel & Dean, 1995). Themes related to sexual risk and social withdrawal indicate the need for culturally tailored public-health outreach.

Beyond the four studies synthesized here, and distinct from the empirical findings, the policy environment presents mounting challenges that require strategic repositioning. In light of the termination of existing federal grants and broader contraction in health-equity funding, capacity for LGBTQ-specific work is constrained. One pragmatic (but politically fraught) approach is to foreground officer mental health as a lever of structural change, lever, leveraging evidence of deteriorating outcomes among officers to justify community-based reforms (Nwokolo & Nwokolo, 2024; Violanti et al., 2017). Narrowing tasks to core functions can mitigate role creep, the incremental expansion of duties that increase stress and error risk (Marwa & Kinuthia, 2022; Vila, 2006) and may reduce unnecessary contacts.

At the same time, decriminalization of survival behaviors and administrative/legal reforms (e.g., streamlined access to legal gender affirmation), (Restar, 2023) may lessen health-harming exposure to policing, consistent with themes of chronic stress and dehumanization identified in this review. These directions extend beyond the current evidence base and should be treated as opportunities for future research, rather than established effects. While reframing may offer a politically viable pathway for funding and intervention design (Nix & Pickett, 2023), it also carries risks: elevating officer wellness can recentralize systems of power and dilute accountability. Any such reframing must not substitute for community accountability. Design and implementation should be co-created with LGBTQ + communities and paired with transparent oversight to avoid recentralizing power.

To mitigate these risks, research and policy responses must be grounded in equitable, community-led partnerships that center the voices of those most affected (Wallerstein et al., 2017), including Black, Latine, Indigenous, LGBTQ+, immigrant and disabled community members. Advancing this work also demands a stronger empirical foundation that centers Indigenous, undocumented, and incarcerated LGBTQ + voices, communities persistently excluded from mainstream health research. Confronting discretionary arrest as a determinant of health requires public health institutions, funders, and policymakers to act decisively in resourcing community-led solutions. Research should evaluate health impacts across multiple points of the police-contact continuum, not only arrest. Without such commitments, efforts to reduce health disparities will remain fragmented and inadequate.

Limitations

We operationalized discretionary arrest as arrest decisions that were not mandated by statute or policy and were instead shaped by officer judgment, as described by participants or inferred by authors. This introduces conceptual ambiguity, since discretion at arrest can be difficult to disentangle from discretion in stops or charging decisions that occur at different points and may involve different actors (e.g., patrol officers, detectives, prosecutors). Our choice reflects a pragmatic, policy-relevant lens, similar to how “stop-and-frisk” scholarship isolates an upstream decision point, to support local stakeholders engaged in efforts to adopt, implement, and evaluate cite-and-release or related enforcement policies. We also note that observed health harms may stem from downstream consequences of arrest (e.g., detention conditions, loss of employment or housing, family separation) as well as from the encounter itself; our synthesis cannot fully disaggregate these pathways.

Only four studies met inclusion criteria and none explicitly used the term “discretionary arrest,” though each documented non-mandated arrest decision-making. Samples were small, non-probability, and geographically specific, with notable gaps (e.g., Indigenous LGBTQ + voices). While the eight themes identified in this scoping review point to urgent and important patterns, they cannot be assumed to consistently represent the full range of LGBTQ + experiences with policing and health across all contexts. Over-extrapolating from this small body of literature risks reinforcing gaps in knowledge by overlooking the experiences of groups that are persistently and systemically excluded from research, such as those that live at the intersections of Indigeneity, Blackness, Latinidad, immigration, and queerness. Future work should seek to capture data on sexual orientation and gender identity, ensure adequate power, and adopt designs that differentiate arrest discretion from stops, charging, incarceration, and supervision.

Conclusion

Discretionary arrest is not simply a policing practice; it is a structural determinant of health that disproportionately harms LGBTQ + individuals, particularly those who are Black, Latine, transgender, or gender-expansive. Although underrecognized in public health literature, discretionary enforcement reinforces intersecting systems of oppression and produces cumulative harms including anxiety, HIV vulnerability, social withdrawal, and profound mistrust in public institutions.

This scoping review provides a foundational framework to understand and address these harms. The eight themes identified point to actionable pathways for structural intervention, from policy reforms that curtail officer discretion to trauma-informed care models grounded in LGBTQ + lived experience. Effectiveness depends on grounding these strategies in culturally responsive, community-led efforts that center those most affected.

Moving forward, reducing the public health consequences of discretionary arrest requires both political will and institutional accountability. Without investment in research, representation, and community-led action, the structural harms outlined here will persist. Public health cannot remain neutral: discretionary policing is a health equity issue, and addressing it demands urgent, collective, and transformative action.
