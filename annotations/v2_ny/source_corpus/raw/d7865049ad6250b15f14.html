Abstract

Background

Women living with HIV (WLHIV) in the United States experience higher rates of death from cervical and breast cancer and lower screening rates for both compared to women without HIV. These disparities highlight the need to better understand access to and uptake of women’s preventive health services—particularly cervical cancer screening, breast cancer screening, and HPV vaccination. Most existing data on these services among WLHIV come from urban settings; therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate factors affecting adherence to these preventive health measures among WLHIV in Vermont, a rural state with unique healthcare challenges.

Methods

This cross-sectional study included a retrospective analysis of electronic medical record (EMR) data and a one-time quantitative survey. The EMR analysis assessed cervical Papanicolaou (Pap) smear, mammography, and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination rates among WLHIV and women without HIV enrolled in care at University of Vermont-affiliated sites from January 2017 to December 2020. The survey collected demographic information and details regarding care received from WLHIV.

Results

Among 98 WLHIV and 481 women without HIV, WLHIV were significantly less likely to receive appropriate Pap smears (56.1% vs. 74.2%, p < 0.001), mammographies (57.1% vs. 88.4%, p < 0.001), and at least one dose of the HPV vaccine (7.1% vs. 20.0%, p = 0.002). Survey data from 41 WLHIV revealed that being sexually active, heterosexual, in a relationship, premenopausal, and housing secure were associated with higher Pap smear adherence.

Conclusions

These findings underscore the importance of addressing both individual and systemic factors to improve the provision and receipt of women’s preventive health services WLHIV, especially in rural healthcare settings.

Keywords: Women living with HIV (WLHIV), Cervical Papanicolaou (Pap) smears, Mammography, Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, Women’s preventive healthcare, Rural healthcare

Plain language summary

Women living with HIV in the U.S. are more likely to die from cervical and breast cancer than women without HIV. Unfortunately, they are also less likely to receive preventive care with respect to these cancers, with lower rates of breast and cervical cancer screening and lower rates of HPV vaccination to prevent the development of cervical cancer. Most research on this issue has focused on cities; therefore, this study examined screening rates and factors influencing them in Vermont, a rural state with unique healthcare challenges.

Researchers analyzed medical records corresponding to clinic visits from 2017 to 2020 and surveyed women with HIV about their health and screening habits. They found that women with HIV were significantly less likely to receive routine cervical cancer tests (Pap smears), breast cancer screenings (mammograms), or the HPV vaccine compared to women without HIV. Only about 56% of women with HIV had up-to-date Pap smears, compared to 74% of women without HIV. Mammogram rates were also much lower (57% vs. 88%), and HPV vaccination was rare in both groups, but even lower among women with HIV.

The survey showed that women with HIV who were sexually active, in relationships, premenopausal, or had stable housing were more likely to stay on track with cervical cancer screening.

This study highlights the need for better support systems to ensure women with HIV, especially in rural areas, get the cancer screening and other preventative measures they need. Addressing barriers at both the personal and healthcare system levels could help improve these rates and ultimately save lives.

Background

In the U.S., cisgender women account for one-in-four HIV infections and nearly a fifth of new HIV diagnoses [1]. Advances in HIV care and treatment are enabling women living with HIV (WLHIV) to live longer and healthier lives, and yet they continue to experience disproportionate rates of preventable morbidity and mortality, in part due to gaps in the delivery and update of key women’s preventive health (WPH) services. Among these, cervical cancer screening, human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, and breast cancer screening represent essential services that remain underutilized among WLHIV. The remainder of this section will explore current evidence on the delivery of and barriers to these three important components of preventive care among women living with HIV—particularly in rural settings —highlighting important gaps in knowledge that motivated the present study.

Cervical cancer

Cervical cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among WLHIV, with the incidence of cervical cancer three to four times higher than in the general U.S. population [2, 3]. This disparity is partly due to HIV-related immune suppression, which contributes to persistent HPV infection and increased risk of progression to cervical cancer, particularly among women with lower CD4 + T cell counts (the immune cells targeted and depleted by HIV) [4, 5]. Socioeconomic and healthcare system factors, including limited access to screening, also likely contribute to this elevated risk.

In recognition of the higher risk of cervical cancer in this population, federal guidelines from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services support more rigorous Papanicolaou (Pap) screening for WLHIV, starting at age 21 and typically continuing once every three years for life [1]. However, a significant portion (22–56%) of women with HIV in the U.S. are not in conformity with these guidelines [6, 7]. There is keen interest in identifying factors that influence adequate screening as a means of elucidating targets for intervention. Studies involving the general population have shown that factors associated with lower rates of screening include older age, rural location, lower level of education, lack of insurance, annual household income of less than $25,000, unmarried status, and lack of primary care engagement in the preceding year [8–10]. A qualitative study from 2021 identified several barriers to cervical cancer screening in rural populations, including clinician shortages, lack of available appointments, lack of adequate insurance/steep copayments, and cost of transportation to appointments [11]. Notably, there is comparatively limited data on these and other factors as they pertain to WLHIV in either urban or rural settings.

HPV vaccination

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommends routine HPV vaccination at age 11 or 12 (with the option to start as early as age 9), routine catch-up vaccination through age 26, and catch-up vaccination through age 45 based on shared clinical decision making [12]. While HPV vaccination is a highly effective tool for preventing high-risk HPV infections that can develop into cervical cancer, its uptake among women living with HIV is lower than in the general population. Some studies suggest that HPV vaccine completion rates among WLHIV may be as low as 12–18%, though disaggregated data are sparse [13]. By comparison, HPV vaccination rates in the general female population aged 19–26 are estimated at approximately 56% [14]. While data regarding the rate of HPV vaccination in this age group in Vermont is not available, it is expected to be the same, if not higher, based on 2023 data that 71% of Vermont female youths aged 13–17 were up to date with HPV vaccines compared to the national average of 66% [15]. A survey of 100 women without HIV in the U.S. showed predominant barriers to HPV vaccination include hesitancy and personal beliefs surrounding the HPV and all vaccines, healthcare barriers, and low perceived risk [16]. These barriers were among those mentioned in focus group discussions with 14 patients living with HIV in Oklahoma [17]. Employment status, low income status, unmarried status, and smoking history were also shown to be demographic factors associated with lower rates of HPV vaccination in a larger cohort of several hundred WLHIV living in Ontario, Canada [18]. We endeavored to build upon these findings with data from our rural Vermont population regarding vaccination rates and factors associated with vaccination status.

Breast cancer screening

While WLHIV are not believed to have significantly elevated rates of breast cancer compared to the general population, studies suggest they experience differences in tumor characteristics and outcomes [19]. Importantly, WLHIV are more likely to present with advanced-stage breast cancer at diagnosis compared to women without HIV, and cancer-specific mortality is significantly higher in WLHIV than in women without HIV, even when adjusting for stage and treatment [20, 21]. Current guidelines recommend biennial screening mammography for women aged 50 to 74; data regarding the adherence of WLHIV to these guidelines are mixed and limited in scope [22]. One cohort study found that only 49% of WLHIV in the recommended age range had received a mammogram in the past two years, compared to 64% of women without HIV [23, 24]. Breast cancer screening rates among WLHIV in rural settings are not as well characterized, and little is known about the demographic, clinical, and provider-related factors that influence screening behavior in this group. By evaluating mammography adherence within a rural HIV care population, this study aims to fill an important gap in our understanding of cancer prevention for WLHIV.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted among WLHIV enrolled in the University of Vermont Medical Center’s (UVMMC) Ryan White HIV/AIDS Comprehensive Care Clinics (CCCs) and women without HIV enrolled in UVMMC’s Adult Primary Care Clinic. From its inception in 1987, the UVMMC Ryan White HIV/AIDS program has expanded by establishing satellite clinics affiliated with the main medical campus, thereby extending services to more rural areas. HIV CCCs are currently located in Burlington, Rutland, Brattleboro, Berlin, and St. Johnsbury, spanning all quadrants of the state. They offer state-of-the-art treatment for all stages of HIV as well as primary and specialty care to people living with HIV throughout Vermont. Services provided at HIV CCCs include gynecologic services, such as cervical cancer screening and contraception provision, along with various primary care services, including other age-appropriate cancer screenings, routine vaccinations, and general age-appropriate screenings and management (e.g., for diabetes and hyperlipidemia). UVMMC infectious disease (ID) providers regularly travel to these sites and use telehealth to follow up with patients there. These include clinicians who have undergone formal training in ID, including some subspecializing in HIV, as well as nurse practitioners without formal ID/HIV training. As of 2017, 678 people were living with HIV across Vermont, and at the time of data collection for this study, roughly 650 (95.8%) of them were engaged in care through the CCCs [25].

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study using retrospective EMR data and a quantitative survey. Pap smears, mammographies, and HPV vaccination rates were reviewed for WLHIV at UVMMC’s HIV Comprehensive Care Clinics (data from CAREWare) and women without HIV at UVMMC’s Adult Primary Care Clinic (data from Epic) who were enrolled into care between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2020. A one-time survey was also administered to WLHIV enrolled in the HIV clinics, and the study was approved by the University of Vermont Institutional Review Board.

Study participants

Inclusion criteria required participants to self-identify in the EMR as cisgender women, be 18 years or older, and be enrolled in care at UVMMC. Additionally, for quantitative survey participants, they had to be willing and able to provide voluntary informed consent. The following populations were excluded: cisgender females under the age of 18 years, cisgender men, transgender individuals, cisgender women not enrolled in care at UVMMC, and, for survey participants, those unwilling or unable to give voluntary informed consent.

Data collection

For the retrospective analysis, data on the receipt of cervical Pap smears, mammographies, and HPV vaccination were extracted from participant medical records for those enrolling in care during the study period (January 2017 to December 2020). Women without HIV were considered up to date with Pap screening if they met 2018 USPSTF guidelines (every three years from ages 21–30, then every five years until 65), whereas WLHIV were evaluated using Department of Health and Human Services recommendations (starting at 21 and continuing at least every three years for life) [26, 27] Breast cancer screening was assessed using the 2016 USPSTF guidance, which was current during the study period, recommending biennial mammography for individuals aged 50–74, rather than the 2024 update that recommends initiation at age 40 [22, 28]. HPV vaccination was defined as receipt of at least one dose, with series completion according to 2020 ACOG guidelines (routine vaccination at ages 11–12, catch-up vaccination through 26, and shared decision-making through 45) [12]. For WLHIV, IDSA recommends a 3-dose series, given their attenuated immune response, which functionally groups them with other immunocompromised patients [29].

The study team also conducted a quantitative survey that covered a range of WPH topics, including the history of sexual and family planning services (including those related to pregnancies, births, and terminations), fertility history, partnership history, and sociodemographic information. Eligible participants enrolled in care at the CCCs received telephone calls from study staff to screen them for eligibility. Those who met the inclusion criteria and expressed interest in participating were then invited to complete a 20–30 min interviewer-administered survey by telephone. Participants were asked to give voluntary verbal informed consent prior to initiating the survey. Consenting individuals were then interviewed by study staff using a standardized script of questions, with participants asked to select the most appropriate response from among several predefined answers (e.g., yes or no) to facilitate analysis. Participants who completed the survey received a $20 grocery card, consistent with research practices in the study area.

Data analysis

Study data were managed in REDCap and analyzed in Stata 16.1 [30–32]. WLHIV and women without HIV were compared for Pap smear, mammography, and HPV vaccination per federal guidelines using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests (asterisk), with significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

EMR analysis

The retrospective EMR analysis included 98 WLHIV and 481 women without HIV. Among WLHIV, the average age was 51 (range 19–71 years), 66.3% were white, and 23.5% were African American/Black. Among women without HIV, the average age was 48 (range 22–69 years), 94.2% were white, and 1.3% were African American/Black (Table 1).

Survey

The survey was completed by telephone with 41 WLHIV. The average age was 54 (range, 20–69 years). Nine (22.5%) were African American/Black, four (10.0%) were Native American/American Indian, and 24 (60.0%) were White (data not shown).

Table 4 conveys Fisher’s exact tests to determine the likelihood of receiving cervical Pap smears. WLHIV who reported being sexually active at the time of the survey were more likely than those not sexually active to have endorsed undergoing a cervical Pap smear in the past three years (p = 0.040). Women who were not post-menopausal at the time of the survey were more likely to have reported a cervical Pap smear in the prior year than women who were post-menopausal (p = 0.041).

Participants who supported their cost of living through wages (reliance on wages) were more likely to report having healthcare providers who counseled them on the importance of cervical Pap smears (p = 0.021). In exploring housing status, participants who lived in their own place were significantly more likely to have received a cervical Pap smear in the prior three years (p = 0.008); however, having spent more than one day in a homeless shelter (shelter dwelling) appeared not statistically significant in participant descriptions of cervical Pap smear practices.

Participants who denied substance use in the prior year appeared equally likely to have received a cervical Pap smear as those who endorsed substance use in this same timeframe (p = 0.091). Women without a history of incarceration appeared more likely than those with a history of incarceration to report having a healthcare provider explain the importance of routine Pap smears to them (p = 0.036).

Several sociodemographic factors were not associated with the likelihood of receiving cervical Pap smears, including the number of pregnancies, number of births, educational status, current employment, and health insurance status (data not shown). In exploring sexual orientation, WLHIV who identified as heterosexual were more likely to report receiving a cervical Pap smear in the prior year (p = 0.035) when compared with WLHIV who identified as bisexual or other sexual orientations (data not shown). For partnership status, participants who reported being in a relationship at the time of the survey were more likely than those not in a current relationship to have received a cervical Pap smear in the previous three years (p = 0.003).

A segment of the survey explored participant impressions of their WRH experiences (Table 5). Participants with a formally HIV-trained clinician were more likely than participants without an HIV-trained clinician to describe the care they received as woman-centered (p = 0.002).

Discussion

Our findings revealed less rigorous cervical cancer screening, HPV vaccination, and breast cancer screening among WLHIV enrolled in HIV care compared to women without HIV engaged in primary care in Vermont. This is meaningful given previous data showing that WLHIV have a higher risk of developing cervical cancer, dying from their cervical cancer, experiencing a recurrence of cervical cancer, having advanced stage breast cancer at the time of diagnosis, and dying from their breast cancer compared to their counterparts of women without HIV [20, 33, 34].

In our Vermont cohort, women living with HIV were markedly less likely to receive Pap screening than their peers without HIV, with differences that were particularly pronounced at the youngest and oldest ages. Screening among WLHIV in their twenties occurred at only about one-third the rate of women without HIV, and in the 51–60 age group, their rates were nearly cut in half. Even in midlife, where gaps narrowed somewhat, WLHIV still lagged by 10–15 percentage points. These findings highlight a consistent pattern of under-screening that persists across the lifespan, suggesting that barriers to preventive care for WLHIV are not confined to one age group but reflect broader, systemic challenges.

These findings mirror the pattern described in other parts of the country, but the magnitude of disparity between WLHIV and women without HIV is greater in our population, suggesting additional barriers to cervical cancer screening, such as rural access, fragmented HIV and gynecologic care, and socioeconomic influences [6, 7, 35, 36]. Survey data from our cohort further underscore the role of socioeconomic context: for example, women who lacked stable housing or who had a history of incarceration reported markedly lower Pap screening rates (Table 4). These findings highlight how structural vulnerabilities intersect with individual-level barriers, underscoring the need for integrated models of care that address factors such as housing stability, financial insecurity, and social inequity to enhance preventive health outcomes for WLHIV in rural settings.

Women living with HIV in Vermont had about one-third the HPV vaccination initiation rate of their peers without HIV, and full vaccination rates showed a similar disparity. This pattern highlights missed opportunities for the incorporation of HPV vaccination into HIV care. Younger women, however, were much more likely to start vaccination, with uptake in both groups—though still unequal—approaching national levels for women in their twenties [19, 37, 38]. After age 30, rates dropped sharply: initiation fell by more than half among women without HIV and declined even more steeply among women with HIV, while virtually no women over 40 began vaccination. These low rates in older age groups pull down overall uptake rates and reflect the relatively recent guideline changes that expanded vaccination eligibility to older individuals. A key takeaway is that meaningful opportunities remain to offer catch-up vaccination to patients beyond their thirties [12].

In our cohort, just over half of WLHIV completed mammography, compared to nearly nine in ten women without HIV. Uptake was relatively high for both groups in the 41–50 age range, but sharp disparities emerged among older women: WLHIV in their fifties were about three times less likely than their peers without HIV to undergo mammography, and those in their sixties had screening rates less than half as high. Nationally, between three-quarters and four-fifths of women aged 50–74 report mammography within the past two years, whereas WLHIV consistently lag behind, often falling in the 40–55% range, though some recent cohorts approach 70% [35, 39, 40]. Our findings, therefore, align with national evidence of reduced breast cancer screening among WLHIV, but the magnitude of disparity in Vermont is especially striking. That the very age groups most targeted by breast cancer prevention guidelines are least likely to be screened underscores concerning gaps in preventive health integration within HIV care.

Our findings highlighted several racial differences in WPH services that warrant further investigation (Table 3), though it should be noted that the small size of the subgroup of black women limits statistical power and the ability to draw definitive conclusions. White WLHIV were markedly less likely than their peers without HIV to receive Pap smears, mammography, or HPV vaccination, whereas black WLHIV showed comparatively higher screening uptake, narrowing the gap with black women without HIV. These patterns suggest that disparities between women with and without HIV in Vermont are most pronounced among white women, raising questions about how structural, cultural, and healthcare access factors shape preventive care. One possible contributor is the country of origin: the American Community Survey (2018–2022) found that 45% of Black Vermonters were foreign-born, compared to 11% of Black Americans nationally, while only 3% of white Vermonters were foreign-born, versus 7% nationally [41, 42]. Future work should examine how country of origin and related factors, such as programs engaging new Americans in healthcare, influence WPH services.

Interestingly, our findings suggest that the type of provider—whether HIV/infectious disease–trained or not—did not significantly affect most measures of women’s preventive health services (Table 5). Across domains such as addressing sexual and reproductive health needs, providing stage-of-life–appropriate care, and delivering information specific to women, patients reported similar levels of satisfaction regardless of provider type. This indicates that in rural settings, where access to HIV-trained providers may be limited, the availability of such specialists is unlikely to represent a major barrier to ensuring adequate preventive care for women living with HIV. That said, factors that are likely implicated in insufficient rural healthcare delivery include geographic distance of clinics from their patients, limited availability of providers (regardless of primary care versus specialist training), and a paucity of medical technologies and EMR functionalities due to financial challenges at smaller practices [43, 44]. Additionally, while telehealth provides meaningful opportunities for clinical engagement in rural populations, it does not accommodate clinical procedures such as Pap smears.

This study is limited by the relatively small population of WLHIV in Vermont compared to many areas of the U.S., limiting the generalizability of our findings to other rural settings. We also relied on cervical Pap pathology that was reported in EMR, which may have missed sexual/reproductive health encounters that occurred in family planning or other women’s healthcare settings. We defined Pap smear compliance as having had one in the past three years, regardless of whether women had received abnormal test results and were due for sooner follow-up, meaning our data likely overestimates the percentage of women who are up to date with their individualized screening recommendations. Assessing HPV vaccine practice offered some challenges. Per ACIP recommendations in 2019, HPV vaccination was recommended for patients through age 26 [45]. As such, the bulk of our described older patient population likely missed vaccine opportunity earlier in life. Catch-up vaccination through age 45 fell under a recommendation by IDSA in more recent years, likely also translating to a sizable number of our older population missing an HPV vaccine opportunity [29]. Additional factors that may have impacted HPV vaccine uptake include hysterectomy status, insurance coverage, and availability of HPV vaccination in all healthcare settings. COVID-19 was also an influencing factor: beginning around the final quarter of the study period, the COVID-19 pandemic impacted not just the way this study was conducted (with survey interviews occurring by telephone as opposed to in-person), but also likely influenced results, in that social isolation recommendations limited in-person visits and screening services starting at the beginning of 2020. Finally, transportation status (e.g., women reporting access to a reliable car or bus service) was not explored, which may have missed a potentially critical factor affecting access to healthcare settings and services in rural areas.

We included only cisgender women in this study, as transgender and non-binary individuals face distinct barriers to respectful and high-quality WPH care that warrant dedicated study. While some experiences overlap across gender identities, grouping them together risked minimizing unique challenges. Future work should also broaden the scope beyond cervical and breast cancer screening and HPV vaccination to include services such as colorectal and osteoporosis screening, STI counseling, reproductive health discussions, domestic violence and mental health screening, cardiovascular risk reduction, and age-appropriate vaccinations.

Conclusions

This research provides insight into missed opportunities to care for WLHIV across a rural New England landscape with a focus on cervical and breast cancer prevention practices. The research offers a reminder of the opportunities to improve the delivery of women’s preventive healthcare to women living with HIV, particularly in rural settings, by addressing barriers to the provision and completion of cervical cancer screening, HPV vaccination, and breast cancer screening.
