Abstract

Background and Objectives

Circumstances more common with aging may challenge the ability for older adults to maintain appropriate housing. The objective of this scoping review was to identify the risk factors and conditions associated with homelessness in older adults.

Research Design and Methods

We searched OVID, CINAHL, Embase, PsycINFO, CENTRAL, and Social Work Abstracts from inception to February 2023. We included studies of older adults (defined as 50 years of age or older) or completed subgroup analyses accounting for older adults. We mapped the risk factors and conditions using an ecological framework (individual, relational, and structural/societal components).

Results

Sixty-five studies were included. Individual factors included identifying membership in a racialized group and experiencing psychiatric illnesses or substance use disorders. Relational factors included being unmarried, relationship breakdown, and adverse childhood events. Structural factors included the lack of affordable housing or income supports and systemic discrimination. Those experiencing first-onset late-life homelessness were more likely to be female, had relative lifelong socioeconomic stability, but experienced sudden relational or economic changes. Those experiencing chronic homelessness were more likely to be male, often facing persistent structural vulnerabilities with comorbid substance use or psychiatric disorders. Important research gaps include measures of social support and age- and sex-disaggregated analyses.

Discussion and Implications

Key individual, relational, and structural factors are associated with homelessness in older adults. Adverse experiences and structural factors, such as social losses and isolation, have important implications toward the risk of homelessness at different stages in life. Further research evaluating age-appropriate interventions is paramount.

Keywords: Homelessness, Successful aging, Ageism, Social roles and social factors

Homelessness among older adults is a rapidly growing concern, particularly in the United States, where individuals aged 50 and older represent the fastest-growing segment of the homeless population (Culhane et al., 2013). Reports from other high-income countries have shown similar trends, and in addition, that approximately one in five individuals experiencing homelessness are older adults (Employment and Social Development Canada, 2019; United Kingdom Government, 2023).

The rise of late-life homelessness cannot be attributed simply to population aging and the aging of individuals already experiencing homelessness. Instead, there are both a population of older adults who have experienced homelessness at younger ages and are now aging while experiencing homelessness, and a worrisome rise in individuals who are experiencing homelessness for the first time in late life. In fact, in Canada, 50% of homeless older adults are experiencing homelessness for the first time (Infrastructure Canada, 2023). Recent research highlights that economic conditions, housing market shifts, and policy changes have contributed to this trend (Culhane et al., 2013).

A nuanced understanding of the factors that make older adults vulnerable to homelessness—either for the first time or ending cyclical homelessness—is required to direct strategies to address this crisis. The pathway into homelessness is often the result of intertwining and intersecting disadvantages unique to the individual, which typically follow one of two trajectories: a gradual accumulation of risk factors over time—such as financial instability and chronic social isolation—or a sudden disruptive event, such as the loss of a partner or eviction, that precipitates a housing crisis (Burns & Sussman, 2019; Cohen, 1999; Grenier & Sussman, 2024). For older adults, these intersecting disadvantages, such as gender or race impacting economic opportunities, are often sustained and accumulate throughout the life course, leading to a cumulative disadvantage and heightened vulnerability to becoming or remaining homeless in later life. Adding to this are life events that become more common when aging, such as a housing transition (e.g., retirement home), a breakdown or decline in social networks, health status changes, the development of cognitive impairment and mobility issues, and experiences of ageism. While the thought of someone in their eighth decade of life, newly homeless, and navigating the shelter system for the first time seems unthinkable, this crisis is easily explained when the dots are connected—the inherent vulnerability of older adults is being experienced during a time when the population is aging, and the demand for affordable and supportive housing tailored to the needs of older adults far outweighs the supply (Weldrick & Canham, 2024). As such, while society values the idea of “aging in place,” older adults do not age equally, and the ability to age in place is not equally accessible to all, especially for those without a stable home.

There have been recent calls from national organizations to understand and reduce homelessness due to its negative health and social consequences, with an emerging focus on older adults (Baggett et al., 2011; Brown et al., 2012; Dobbins et al., 2024; Lewin Group, n.d.; Serchen et al., 2024; Weldrick & Canham, 2024). Previous research has identified important risk factors toward homelessness, including mental health conditions, substance use disorders, and adverse childhood events; however, these have been primarily studied amongst younger adults (Kelly, 2020; Nilsson et al., 2019; Schreiter et al., 2021). To better inform strategies, a more in-depth understanding of risk factors for homelessness among older adults is needed. As such, the objective of this study was to review the available evidence on quantitative and qualitative risk factors and conditions leading to homelessness in older adults and identify the gaps in the literature to guide future directions for researchers and policymakers.

Methods

We used the JBI Scoping Review Network methodology for our scoping reviews (Aromatis & Munn, 2020). Given the complex and heterogeneous nature of homelessness, we selected a scoping review to broadly understand its associated factors and conditions, in addition to the need to identify knowledge gaps. Our review was registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/a9tpj). We report our findings according to the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews and the Sex and Gender Equity in Research Guidelines (Heidari et al., 2016; Tricco et al., 2018) (Supplementary Checklist, see online supplementary material).

Eligibility criteria

We included research studies of all designs that included adults aged 50 years of age or older. We used a cutoff at 50 years of age, as this population is more likely to experience chronic health conditions that are associated with the traditional definition of older adults (e.g., 65 years of age and older) (Brown et al., 2012). The study was required to exclusively include older adults or complete subgroup analyses of older adults. We did not set any a priori restrictions on the risk factors. The context of interest was homelessness, which we operationalized as absolute homelessness (i.e., living on the street), living in a shelter, or unspecified (Gaetz et al., 2016). We did not place language restrictions in our search strategy. We excluded studies where housing was lost due to major natural events, as these are not representative of the general population experiencing homelessness. Eligible study designs included quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-/multiple-method studies.

Search strategy and selection criteria

The search was conducted in February 2023. We searched OVID Medline, CINAHL, Embase, PsycINFO, CENTRAL, and Social Work Abstracts from inception to February 2023. Our concepts included homelessness, older adults, and risk factors. We searched the references of included studies and previous reviews through backward citation searching (Supplementary Table 2, see online supplementary material) (Hirt et al., 2024). We included dissertations and abstracts, and for abstracts, we included the study if there was a subsequent publication. We searched gray literature from national governmental organizations. These included Canadian, American, Australian, and European sources and governmental websites for reports and briefs (Supplementary Table 2, see online supplementary material). We completed a search of Google Scholar for the first 500 articles (Supplementary Table 2, see online supplementary material) (Bramer et al., 2013). A search strategy can be found in Supplementary Table 1 (see online supplementary material). Our search strategy was reviewed by a health sciences librarian at the University of Toronto.

Protocol deviation

We initially sought to include the full spectrum of housing precarity, including transitional housing, social and supportive housing, and low-income housing, recognizing that these categories encompass individuals at risk of homelessness. However, during the screening process, we identified substantial heterogeneity in how homelessness was defined across studies, particularly concerning older adults. We refined our inclusion criteria to focus exclusively on studies that explicitly defined “absolute homelessness” and “shelter use.” This decision was made to improve consistency across included studies and address definitional discrepancies in the literature. Articles were not re-screened or re-abstracted following this protocol modification.

Data screening

We developed a priori screening criteria with team members (P.M.H., C.v.B., R.A.S., M.G., C.R.J.W., and V.D.) and completed a pilot test of a sample of 50 articles for abstract screening. Raters were first assigned as pairs and only permitted to continue if at least 90% agreement was met between the pilot of 50 randomly assigned abstracts. When inter-rater agreement was over 90%, pairs of reviewers independently screened abstracts, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion and, if necessary, a third reviewer. We stored citations from our search on Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, n.d.).

Data extraction and analysis

Data were extracted into Excel version 16.87 (Microsoft Corp) via a template that was piloted with team members (P.M.H., C.v.B., R.A.S., C.R.J.W., and Y.Q.H.). Data were extracted in duplicate. The following items were extracted: (1) study characteristics (author, publication year, country, setting, study design, number of participants, inclusion and exclusion criteria), (2) participant characteristics (age, psychiatric and medical comorbidities, participant’s primary language, income level, source of income, education level, occupation, history of incarceration, measure of social capital, religion, frailty, substance use history, hospital admissions in the past year, gender or sexual orientation (2SLGBTQ+), as part of the PROGRESS-Plus framework, and (3) description of homelessness and associated history of homelessness (definition of homelessness, first age and number of episodes of homelessness, shelter use frequency in the last 12 months, and last housing circumstances before experiencing homelessness). Where studies clearly reported women and men and reported collecting gender, or exclusively recruited women or men, we also extracted that as gender. For example, if a study reported gender as male and female, we did not extract that as the study having reported gender. We subsequently use women and men to indicate gender, and female and male to indicate biological sex. We used the PROGRESS-Plus framework to assess the reporting of social determinants of health identified by the World Health Organization (O’Neill et al., 2014). We included a history of incarceration, hospital admissions, and substance use history, as these have been shown to be risk factors for homelessness in individuals of all ages (John Howard, 2006).

For quantitative studies, we extracted descriptive conditions or the measure, strength, and direction of the association if there was a comparator, using the odds/risk ratio, 95% confidence interval, and p-values. For qualitative studies, we extracted the themes identified in qualitative studies relating to precipitants of homelessness. Where a measure of association was assessed statistically, we considered these to be risk factors, as the characteristic would be associated with an increased or decreased likelihood of homelessness; if the factor was described, these were considered conditions, with the latter encompassing descriptive and qualitative studies. In other words, as long as there was a comparator group defined, this would allow for a measure of association, but the study may not have necessarily reported a statistical measure (which would have been reported as Not Reported [NR]). When the comparator was used as a reference group to compare to other strata (e.g., females vs. males), this comparator was listed as Not Applicable (N/A). A measure was considered significant if the association had a p-value <.05 or the ratio did not cross the null value (e.g., 1.0). The extracted factors were grouped into structural components, defined as individual, relational, and structural/societal factors using concepts identified by Cohen, Grenier & Sussman, and an ecological framework (Cohen, 1999; Daaleman & Preisser, 2018; Fielding et al., 2010; Grenier & Sussman, 2024). We defined individual components as those that are demographic, physiological, or psychological in nature (e.g., age, sex [female or male], race [e.g., Black, Caucasian, Hispanic, Latino], or history of incarceration [e.g., imprisonment, criminal charges]). As only two studies indicated gender (women and men) as a quantitative risk factor, we grouped this into the overall quantitative findings for sex and gender and separated studies measuring gender as subgroup analyses. We defined relational components as immediate social relationships, which included adverse childhood events, marital status victimization (e.g., financial victimization, intimate partner violence), and social supports (Crane, 1998; Morris et al., 2005; Van Den Berk-Clark & McGuire, 2013). Structural components included the systematic service gaps and policies (e.g., income supports, social security, available affordable housing, and service programs) and urban/rural living (due to the structures in which these settings provide services and supports). We grouped military service/veteran status as a structural factor, as there are important confounders that can lead to structural inequities. While placement of military service under structural factors may be debated, we justify this based on systemic inequities and policy-level influences. This can include a history of childhood poverty, which has been found to be associated with future voluntary military service, as well as higher unemployment rates following certain global conflicts (e.g., following the September 11 attack period) (Bareis & Mezuk, 2016; Committee on the Assessment of the Readjustment Needs of Military Personnel et al., 2013). We recognize that this would also be affected by the policies present at different historical time periods, as World War II veterans are thought to have gained social mobility and labor market status following their return from service (Fredland & Little, 1985). We grouped urban and rural status into structural factors, as these are generally thought to be associated with healthcare disparities, including clinical care access, healthy lifestyle behaviors (e.g., food security), and income inequalities (Weeks et al., 2023).

We categorized multidimensional components (e.g., cumulative disadvantages) as structural due to their intersections of the individual’s needs and the systemic barriers that contribute to disadvantage. Definitions and the extracted ecological components can be found in Supplementary Table 3 (see online supplementary material). We recognize that there is overlap between risk factors and conditions across these groups and that the intersection of these factors must be contextualized within structures that lead to poverty and ageism (e.g., an individual’s factors of age, race, sex, and incarceration history are intimately related to the structural features that can lead to and propagate homelessness) (Grenier & Sussman, 2024). To highlight these important intersections, we further synthesized studies that identified a first age of homelessness, given the different trajectories among older adults who experience late-life homelessness. We completed sensitivity analyses based on whether the study was published before or after 2008, using the Great Recession to indicate global trends and policies. We used Google Translate for non-English articles. We calculated a weighted mean for the percentage female, based on the total number of participants experiencing homelessness in the study, where reported.

Results

Study characteristics

Sixty-five studies were included from 7,589 unique abstracts and 275 full-text articles (Figure 1). Of these, 45 studies were eligible for inclusion. Reference and gray literature searches identified 87 studies for review, of which 20 additional studies were included. Of the 65 studies, 47 were cross-sectional, and 18 were cohort studies. There were 41 quantitative studies, 14 qualitative studies, and 10 studies using mixed methods or multiple methods. Thirty-five studies had a comparator group. The sample sizes range from 10 to 7,477,757, including both homeless (range 5–256,576) and housed (range 29–7,420,017) individuals. Among the 809,907 individuals experiencing homelessness, the weighted female percentage was 31.70%. Forty-one studies were conducted in the United States, eight in Canada, and five both in the United Kingdom and Australia. The dates of publication ranged from 1991 to 2023. Study characteristics can be found in Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 4–7 (see online supplementary material). A summary of risk factors and conditions can be found in Figures 2 and 3.

Participant characteristics

Studies primarily reported psychiatric comorbidities: depression (n = 17), anxiety (n = 5), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (n = 12), schizophrenia or psychotic disorder (n = 18), alcohol use or use disorder (n = 22), drug use or drug use disorder (n = 14), opioid use disorder (n = 2), bipolar disorder (n = 4), personality disorder (n = 2), and gambling (n = 2). Measures of homelessness and previous housing

There was a large variation in how studies measured homelessness. These primarily included shelter users (n = 30) by self-reported measure or from shelter information systems. Other methods included administrative databases using International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes. Seven studies used a previous history to define homelessness, ranging from a history in the last 2 months to the last 5 years. Sixteen studies measured a first age of homelessness, primarily reported as a percentage before and after age 50, or only included individuals who had experienced homelessness following the age of 50. Only two studies reported the number of episodes an individual experienced homelessness (range: 1–3 and mean 3.1 [SD 4.0]; Van Den Berk-Clark & McGuire, 2013). Last housing prior to homelessness was measured in 21 studies, including rental properties (n = 10), staying with friends (n = 3), subsidized housing (n = 3), and hospitalization (n = 2). Fourteen studies indicated co-housing members in the last living setting prior to homelessness, which included (ex-partner/spouse, family, friends, and roommates. While 30 studies indicated that participants were using shelters, the frequency of shelter use was not reported. Measures of homelessness can be found in Table 1.

Individual risk factors and conditions

Among the 35 quantitative studies that had a comparator, stratified age groups included: age >50 (n = 2), age >55 (n = 2), age >60 (n = 1), and age >70 (n = 2); these were generally compared to those aged <50. There was no consistent association between age and the risk of homelessness, limited due to the strata designated in these studies. Sex was measured in 19 studies (female compared to male); males were generally more frequently described as experiencing homelessness. In two studies that measured gender and had a measure of association, one reported that being a man was associated with homelessness (Taylor et al., 2019). Race was identified as a risk factor for Black individuals (n = 17, 53% of studies reported a positive association) and, less consistently, Hispanic/Latino individuals (n = 10, 20% of studies reported a positive association); these were compared to Caucasian individuals. Psychiatric risk factors that were associated with homelessness included: depression (n = 7), PTSD (n = 7), schizophrenia (n = 10), anxiety disorder (n = 5), personality disorder (n = 3), alcohol use or alcohol use disorder (n = 11), and drug use or drug use disorder (n = 14). Having a high school education or less (n = 4) was associated with homelessness. There was also heterogeneity in the association of chronic medical conditions (diabetes, liver disease, renal disease, or any chronic non-psychiatric condition) with homelessness. One study identified that medical admissions were a preceding risk factor associated with older adults experiencing homelessness compared to middle-aged adults experiencing homelessness (Kimbler et al., 2017).

In cross-sectional descriptive studies or qualitative studies, the intersections of sex, gender, experiencing psychiatric conditions or substance use disorders, physical illness, cognitive impairment, and a history of incarceration were in an individual’s path to homelessness (Crane et al., 2005; Rota-Bartelink & Lipmann, 2007; Stergiopoulos & Herrmann, 2003; Sullivan, 1991; Van Den Berk-Clark & McGuire, 2013; Vandenberg et al., 2022). While men were reported to be more likely to experience homelessness overall (Rota-Bartelink & Lipmann, 2007), other studies that examined the experiences of women described older women as typically having a high level of education, but the conditions they experienced included limited income, financial hardship, and frequent diagnoses of schizophrenia (Butler & Weatherley, 1995; Marshall & Reed, 1992). Relational risk factors and conditions

Among the quantitative studies with a comparator, not being married (n = 11) and having adverse childhood experiences (n = 5) were associated with homelessness. Descriptive and qualitative studies identified conditions such as the death of a relative or close friend, breakdown of a marital or cohabitational relationship, eviction or disputes with landlords, adverse childhood events, domestic violence in adulthood, episodes of financial victimization in later life, and social marginalization (Burns & Sussman, 2019; Butler & Weatherley, 1995; Crane et al., 2005; McDonald et al., 2004; Mills-Dick, 2016; Morris et al., 2005; Okamura et al., 2023; Palmisano, 2022; Petersen & Parsell, 2015; Reynolds et al., 2016; Stergiopoulos & Herrmann, 2003; Van Den Berk-Clark & McGuire, 2013; Warnes & Crane, 2006). The absence of social support resulted in the loss of a buffer between abrupt changes in life; this condition was similarly reported in studies of older veterans (Acre, 2019; Burns & Sussman, 2019; Van Den Berk-Clark & McGuire, 2013).

Structural and societal risk factors and conditions

Quantitative studies with comparators identified urban living (n = 9) and military service (n = 7) as structural risk factors. Descriptive and qualitative studies further elaborated on structural conditions, including barriers to housing stability, limited employment opportunities, discrimination within institutions, and challenges faced by veterans in securing post-service benefits (Acre, 2019; Monari et al., 2020; Reynolds et al., 2016). One study that measured military history reported an age of first homelessness (Supplementary Table 9, see online supplementary material) and found that age was negatively associated with an incidence of homelessness identified from a healthcare encounter among veterans diagnosed with dementia (Jutkowitz et al., 2021); however, this may not indicate a first episode of homelessness due to the limitations of administrative health data. Among veterans, Montgomery et al. (2020) also indicated that those with mental health conditions and substance use disorders had a higher likelihood of being evicted. Housing affordability and systemic inequities were also recurrent conditions described in qualitative studies of all older adults experiencing homelessness. The inability to access affordable housing, particularly in the private rental market, was frequently cited (Australian Government, 2024; Warnes & Crane, 2006). Gendered economic disadvantages, such as the impact of unpaid caregiving on pension accumulation, were identified as key conditions affecting older women’s housing stability (Butler & Weatherley, 1995). While few studies specifically reported gender, we use the term gendered economic disadvantages to incorporate a high proportion of gender-based roles that disproportionately affect females (Global Report on Ageism, 2021; Saugeres, 2009). Among studies that reported a gendered experience, studies reported that women were vulnerable to marginalization (e.g., interpersonal violence, economic disparities), poor social supports, and ostracization in shelters (Burns, 2016; Cohen et al., 1997; Walsh et al., 2020). Additionally, transgender individuals faced compounded challenges to housing stability due to economic inequality and systemic oppression (Rodriguez et al., 2023).

Pathways based on the age of first homelessness

In studies that reported an index age of homelessness, men were more likely to have experienced homelessness earlier in life and have a longer duration of homelessness than women (Rota-Bartelink & Lipmann, 2007). The conditions that affected males included lifelong social isolation and limited contact with family, children, or ex-spouses (Morris et al., 2005). Studies from the HOPE HOME cohort identified the unique differences related to the age of first onset of homelessness, wherein those who experienced homelessness before the age of 50 were more likely to have been incarcerated and have comorbid substance use disorders or psychiatric illness (Brown et al., 2012; Crane et al., 2005). As such, older adults with a history of chronic homelessness often experience persistent structural vulnerabilities intersecting with individual and relational conditions. The majority of research on homelessness among older adults has focused on this group, leading to a concentration of findings on these central conditions.

Older adults who experience homelessness for the first time after age 50 present with a different set of risk factors and conditions. Their lives were often relatively stable before homelessness, characterized by long periods of employment and residential stability and, accordingly, a shorter duration of homelessness (Lee et al., 2016). This group is more likely to have become homeless due to a combination of economic and social shocks rather than long-standing structural vulnerabilities (Cohen, 2003; Crane et al., 2005). The conditions highlighted by descriptive and qualitative studies indicate that stressful life events such as the death of a spouse or partner, divorce, job loss, eviction, domestic violence, or the onset of significant health problems, coupled with fixed or limited incomes, were primary precipitants of homelessness in this group; these factors disproportionately affected females (Burns, 2016; Butler & Weatherley, 1995; Lee et al., 2016; Mills-Dick, 2016; Morris et al., 2005; Sullivan, 1991). These studies suggest that the first onset of late-life homelessness is typically a result of sudden losses within the context of structural vulnerabilities (Burns & Sussman, 2019; Van Den Berk-Clark & McGuire, 2013).

Sensitivity analyses of temporal trends

Due to the temporal policies that can affect housing supports, we also compared the risk factors based on whether the study was published before 2008 or after 2008. We selected 2008 as the Great Recession, as it had the largest global impacts, as policies otherwise fluctuate by country based on country-level economic and political dynamics. Studies following the year 2008 measured a greater number of factors, including military service, rural living, substance use disorders, PTSD, anxiety, depression, and certain age ranges (e.g., age greater than 70). After 2008, there was a greater proportion of studies reporting a positive association of identifying as a racialized minority and marital status. Before 2008, there was a greater proportion of studies reporting a positive association among females, adverse childhood events, and having a high school level of education or less to homelessness. A figure of the quantitative risk factors published before and after the year 2008 can be found in Supplementary Figure 1 (see online supplementary material).

Discussion

This scoping review of 65 studies aimed to identify the risk factors and conditions associated with experiencing homelessness as an older adult. Our review indicates that key individual, relational, and structural components are associated with homelessness among older adults, expanding on the known gradual and rapid pathways to homelessness and conceptualizing them using the components of an ecological framework (Brown et al., 2016). Our study also describes the different intersecting risk factors and conditions based on whether homelessness was experienced for the first time earlier or later in life. This framework allows for the distinction between chronic homelessness (long-term homelessness beginning earlier in life) and new-onset homelessness (first-time homelessness occurring in later life). Understanding these distinctions is critical, as the pathways leading to and experiences of homelessness differ substantially between these groups.

Among all older adults experiencing homelessness, there are overarching themes of structural (e.g., identifying as a racialized group, low socioeconomic status) and relational inequalities (e.g., adverse childhood events, limited social support, relationship breakdown) that contribute to homelessness. However, chronic and early-life homelessness is often rooted in early-life adversities, persistent poverty, and long-standing psychiatric and substance use disorders. In contrast, a first episode of homelessness experienced in later life is more frequently associated with later-life disruptions such as bereavement, financial exploitation, cognitive decline, or a sudden loss of stable housing.

Across all older adults experiencing homelessness, there was variation in the strength and direction of the association between age and sex and homelessness. Generally, in studies that compared older and younger adults, being younger was associated with homelessness. For example, an age greater than 65 was negatively associated with homelessness as compared to being 40 years or younger (Montgomery et al., 2020). These overall findings should be interpreted recognizing their limitations. First, these studies seldom reported an age of first homelessness and may sample from select environments (e.g., shelters) that may not accommodate the unique challenges of older adults (e.g., mobility and frailty), leading to selection bias (Baggett et al., 2013). Second, measurement bias can occur, as those experiencing hidden homelessness are more likely to be females, which would not be captured in these data (Schwan et al., 2020). We define hidden homelessness as situations opposite to homeless-specific settings and services (e.g., shelters, drop-in spaces); this can include staying with friends and family, motels, or using vehicles (Palmisano, 2022). Previous reviews have shown that women may experience hidden homelessness due to a multitude of factors—these may include specific settings that also accommodate children and reduce the risk of living on the street or using high-risk survival strategies (e.g., exploitative relationships) (Baptista, 2010; Schwan et al., 2020). These limitations may mask important sex, gender, and age disparities, and this research needs to be further interpreted in the context of studies that separate early and late-life homelessness.

As described in the qualitative studies, males were more likely to experience chronic homelessness, while females were more likely to experience homelessness in late life. There were also important gendered experiences (e.g., caregiving and economic marginalization) that affected women in later life. Further, important changes such as new-onset cognitive impairment and social changes (e.g., widowhood and caregiver roles) at specific points in life may disproportionately affect females in later life (Hurstak et al., 2017). The qualitative literature has consistently described the intersection between aging and homelessness, particularly in the experiences of social exclusion and gendered ageism among older adults (Burns & Sussman, 2019). While there may be a smaller proportion of individuals who are older and homeless compared to younger adults, these experiences of ageism can have important implications in how older adults become homeless and create barriers to exiting homelessness (Garibaldi et al., 2005). Irrespective of the underlying pathway, experiencing homelessness in late life has been shown to increase healthcare utilization and worsen physical and psychological health (Patanwala et al., 2018; Raven et al., 2017).

Many studies reported that identifying as a racialized group was associated with homelessness compared to being Caucasian; these have been well studied and are likely related to the structural inequalities that these minorities experience (Angel & Angel, 2006). Although Indigenous persons are overrepresented among homeless populations in Canada, none of the included Canadian studies explicitly identified Indigenous identity as a risk factor for homelessness among older adults. This absence may reflect limitations in how race and ethnicity were reported rather than the actual distribution of homelessness in this population. Furthermore, in Canada, it is known that Indigenous persons have a life expectancy up to 10 years lower than those who do not identify as Indigenous (Tjepkema et al., 2019). As such, Indigenous individuals may also not survive to late life. Given the historical and systemic factors contributing to housing instability among Indigenous peoples—including the impacts of colonialism, intergenerational trauma, and displacement—this omission represents a significant gap in the literature.

While some studies have identified that medical comorbidities and recent hospitalization were preceding events to homelessness, there was not a consistent association identified in the quantitative studies. This is in contrast to the qualitative studies, which described how medical illnesses and their associated costs led to the inability to maintain employment, suggesting that this may be insufficient on its own to lead to homelessness and may compound in the presence of other vulnerabilities (Gonzales et al., 2020). As an extension to this, multiple studies report a cumulative effect of multiple contributing factors (i.e., individual, relational, and structural) that lead to homelessness in later life; these are represented in the rapid and gradual pathways to homelessness (Burns & Sussman, 2019; Reynolds et al., 2016). This highlights the complex intersection of specific factors in the presence of other vulnerabilities that lead to homelessness.

Our review has identified important gaps in the literature. First, measures of social relationships are poorly identified in quantitative studies despite the pronounced impact described in qualitative studies. Most studies that measured social relationships used marital status, with few identifying other types of social networks. Of those that included other contacts, studies generally reported that few individuals experiencing homelessness regularly contacted their children or did not wish to burden their children (Butler & Weatherley, 1995). Older men who experienced homelessness were also less likely to have children. Some qualitative studies reported paradoxical relationships in shelters with other individuals experiencing homelessness (both positive and negative social connections). For example, some relationships resulted in feelings of support, others led to accusations and threats of violence (Palmisano, 2022). While most described social losses, some social supports included professionals (e.g., social workers) (Walsh et al., 2020). Second, only a small number of studies reported on cognitive impairment (n = 5) and frailty (n = 3), despite the relative prevalence expected among older adults. The association between dementia and homelessness has also been recently described (Hoang et al., 2026; Stone et al., 2019). Third, many studies that were included were from North America, which may reduce generalizability, particularly in the context of different national social security and healthcare plans. Fourth, while we attempted to understand policy-level temporal trends, studies generally did not report policy-level changes that may have occurred. It is possible that certain factors have become more prominent following 2008, including mental health disorders, identifying as a racialized minority, and greater age. These changes may potentially be due to austerity measures implemented following the crisis that led to greater disparities (Austerity in the Aftermath of the Great Recession | CEPR, n.d.). Finally, more studies measuring socioeconomic characteristics from the PROGRESS-Plus framework are needed to improve our understanding of how health inequities lead to homelessness.

There are several limitations to this study. While we aimed for inclusivity in our review, the feasibility of including studies in all languages was inherently constrained by our search strategy. Since our database searches were conducted in English, the vast majority of retrieved articles were either published in English or had English-language abstracts. Though we had planned to use translation tools, given these limitations, we acknowledge that our review is likely skewed toward English-language research. This is an important consideration for the interpretation of our findings and a potential area for future systematic reviews with dedicated multilingual strategies. We had initially aimed to identify risk factors associated with homelessness across its spectrum (i.e., from those at risk of homelessness to absolute homelessness). Due to the heterogeneity in how “at risk” and transitional/supportive housing were measured, these were not included in the review; this represents an important area of future study. Further, few studies described the frequency and intensity of homelessness. This limits our ability to understand whether a dose–response relationship exists with any of these risk factors. For example, some factors may predispose individuals to a longer duration of homelessness than others (e.g., severe psychosis or loss of income and social networks), compared to others (e.g., landlord–tenant disputes). Finally, these studies were observational, and factors may be prone to bias. This may include residual confounding and reverse causality. For example, military history/veteran status may be strongly confounded by access to mental health services, benefit models, and employment opportunities (Bond et al., 2022). In addition, homelessness was frequently identified from ICD codes. These can be prone to misclassification and are generally specific but not sensitive (Richard et al., 2019). We hypothesize that these will generally be biased toward a higher degree of comorbidities and social risk factors (e.g., social isolation), as these are individuals who would be identified from frequent healthcare encounters and have limited social supports. This misclassification is expected to bias away from females and women due to a higher frequency of hidden homelessness, as described earlier (Schwan et al., 2020).

Our research fills a critical knowledge gap for older adults who experience absolute homelessness and use shelters. We aimed to group risk factors identified in these studies while maintaining generalizability. Although we did not a priori define risk factors, we made groupings upon review of articles for extraction and analysis, to which a framework was then applied (Daaleman & Preisser, 2018b; Kuh et al., 2014). As a result, certain factors that were unique to countries (e.g., insurance levels for American veterans) were not synthesized. As described above, the quantitative studies that analyzed older adults used age subgroups with incomparable categories. This may affect the ability to specifically learn about the unique experiences of older adults; however, the factors identified are still relevant to older adults. Though most studies did not report gender, the experience of gendered ageism remained applicable to this study (Global Report on Ageism, 2021).

To translate these findings into meaningful action, a multi-pronged approach is required at the policy and practice levels. First, expanding housing programs that specifically address the needs of older adults—such as subsidized housing with integrated health and social services or improving long-term care eligibility—can mitigate the structural barriers contributing to homelessness. Second, proactive screening for housing instability within healthcare and social service settings, particularly for individuals with cognitive impairment, limited social networks, recent bereavement, or financial vulnerability, can help identify at-risk individuals before they experience homelessness. Third, policies should prioritize Indigenous-led housing and support initiatives to address the disproportionate representation of Indigenous adults in homeless populations. Lastly, strengthening coordination between healthcare, housing, and social services through integrated case management models can provide a safety net for older adults at risk of homelessness. Future research is paramount to evaluate the effectiveness and sustainability of these interventions.
