Several interconnected social, institutional, and environmental factors influence harassment and bullying of transgender people in educational settings.
## Social and Cultural Norms

* Cisnormativity and Heteronormativity: Societal assumptions that a person’s gender identity must align with their sex assigned at birth ([cisnormativity](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-dUPODTWevk)) create rigid expectations. Students who express a gender different from traditional expectations face a higher risk of targeting ([GLSEN National School Climate Survey via GoodTherapy](https://www.goodtherapy.org/blog/how-to-protect-transgender-kids-from-bullying-at-school-1211184)).
* Transphobia and Stigma: General animus or fear toward transgender individuals manifests as verbal abuse, social exclusion, cyberbullying, and physical aggression from peers. [1, 2] 

## Institutional and Policy Factors

* Lack of Inclusive Policies: Schools without explicit non-discrimination protections or clear guidelines for using affirmed names, pronouns, and single-sex facilities (like bathrooms and locker rooms) often experience higher rates of harassment. Enforced restrictions on pronouns or bathrooms correlate with increased vulnerability. [2, 3] 
* Staff Response and Preparedness: A disconnect often exists between student experiences and staff awareness; teachers and administrators may fail to recognize subtle or severe bullying, or they may lack training to intervene effectively. Institutional victimization can also occur if school rules or staff behaviors reinforce harmful stereotypes. [1, 4] 
* Broader Political and Legislative Climate: Coordinated campaigns and legislative debates targeting transgender rights influence local school climates, sometimes emboldening discriminatory behavior or increasing administrative challenges in protecting students. [5] 

## Environmental and Protective Factors

* School Climate and Socioeconomic Context: Research indicates that trans youth attending more affluent schools or schools with higher levels of college-educated parents experience lower overall rates of victimization, potentially due to different resource allocations or community norms. [6] 
* Family and Community Support: Strong support systems at home and within the community serve as vital protective buffers, decreasing the psychological distress and negative academic impacts associated with school victimization. [6]
