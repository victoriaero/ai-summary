Several critical factors influence housing access and stability for transgender people, ranging from legal frameworks and direct discrimination to economic and social hurdles. [1, 2] 
## Discrimination and Direct Exclusion

* Housing Discrimination: According to the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), transgender individuals frequently report being refused apartments, quoted higher prices, or outright denied housing based on their gender identity and expression. [3, 4] 
* Identification Barriers: Landlords and housing programs often require legal identity documents—such as driver's licenses or birth certificates—that do not match a person's lived gender identity or presentation, creating administrative and exclusionary barriers. [1] 
* Shelter Access and Safety: Transgender people seeking emergency housing face high rates of harassment, mistreatment, or outright rejection from shelters that fail to accommodate them according to their gender identity. [5, 6] 

## Legal Protections and Policy Shifts

* Lack of Comprehensive Federal Laws: There is no uniform federal statute explicitly prohibiting housing discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity. Protection heavily depends on state or local laws, leaving nearly half of LGBTQ+ individuals living in states without explicit housing discrimination protections. [2, 7] 
* Regulatory Changes: Federal guidelines governing inclusive access—such as HUD’s Equal Access Rule—have experienced shifting enforcement and proposed rollbacks that influence whether federally funded shelters are mandated to serve transgender individuals safely and equitably. [6, 8] 

## Economic and Financial Insecurity

* Affordability and Rent Burdens: Gender-expansive and transgender households are statistically more likely to rent rather than own homes and often spend a disproportionate amount of their income on housing, elevating their risk of eviction. [1, 9] 
* Lower Homeownership Rates: Historical and systemic economic disparities contribute to significantly lower homeownership rates among transgender adults compared to cisgender populations. [9] 

## Social and Familial Support

* Family Rejection: Rejection by family members—particularly during youth and adolescence after coming out—disproportionately drives young transgender individuals into homelessness and housing instability early in life. [10, 11]
