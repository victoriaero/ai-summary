Several key structural, clinical, and social factors influence preventive care access and outcomes for transgender individuals.
## Provider Knowledge and Cultural Competence

* Lack of training: Many healthcare providers lack education on transgender health, leading to missed screening opportunities or refusal of care. [1] 
* Ininclusive environments: Fear of discrimination, disrespect, or judgmental attitudes from staff often causes patients to delay or avoid routine checkups and cancer screenings. [1, 2] 

## Anatomical and Physiological Considerations

* Anatomy-based screening: Preventive screenings (such as Pap smears, mammograms, or prostate checks) depend on a patient's current organs, not their gender identity. Misconceptions by providers or patient discomfort can disrupt these necessary tests. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] 
* Hormone therapy impacts: Gender-affirming hormone therapy can alter cardiovascular, metabolic, and bone health risks, requiring tailored monitoring (such as lipid panels or checks for blood clots). [3, 5, 6] 

## Structural and Policy Barriers

* Insurance coverage: Transgender people experience higher rates of being uninsured or underinsured compared to the general population. [7, 8] 
* State-level policies: Regional and state-level policy environments affect utilization; disparities in preventive health services are often wider in non-affirming states than in gender-affirming states. [9] 
* Social determinants: Stable housing, adequate employment, and supportive public health infrastructures strongly correlate with better engagement in routine medical care. [8]
