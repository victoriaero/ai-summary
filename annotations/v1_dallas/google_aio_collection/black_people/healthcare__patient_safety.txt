Systemic racism, implicit bias among clinicians, and unequal access to high-quality hospitals significantly influence patient safety for Black people in healthcare.Systemic and Institutional FactorsHospital Quality Disparities: Black patients are disproportionately admitted or referred to lower-rated hospitals that often lack optimal resources and have higher rates of patient safety incidents, such as postoperative complications.Under-resourced Facilities: Facilities that predominantly serve Black communities often experience structural and financial constraints that correlate with higher rates of hospital-acquired infections and surgical complications.Data Tracking Deficiencies: Many healthcare institutions fail to collect or stratify race-specific patient safety data, preventing the identification and correction of safety disparities.Interpersonal and Clinician FactorsImplicit and Conscious Bias: Provider bias leads to the dismissal of symptoms, under-treatment of pain, misdiagnoses, and longer emergency department wait times for Black patients.Clinical Design Limitations: Medical training, diagnostic tools, and reference materials have historically been designed around white patient populations, leading to delayed recognition of conditions like rashes or skin infections on darker skin.Communication Gaps: Ineffective communication and a lack of cultural competence from clinicians reduce consumer engagement and patient safety.Patient and Community FactorsMedical Mistrust: Past and present mistreatment fosters valid medical mistrust, which can lead patients to delay seeking care, avoid speaking up about safety events, or feel the need to prepare for unfair treatment.Provider Diversity Deficiencies: The severe underrepresentation of Black
