Several socioeconomic, geographic, and systemic factors influence access to banking and credit services for Black individuals and communities in the United States.Geographic and Physical ProximityBank Branch Deserts: Majority-Black neighborhoods have fewer physical bank branches compared to non-Black or majority-White neighborhoods. Research from institutions like the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston and Yale Insights highlights that a lack of nearby physical branches remains a primary barrier to consistent banking access.Reliance on Alternative Services: Due to physical distance from mainstream banks, Black consumers are more likely to depend on costly alternative financial institutions, such as payday lenders and check-cashing counters, which charge higher service fees.Credit Scoring and Underwriting BarriersLower Average Credit Scores: Due to broader systemic and historical economic disparities, Black individuals are statistically more likely to have lower credit scores, leading to higher rates of exclusion from conventional financial services.Algorithmic and Screening Bias: Traditional FICO and risk-assessment models often act as restrictive screening tools.
