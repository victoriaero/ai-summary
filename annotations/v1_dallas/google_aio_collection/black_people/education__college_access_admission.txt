Several systemic, legal, and socioeconomic factors significantly influence college access and admission for Black students in the United States.Legal and Policy ChangesAffirmative Action Rulings: The U.S. Supreme Court's 2023 decision prohibited colleges from explicitly considering race in admissions, leading to drops in Black student enrollment at many predominantly white institutions (PWIs).Legacy Preferences: Admissions advantages given to the children of alumni disproportionately benefit white applicants whose families have multi-generational attendance histories, limiting structural space for diverse candidates.Institutional and State Bans: Restrictions on diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) programs and race-based scholarships in various states reduce targeted outreach and financial support.K–12 Academic Preparation and ResegregationUnequal School Funding: Housing wealth disparities and resegregated public schools mean students of color often attend under-resourced K–12 facilities with higher student-to-teacher and student-to-counselor ratios.Coursework Availability: Limited access to advanced placement (AP) or rigorous college-preparatory classes hinders competitive eligibility for selective universities.Support Deficits: Overburdened school counselors have less time to write contextualized recommendation letters or guide students through complex application requirements.Socioeconomic and Financial BarriersFAFSA Complications: Errors, delays, and technical hurdles in financial aid rollouts disproportionately disrupt application plans for low-income students and students of color.Affordability and Debt: Prohibitive tuition prices and financial uncertainties push many Black applicants toward lower-cost options or discourage attendance entirely.Institutional Culture and AlternativesRole of HBCUs: Historically Black Colleges and Universities offer targeted mentorship, supportive environments, and higher rates of upward economic mobility, serving as vital access points.Holistic and Contextual Reviews: Some institutions evaluate applicants using contextual data (such as high school performance relative to neighborhood
