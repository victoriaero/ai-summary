A combination of academic preparation, alternative economic paths, and shifting enrollment trends heavily influences college access and admission for men.
Academic Preparation and Performance
• High school grades and test scores: On average, female high school graduates achieve higher overall GPAs than males.
• Reading and writing skills: Boys often face disadvantages in reading comprehension early in schooling, which impacts long-term academic alignment and standardized test performance in reading and writing on assessments like the SAT and ACT.
• Immediate enrollment rates: Fewer male high school graduates transition directly to college compared to female graduates.
Economic and Career Alternatives
• Skilled trades and manufacturing: Many young men opt out of traditional four-year degrees because viable routes to the middle class exist through technical training, manufacturing, and trade professions.
• Perception of value: A growing public narrative questions the immediate financial necessity and high expense of a traditional college degree.
Socioeconomic and Family Background
• Parental education and structure: Children from single-parent households or families where parents lack a college degree experience lower rates of high school completion and college enrollment.
• Socioeconomic status: Income levels and regional geography heavily dictate which students apply to and access selective versus open-enrollment campuses.
Institutional and Policy Factors
• Recruitment and outreach: Open-enrollment and regional colleges increasingly tailor mentorship and campus programs specifically to re-engage underrepresented male applicants, including men of color and rural students.
• Admissions policy shifts: Policy changes—such as legal challenges or restrictions regarding diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) initiatives—can alter how institutions legally target or support specific underrepresented demographics on campus, potentially impacting male recruitment strategies.
