Harassment and bullying in educational settings are driven by a combination of individual traits, institutional environments, and broader social dynamics.
Individual and Personal Characteristics
• Perceived Differences: Students or staff who are viewed as different—such as those with distinct ethnic, linguistic, cultural, or religious backgrounds—face higher targets of discrimination.
• Physical Appearance and Disability: Physical traits are among the most common catalysts for teasing and abuse, alongside physical, cognitive, or learning disabilities.
• Gender and Identity: Non-conformity to traditional gender norms, stereotypes, and expressions often triggers gender-policing and targeted harassment.
Social and Interpersonal Dynamics
• Power Imbalances: Bullying is fundamentally rooted in perceived power disparities. In peer groups, this relates to social status or physical dominance; in academic careers, it often involves hierarchical power where senior faculty, professors, or supervisors hold immense leverage over students or junior staff.
• Digital Behavior and Anonymity: In the context of cyberbullying, factors like internet addiction, excessive social media access, and online anonymity increase aggressive behavior and vulnerability to privacy attacks.
Institutional and Environmental Factors
• School Climate and Policies: Institutions that lack clear anti-bullying policies, fail to enforce regulations, or maintain weak disciplinary management foster environments where aggressive behavior goes unchecked.
• Resource and Support Gaps: Overburdened staff, high turnover, and inadequate mental health or social services limit an institution's capacity to prevent and properly address incidents.
• Family and Community Context: Parental involvement, family dynamics, and neighborhood safety significantly influence student conduct and coping resources within the school ecosystem.
