Housing stability for people without general or physical disabilities is primarily driven by financial affordability, steady employment, and housing tenure type. When households spend a manageable portion of their income on shelter, they are far less likely to face eviction or foreclosure.
Financial Affordability and Income
• Cost Burden: Households face high instability if they spend more than 30% of their income on housing, and severe risk if they spend over 50%.
• Income Level: Low or unpredictable income limits choices, leaving families with fewer resources for food, medicine, and emergencies.
• Assistance Access: Federal programs like HUD Section 8 provide vital rental assistance that lowers cost burdens.
Housing Tenure (Renting vs. Owning)
• Renting Risks: Renters experience higher rates of housing insecurity and displacement compared to homeowners.
• Market Pressures: Rising rent prices in tight private markets force frequent moves or overcrowding.
• Homeownership Security: Owning a home generally offers long-term fixed costs and builds equity, shielding occupants from sudden rent spikes.
Employment and Economic Security
• Job Stability: Steady employment provides the predictable cash flow required to make monthly rent or mortgage payments on time.
• Economic Shocks: Job loss, reduced hours, or unexpected medical debt quickly drain savings and trigger missed payments.
Rental History and Legal Barriers
• Eviction Records: Past evictions make finding a new landlord difficult and lead to a cycle of unstable housing.
• Criminal Justice History: A history of incarceration often disqualifies individuals from public housing and prompts landlord rejections.
Neighborhood Quality and Environment
• Substandard Conditions: Low-cost options sometimes feature structural damage, mold, or leaks that threaten well-being.
• Community Resources: Access to transit, grocery stores, and stable schools supports long-term residency and reduces stress.
