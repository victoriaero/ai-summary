Several primary factors influence housing access for people with disabilities, including affordability, physical accessibility, and discrimination. [1] 
## Affordability and Income

* Low fixed incomes: Many individuals with disabilities rely on programs like Supplemental Security Income (SSI), which often fails to cover market-rate rent. [2, 3] 
* Cost burdens: People with disabilities are twice as likely to have low incomes and frequently spend more than 30% or 50% of their income on housing, raising the risk of eviction. [4, 5] 
* Healthcare and medical debt: High out-of-pocket medical expenses reduce the funds available for rent and daily necessities. [1, 6] 

## Physical Inaccessibility

* Scarcity of accessible units: Less than 5% of the overall housing stock in the United States meets basic physical accessibility standards. [7] 
* Older buildings: Much of the affordable and federally assisted housing was built before modern accessibility laws like the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and lacks elevators, ramps, or modified bathrooms. [8, 9] 
* Modification barriers: Landlords may deny requests to modify living spaces or fail to perform necessary structural updates. [2, 10] 

## Discrimination and Systemic Policies

* Landlord bias: Housing providers may hold negative assumptions about disabled tenants or refuse reasonable accommodations, such as allowing service animals. [2, 10] 
* Complex application systems: Navigating fragmented housing waitlists and burdensome accommodation paperwork creates a difficult "housing labyrinth". [9] 
* Intersectional disparities: Disabled people of color face compounded barriers, experiencing higher rates of housing instability and discrimination based on both race and disability. [5, 11]
