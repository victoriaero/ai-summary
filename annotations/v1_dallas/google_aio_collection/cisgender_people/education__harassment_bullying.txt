While cisgender students face lower rates of identity-based bullying compared to transgender and nonbinary peers, harassment and bullying among cisgender individuals in education are primarily driven by gender-atypical expression, sexual orientation, racial intersections, and institutional dynamics.Key Factors Influencing Bullying for Cisgender Students1. Gender Typicality and Non-ConformityCisgender students are frequently targeted if they do not conform to traditional societal expectations of masculinity or femininity.Perceived Gender Roles: A cisgender boy perceived as "too feminine" or a cisgender girl perceived as "too masculine" experiences significantly higher rates of verbal abuse, exclusion, and harassment.Male-Dominated Fields: In higher education, cisgender women in heavily male-dominated academic disciplines (such as engineering) experience significantly higher rates of crude gender harassment and unwanted attention from peers compared to those in gender-balanced fields.2. Sexual Orientation and "Perceived" IdentitiesBias-based bullying strongly targets cisgender students who identify as—or are merely perceived to be—part of a sexual minority.Cisgender lesbian, gay, bisexual, or queer (LGBQ) students face vastly elevated rates of verbal insults, homophobic name-calling, and social isolation compared to heterosexual cisgender students.Even heterosexual cisgender students can become targets of homophobic bullying if peers falsely assume their sexual orientation based on social circles or hobbies.3. Intersectional DemographicsThe risk and severity of bullying change when gender identity intersects with other demographic factors:Race and Ethnicity: Cisgender students of color face different baseline risks; certain surveys indicate varying reporting rates of school bullying across white, multiracial, Indigenous, Black, and Hispanic student populations.Socioeconomic Status: Educational environments in higher-income areas or schools with higher parent education levels generally correlate with lower overall victimization rates.4. School Climate and Institutional ResponseThe physical and cultural environment of an educational institution heavily regulates how frequently bullying occurs.Lack of Consequence: A major factor perpetuating harassment is the student perception that school staff will not intervene effectively. When cisgender students believe reporting does nothing, underreporting increases, allowing hostile behavior to persist.Affirmative Policies: Schools lacking strict, comprehensive anti-bullying guidelines or diversity frameworks inadvertently foster environments where social exclusion and relational aggression thrive.
