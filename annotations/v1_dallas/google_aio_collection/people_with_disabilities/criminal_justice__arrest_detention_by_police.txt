Several key factors influence the arrest and detention of people with disabilities by police within the criminal justice system.
Misinterpretation of Behaviors
• Hidden Disabilities: Intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD) or autism are often not immediately noticeable to law enforcement officers.
• Behavioral Cues: Symptoms or behaviors related to conditions like autism, sensory overloads, or mental health crises (such as repetitive movements, lack of eye contact, or fleeing) are frequently misinterpreted as noncompliance, aggression, or dangerous behavior.
• Escalation: Misinterpretations by officers who lack specialized training can quickly escalate routine encounters into physical confrontations, leading to additional charges like resisting arrest.
Communication Barriers
• Expressive and Receptive Deficits: Individuals with speech, hearing, cognitive, or neurological disabilities may struggle to process verbal commands, answer questions quickly, or express basic needs.
• Lack of Accommodations: The immediate absence of alternative communication methods—such as visual aids, written text, or sign language interpreters—often results in misunderstandings during high-stress police stops.
Systemic and Intersectional Biases
• Racial Disparities: The risk of arrest is significantly higher for disabled individuals who also hold minoritized racial identities, particularly Black and brown people with disabilities.
• The "Disability Penalty": Research indicates that people with disabilities face a substantially higher cumulative likelihood of arrest by young adulthood compared to their non-disabled peers, compounded by systemic policing patterns in vulnerable communities.
• Over-Policing of Mental Health: Law enforcement is frequently the default first responder to mental health crises due to a lack of robust community-based social and medical service alternatives.
