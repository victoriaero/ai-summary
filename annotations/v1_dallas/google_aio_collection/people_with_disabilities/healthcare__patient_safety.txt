Multiple structural, clinical, and interpersonal factors influence patient safety for people with disabilities in healthcare settings.
Provider Knowledge and Bias
• Diagnostic Overshadowing: Clinicians often mistakenly attribute a patient's new or worsening symptoms entirely to their pre-existing disability rather than investigating a separate, treatable health condition.
• Training Gaps: Many healthcare professionals lack formal education on how to care for patients with specific physical, sensory, or intellectual disabilities.
• Attitudinal Barriers: Implicit bias and negative stereotypes among staff can lead to dismissive communication, mismanaged challenging behavior, or inappropriate non-treatment decisions.
Communication Barriers
• Lack of Accessible Formats: Information is frequently not provided in plain language, Braille, large print, or via sign language interpreters and digital tools.
• Exclusion of Caregivers: Providers may fail to involve trusted family members or support workers who understand the patient's baseline behavior and communication cues.
Physical and Environmental Inaccessibility
• Inadequate Equipment: Facilities often lack height-adjustable examination tables, accessible weight scales, patient lifts, or specialized dental chairs.
• Structural Obstacles: Narrow doorways, heavy doors, broken elevators, and poor signage prevent safe navigation within medical offices.
Systemic and Clinical Risks
• Care Coordination Gaps: Preventive services and multi-specialty follow-ups are harder to schedule and coordinate, leading to missed screenings and delayed treatments.
• Basic Care Omissions: Hospitalized patients with intellectual or developmental disabilities face heightened risks of medication errors, missed basic nursing care, falls, and pressure sores.
