Students with disabilities are two to three times more likely to be bullied than their peers, influenced by a combination of individual vulnerability, social dynamics, and environmental factors.
Individual and Behavioral Factors
• Physical and Functional Vulnerability: Students may have physical traits or medical needs (such as cerebral palsy, epilepsy, or diabetes) that make them stand out or require visible accommodations, making them targets for intimidation.
• Social and Communication Challenges: Difficulties in reading social cues, misinterpreting interactions, or having limited self-regulation and communication skills can lead to unintentional social missteps that peers exploit.
• Behavioral and Emotional Difficulties: Conditions involving emotional disturbances, Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), or Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) often bring behavioral differences or visible frustration responses that elevate the risk of victimization.
• Multiple Conditions: Individuals managing a combination of multiple disabilities face an even higher statistical risk of being targeted.
Social and Environmental Factors
• Power Imbalances: Bullying relies on power, and students with intellectual, physical, or emotional limitations are frequently perceived as less capable of defending themselves.
• Lack of Peer Support and Protection Networks: The absence of supportive friends or a protective social circle leaves vulnerable students isolated and easier targets.
• Intolerant or Unprepared Environments: Schools that fail to foster inclusive cultures—or rely on anti-bullying programs that are not adapted or accessible for students with varying intellectual or reading levels—allow hostile behaviors to persist.
• Adult Misconceptions: When school staff minimize bullying as "kids being kids" rather than addressing disability-based harassment under civil rights guidelines like Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, the behavior goes unchecked.
