AI OVERVIEW TEXT
============================================================

Access to credit for people with disabilities is heavily influenced by systemic income disparities, lower average credit scores, digital and physical accessibility barriers, and lingering institutional discrimination.
Income and Employment Disparities
• Lower earnings: People with disabilities face higher rates of unemployment and underemployment, leading to lower overall household incomes.
• Fixed benefit reliance: Many rely on fixed incomes from programs like Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) or Supplemental Security Income (SSI), which traditional lenders may misinterpret or undervalue during underwriting.
• Higher medical expenses: Ongoing costs related to healthcare, personal care assistants, and adaptive equipment reduce disposable income and the capacity to absorb financial shocks.
Credit Health and Scoring Gaps
• Lower credit scores: Data shows that adults with disabilities report lower credit scores across all income levels compared to non-disabled peers.
• Reliance on high-cost credit: Due to limited access to mainstream bank loans, people with disabilities turn to alternative financial services—such as payday loans, pawn shops, and title loans—at significantly higher rates.
• Credit invisibility: A lack of traditional credit-building tools or difficulty reporting non-traditional payments (like utility or rent payments) keeps some consumers from establishing a robust credit history.
Physical and Technological Barriers
• Inaccessible digital interfaces: Many banking websites, mobile applications, and online loan portals fail to comply with basic digital accessibility standards (such as screen-reader compatibility).
• Physical infrastructure: In-person branch visits can be challenging due to inaccessible physical locations, lack of accessible ATMs, or transportation barriers.
• Communication obstacles: A lack of alternative communication formats (such as Braille, large print, sign language interpreters, or telecommunications relay services) restricts informed financial decision-making.
Institutional Stigma and Regulatory Gaps
• Implicit bias and discrimination: Despite protections under laws like the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) in the United States—which prohibits discrimination against applicants receiving public assistance income—lenders sometimes exhibit bias against disability-related income or medical histories.
• Inflexible underwriting criteria: Standardized risk models often fail to account for the unique financial rhythms of disability benefits, leading to improper credit denials or overly restrictive loan terms.
