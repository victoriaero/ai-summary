Socioeconomic status, income levels, and geographic proximity to bank branches are primary factors influencing access to banking and financial services for non-Latino populations (such as Black and low-income white communities).
Key Factors Influencing Access
• Income and Wealth Disparities: Low socioeconomic status (SES) and low household income heavily restrict individuals from opening bank accounts or qualifying for affordable credit products. Low-income consumers often cannot meet minimum balance requirements or afford high monthly maintenance fees.
• Geographic and Physical Presence: The profitability models of traditional banks mean financial institutions often bypass vulnerable, low-income, or rural neighborhoods, creating "bank deserts" where physical branches are scarce.
• Racial and Structural Disparities: Data from organizations like the Joint Economic Committee shows that Black and low-income Americans face disproportionate gaps in basic financial access compared to higher-income white counterparts, driving them toward costly alternative financial services like payday loans or check-cashing outfits.
• Credit History and Scoring Gaps: A lack of traditional credit or past negative banking experiences (such as overdraft fees or past involuntary account closures) can lock individuals out of mainstream banking, forcing reliance on second-chance or non-bank alternatives.
• Digital and Internet Access: Disparities in reliable internet and digital infrastructure influence whether consumers can successfully use mobile and online banking tools to lower costs and manage financial health.
