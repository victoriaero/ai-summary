Access to government benefits for women is primarily influenced by structural economic factors, caregiving responsibilities, and policy design.
Economic and Employment Factors
- The Gender Wage Gap: Because many contributory benefits (such as Social Security or unemployment insurance) calculate payouts based on lifetime earnings or active contributions, lower average earnings for women directly result in lower benefit amounts .
- Part-Time and Informal Work: Women are statistically more likely to work in part-time, low-wage, or informal sector jobs that often lack employer-provided safety nets or fail to meet minimum contribution thresholds for certain public programs.
- Career Interruptions: Time taken out of the paid workforce for childbearing or continuous employment gaps reduces total contribution years, depressing future retirement and social insurance benefits.
Caregiving and Social Norms
- Unpaid Care Work: Women shoulder a disproportionate share of domestic labor, childcare, and eldercare. Policy frameworks that fail to credit or accommodate caregiving roles create major hurdles in qualifying for or sustaining access to benefits .
- The "Motherhood Penalty": Taking extended leave or reducing work hours after having children permanently lowers cumulative earnings and future benefit calculations.
Policy Design and Administrative Barriers
- Eligibility Rules: Strict asset tests or income thresholds can exclude single mothers or low-income women who earn slightly above arbitrary cutoffs but still experience financial precarity.
- Intersectionality: Systemic inequalities compound for women of color, immigrant women, and women with disabilities, who face overlapping hiring discrimination, wealth gaps, and heightened structural barriers when navigating administrative systems.
