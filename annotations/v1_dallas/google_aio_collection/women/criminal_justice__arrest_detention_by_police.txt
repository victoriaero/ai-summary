Several legal, situational, and social factors influence police decisions to arrest or detain women in the criminal justice system.
Legal and Situational Factors
- Offense Severity: Just as with male suspects, the gravity of the alleged crime (felony versus misdemeanor) is a primary driver for police action.
- Type of Crime: Women are more frequently arrested or detained for non-violent offenses—such as property crimes, low-level drug offenses, or shoplifting—than for violent crimes. Survival-related offenses like sex work also lead to frequent police contact.
- Mandatory Arrest Policies: In domestic violence calls, strict jurisdictional laws often require police to make an arrest if there is probable cause, regardless of the primary aggressor's gender or whether the female involved is a victim defending herself.
Social and Intersectional Factors
- Race and Ethnicity: Data from organizations like the Prison Policy Initiative shows significant racial disparities; Black women and other women of color experience higher rates of stops, searches, and arrests compared to white women .
- Demeanor and Stereotyping: Sociological research indicates that women who conform to traditional gender norms may receive more leniency (the "chivalry effect"). Conversely, women who appear hostile, are under the influence of alcohol or drugs, or deviate from expected behavioral stereotypes are treated more harshly and face a higher likelihood of arrest.
- Underlying Health and Social Crises: Police frequently encounter women undergoing mental health crises, homelessness, or substance addiction. Rather than being diverted to community health services, women are sometimes arrested for minor public-order infractions (such as being drunk and disorderly or begging) when other social safety net services fail.
