Female officers generally use physical and lethal force less frequently than male officers, a dynamic influenced by communication styles, situational variables, and departmental culture.
Research from organizations like the National Institute of Justice and criminology studies highlight several key factors influencing the use of force as it relates to women in policing—both as officers and as suspects:
Officer Gender and Communication Style
- De-escalation Skills: Female officers often rely on advanced problem-solving and verbal de-escalation skills rather than physical dominance, which helps resolve confrontations without escalating the use of force continuum .
- Lower Rates of Physical Force: Studies show that female officers—and particularly all-female partner pairs—report lower rates of physical and excessive force compared to male counterparts.
- Narrower Force Spectrum: Research analyzing officer response relative to suspect resistance suggests that female officers operate within a more measured or narrower range of physical coercion before calling for backup or utilizing non-lethal tools like Tasers.
Situational and Suspect Dynamics
- Type of Call: The nature of the call dictates response intensity; for instance, studies note that female officers may face unique resistance levels or higher hostility during domestic violence calls.
- Suspect Demographics and Intersectionality: Data indicates that the probability of physical intervention or searches can vary based on suspect gender and race, with women of color often experiencing disproportionate rates of police stops and use of force relative to white women.
- Presence of Backup: The composition of the responding team (mixed-gender vs. single-gender units) alters on-scene behavior, accountability, and the collective threshold for applying physical restraint.
Organizational and Cultural Factors
- Departmental Standards: Traditional training models that overemphasize physical bravado and a "masculine ideal" can create friction and unique occupational stress for women on the force.
- Public Perception and Bias: Civilian and observer perceptions often interpret a female officer's use of force as a response to external, unavoidable danger rather than internal aggression, impacting how these incidents are reviewed and trusted by the community.
