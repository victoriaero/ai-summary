Several key economic, social, and systemic factors influence housing stability and homeownership for Latino households in the United States, according to research from organizations like the Urban Institute and the Joint Center for Housing Studies of Harvard University.
Financial and Wealth Gaps
• Income and Wealth Disparities: A long history of racial exclusion in the economy leaves many Latino families with lower median household wealth and income compared to white households, making it harder to save for a down payment.
• Intergenerational Wealth: Latino young adults have significantly lower median parental wealth to draw on for down payments, widening the down-payment gap as homebuying becomes more competitive.
• Non-Traditional Income: Latino workers are more frequently self-employed or engaged in enterprising and informal work. Rigid mortgage income-verification standards often fail to accommodate non-W-2 earnings, artificially increasing calculated debt-to-income ratios.
• Credit Access: Systemic barriers and historical banking practices mean Latino consumers are more likely to be "credit invisible" or carry higher debt-to-income burdens.
Language and Immigration Factors
• English Proficiency: Data from the American Community Survey show that Latinos who speak English very well or exclusively report higher rates of homeownership, and neighborhoods with higher English proficiency tend to have higher overall homeownership rates.
• Immigration Status and Documentation: Fear of deportation or concerns regarding public charge policies can discourage newer immigrants from seeking rental assistance, entering traditional credit systems, or accessing housing and homelessness programs.
• Country of Origin: Homeownership rates vary within the Latino community, with households of Cuban origin historically showing higher homeownership rates than those of Mexican, Puerto Rican, or other origins.
Systemic Discrimination and Market Barriers
• Lending Discrimination: Hispanic and Latino applicants face higher mortgage rejection rates and higher interest rates compared to non-Hispanic white applicants with similar financial profiles.
• Housing Discrimination: Overt and subtle discrimination in the housing market limits choices, steers buyers toward lower-quality housing, or requires more extensive paperwork.
• Underutilization of Assistance: Studies indicate that low-income Latino households are underrepresented in public housing, Housing Choice Vouchers, and other housing assistance programs relative to their poverty rates, often due to language and administrative barriers.
Cultural and Household Structures
• Multigenerational Living: Latino households feature a higher proportion of multigenerational living arrangements, which can affect space requirements, overcrowding risks, and property upkeep needs.
• Financial Priorities: Cultural norms and financial goals—such as prioritizing support for extended family members or saving for children's education over purchasing a home—can also shape housing choices.
