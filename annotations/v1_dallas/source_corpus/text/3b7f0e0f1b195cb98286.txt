Abstract
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ+) individuals face health care disparities, highlighting the need for inclusive and affirming health care environments. Critical to this issue is the willingness to disclose sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) in health care settings, as it can significantly influence the quality of care. Despite prior literature emphasizing affirming spaces, the link between the physical health care office environment and SOGI disclosure remains underexplored. This study investigates the impact of health care office environments on the disclosure of SOGI in a diverse sample of N = 399 participants (LGBTQ+ n = 196; cisgender and heterosexual: cishet n = 203). Findings reveal that affirming health care settings enhance willingness to disclose SOGI status, particularly among sexual minorities who identify as cisgender (LGBQ) individuals, while transgender and gender diverse individuals exhibit less willingness to disclose compared to LGBQ and cishet individuals. Individual factors (e.g., outness, internalized stigma) significantly influence willingness to disclose SOGI status. Affirming health care environments mitigated the negative impact of internalized LGBTQ+ stigma on willingness to disclose. These results highlight the need for affirming spaces, addressing both external discrimination and internalized LGBTQ+ stigma to mitigate LGBTQ+ health disparities.
Keywords: health care environments, sexual orientation and gender identity disclosure, affirming cues, lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer health
In health care settings, patients encounter numerous cues—both subtle and explicit—that profoundly influence their experience. For stigmatized patients, such cues can mean the difference between feeling accepted or marginalized. Stigmatized individuals, like lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ+) patients, are especially vigilant to cues in the environment due to rational fears of discrimination (H. Brooks et al., 2018; R. Johnson et al., 2018). Given the history of stigma and inappropriate care in health care (e.g., HIV-related stigma, gender-affirming care restrictions, conversion therapy), LGBTQ+ patients may rely on contextual cues to assess their safety in disclosing their identity (Diamond & Alley, 2022). This heightened awareness necessitates a closer examination of how these cues are constructed and managed by health care systems, as they directly impact LGBTQ+ patients’ health care experiences.
Beyond LGBTQ+ Disparities
Understanding the unique cues and contexts that impact LGBTQ+ patients is essential for addressing health disparities, as these inequalities stem from societal treatment, such as discrimination, prejudice, and inadequate access to health care services (Fingerhut & Abdou, 2017). Because of this mistreatment from society, LGBTQ+ individuals are more likely to be at heightened risk for various health issues, such as increased substance use (Goffnett & Goldbach, 2020), lower cancer prevention service uptake among lesbian women (Waterman & Voss, 2015), increased mental health issues for bisexual individuals (Semlyen et al., 2016), elevated HIV and sexually transmitted infections risks for gay and bisexual men, particularly men of color (CDC, 2019), high HIV rates for transgender women (Van Gerwen et al., 2020), and greater psychological distress for transgender and gender diverse (TGD) individuals (Valentine & Shipherd, 2018), compared to cisgender and heterosexual (cishet) individuals. The current rise in anti-LGBTQ+ legislation only further exacerbates these health disparities (Haas & Lannutti, 2024). Therefore, it is imperative to explore how to mitigate the health disparities gap experienced by LGBTQ+ individuals (S. S. Johnson, 2024).
These disparities are largely driven by ongoing discrimination and prejudice, as explained by the minority stress model (V. R. Brooks, 1981; Meyer, 1995, 2003). This model posits that LGBTQ+ individuals experience unique and chronic stressors due to mistreatment, such as medical discrimination and internalized stigma which contribute to poorer mental and physical health outcomes (Frost et al., 2015; Newcomb & Mustanski, 2010). Discrimination remains a significant stressor for LGBTQ+ patients on both interpersonal and policy levels, with fear of discrimination in health care settings serving as a primary barrier to sexual orientation (SO; a person’s lasting pattern of emotional, romantic, or sexual attraction to people that reflects a person’s identity based on these attractions, behaviors, and community membership) and gender identity (GI; a person’s internal sense of their gender) or sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) disclosure (Malik et al., 2019). Knowing and understanding SOGI data are essential for creating effective interventions, providing appropriate care, and affirming LGBTQ+ identities (Streed et al., 2020). Patients report numerous issues related to SOGI disclosure, such as anticipating discomfort, navigating anger, preparing for embarrassment, and experiencing shame (H. Brooks et al., 2018). Some LGBTQ+ patients may be less open about their identities to protect themselves from potential negative reactions (Maciel & Barnett, 2021). Additionally, higher levels of internalized homophobia are linked to reduced openness and less frequent SOGI disclosure (Durso & Meyer, 2013). Therefore, accurately capturing SOGI data helps in counting and understanding the specific needs of LGBTQ+ individuals, ensuring they receive the necessary support and affirmation in health care settings.
Heteronormativity and Cisnormativity: Nonaffirming Environment
Heteronormativity and cisnormativity marginalize LGBTQ+ individuals by assuming heterosexuality and binary gender as the norm (Boe et al., 2020; Enson, 2015). This can manifest in medical settings through forms, questions, and knowledge shared with patients, creating a nonaffirming environment (Utamsingh et al., 2015). The fear of facing discrimination and the need to conceal one’s identity can lead LGBTQ+ patients to avoid health care altogether (Venetis et al., 2017). Research in health care settings has shown that LGBTQ+ individuals, particularly TGD individuals, perceive doctors and hospitals as unsafe due to unrelenting hostility, misunderstanding, and disrespect (Romanelli & Lindsey, 2020). These negative cues serve as potential indicators of disapproval and can elicit physiological stress responses, leading LGBTQ+ patients to respond with increased vigilance (Ellis et al., 2022).
LGBTQ+ Cues: Affirming Environment
To counter these issues, it has been suggested that affirming cues in health care can improve the experiences of stigmatized groups (Cipollina & Sanchez, 2019). Efforts to create affirming environments, such as utilizing inclusive language, diverse imagery, LGBTQ+ symbols, all-gender bathrooms, advertisements of gender-affirming care and treatments, and nondiscrimination policies, have been suggested to shape LGBTQ+ patient experiences (Lim et al., 2014; McClain et al., 2016). The social safety perspective, encompassing aspects such as social connection, inclusion, protection, recognition, and acceptance, may play an important role in such experiences (Diamond & Alley, 2022). Awareness of cues in health care environments, which can either foster or hinder social safety, may shape the willingness of LGBTQ+ individuals to disclose their SOGI status (Diamond & Alley, 2022; Meyer, 2013). For example, Wilkerson et al. (2011) found LGBTQ+ patients reported specifically looking for a cue with LGBTQ+-affirming images in health care (posters, magazines, and patient education materials), and once identified feeling they were more likely to be at ease, highlighting affirming physical cues’ potential in such contexts. Such subtle yet powerful signals may be key in reducing minority stress and promoting disclosure of SOGI status by fostering a sense of safety (Diamond & Alley, 2022) and encouraging open communication, which is critical for receiving appropriate care (Cipollina & Sanchez, 2019).
Missing Comparisons: Identity-Neutral Cues and Cishet Individuals
Additional comparison groups could aid in understanding the effectiveness of affirming physical cues. For example, little is known about LGBTQ+ individuals’ perspectives of cues that are neither affirming nor nonaffirming. Such identity-neutral physical cues in health care contexts may activate previous experiences of discrimination in medical care (Diamond & Alley, 2022), but this has yet to be systematically tested in terms of willingness to disclose SOGI status. Such a comparison inclusion is crucial for understanding how the hospital setting itself may hinder patient SOGI disclosure. Additionally, limited research has examined the impact of LGBTQ+ cues in health care on cishet patients. One study found that TGD-affirming cues on mental health provider websites were positively evaluated by TGD individuals and were seen as welcoming by cisgender individuals (Parent & Tebbe, 2022). Some research suggests that heterosexual patients may be hesitant to disclose their SOGI status (Cahill et al., 2014), while other studies show that even in seemingly more conservative populations, such as veterans, cishet individuals are willing to report their identity characteristics (Ruben et al., 2021). The inclusion of both comparison groups (cishet and LGBTQ+) and identity-neutral cues will allow for further exploration of the effectiveness of various cues on more diverse patient populations.
Current Study
Implementation of physical affirming cues has been suggested in multiple LGBTQ+ inclusive training guides, yet little experimental research has tested the effectiveness of displaying physical affirming cues in a health care setting for LGBTQ+ patients. With heteronormativity/cisnormativity existing within the medical field, it is unknown how the effect of nonaffirming physical cues may be hindering LGBTQ+ individuals’ health care experience. While some have posited that these nonaffirming cues may be so common that they become white noise in the background of one’s life (Diamond & Alley, 2022), they may impact the willingness to disclose one’s identity to health care providers, especially among those who experience more internalized LGBTQ+ stigma, or those with a history of discrimination in medical care (Ruben & Fullerton, 2018). Thus, this study aimed to understand the relationship between physical cues of the primary care office (affirming, nonaffirming, vs. identity-neutral) and willingness to disclose SOGI status as well as individual difference moderators (i.e., internalized LGBTQ+ stigma, internalized transphobia, and past experiences of medical discrimination).
Hypotheses
We hypothesized that all participants would report more comfort disclosing their SOGI status in the affirming office condition compared to the identity-neutral and nonaffirming condition. Of our LGBTQ+ participants, we further hypothesized that sexual minorities who identify as cisgender (LGBQ) participants would report the least willingness to disclose their SO compared to those who identify as gender minorities (TGD) and cisgender and heterosexual (cishet) participants and that TGD participants would report the least willingness to disclose their GI compared to LGBQ and cishet participants, given societal stigma and history of discrimination in medical care, following a social safety perspective (Diamond & Alley, 2022). While minority stress has been crucial in understanding health disparities among stigmatized populations (emphasizing the impact of stressors like discrimination and internalized stigma) we aimed to include the concept of social safety, highlighting that the inclusion of safety cues may counter the presence of LGBTQ+-related stressors. As a secondary aim, we intended to examine whether and how LGBTQ+-related stressors (i.e., internalized LGBTQ+ stigma, internalized transphobia, past medical discrimination, and outness) impacted LGBTQ+ participants’ willingness to disclose their SOGI status and whether this was moderated by office condition. We hypothesized that individuals who reported higher levels of LGBTQ+-related stressors would be less willing to disclose their SOGI status, but this would be most pronounced for those assigned to the non-affirming condition compared to the affirming condition. It was exploratory as to what impact the identity-neutral condition would have on these effects.
Method
We recruited a total of N = 540 participants (n = 270 LGBTQ+ individuals and n = 270 cishet individuals) via the online platform Prolific. The study took approximately 15 min to complete, and participants received $2.63 as compensation. To determine the minimum sample size required for the study, an a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power Version 3.1.9.7 (Faul et al., 2007). The analysis established that a sample size of N = 302 was necessary to attain 95% power for detecting a medium effect size (f = 0.25) at α = .05. Participants displaying flat responses, failing any of the three attention checks, or completing the survey in under 8 min were excluded. The final sample comprised N = 399 participants, consisting of n = 196 LGBTQ+ individuals and n = 203 cishet individuals (Table 1). Of the LGBTQ+ participants, participants were further grouped by cisgender (LGBQ; n = 149) and trans and gender diverse (TGD; n = 47). This study was approved by the University of Rhode Island Institutional Review Board.

After providing informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of three simulated primary care office background conditions: affirming, nonaffirming, or identity-neutral. While viewing each photograph in color, participants rated their intention to disclose their SOGI status. Participants then responded to questions related to the office space in a more general sense to avoid creating overt demand characteristics (e.g., “This office is professional”). Participants completed demographic questions and LGBTQ+ participants completed questions related to LGBTQ+-related stressors.
Health Care Office Conditions
The affirming health care office condition included LGBTQ+ affirming cues, followed by American Medical Association (AMA) and The Fenway Institute guidelines, such as LGBTQ+ health materials, inclusive nondiscrimination policies, and posters featuring LGBTQ+ individuals (AMA, 2021). These cues included images of same-sex couples, pride flags, and Human Rights Campaign Awards in LGBTQ Healthcare Equality (indicating the health care system was a leader in establishing inclusive nondiscrimination patient policies). The nonaffirming health care office condition aimed to reinforce heteronormativity and cisnormativity, with two cues focused on heteronormativity (one poster depicting nuclear families, another poster depicting an image of a heterosexual relationship), and one cue that focused on cisnormativity (two figures focused on anatomical references, reinforcing the gender binary). In the identity-neutral health care office condition, identity-neutral cues (abstract art, water, landscapes) were used as control stimuli (see Figure 1 for examples of all three office conditions; see the online supplemental materials for information about the development of the office conditions).
Measures
Willingness to Disclose SOGI Status
All participants responded to 14 items, adapted from Cipollina and Sanchez (2022), assessing their willingness to disclose their SO and GI on a scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. These items included questions about disclosure on patient intake forms, when asked by the physician, when they considered it relevant or irrelevant to medical advice, their comfort with disclosing, and their overall belief in the importance of disclosing to a health care provider (αSO = .89, αGI = .88).
Demographic Information
All participants provided information regarding their age, sex assigned at birth, GI, sexual identity, race, ethnicity, and education.
LGBTQ+-Related Stressors
Previous Medical Discrimination (α = .94).
LGBTQ+ participants completed a six-item LGBTQ Medical Discrimination Scale (Fisher et al., 2018), measuring the frequency of discrimination experienced by LGBTQ+ individuals when seeking health care on a scale from 1 = never to 5 = always. Language specific to one’s LGBTQ+ identity was included. An example item included, “A healthcare physician or staff member has criticized or insulted me for being LGBTQ+ .”
Outness (α = .84).
LGBTQ+ participants completed the Outness Inventory (Mohr & Fassinger, 2000), which assesses the degree of openness about one’s sexual identity specifically, across 11 different social groups/individuals, using a scale ranging from 1 = person definitely does not know about your SO status to 7 = person definitely knows about your SO status and it is openly discussed. These groups included the participant’s mother, father, siblings, extended family, new straight friends, work peers, work supervisors, members of their religious community, leaders of their religious community, strangers, and old heterosexual friends, with a “not applicable (N/A)” option for cases where a group did not exist in the participant’s life.
Internalized LGBTQ+ Stigma1 (α = .84).
LGBTQ+ participants completed a modified version of the Internalized Homonegativity subscale from the Outness Inventory Scale (Mohr & Fassinger, 2000), which gauges negative beliefs and feelings about one’s sexual identity and was modified to include negative beliefs and feelings about one’s LGBTQ+ identity on a scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. An example item included, “I would rather not be LGBTQ+ if I could.”
Internalized Transphobia (α = .93).
LGBTQ+ participants completed the internalized transphobia subscale of the Gender Minority Stress and Resilience Measure (Testa et al., 2015), which assesses negative beliefs and feelings about one’s transgender or gender diverse identity on a scale from 1 = never to 5 = always. An example item included, “I often ask myself: Why can’t my gender identity or expression just be normal?”
Results
Impact of Affirming, Nonaffirming, and Identity-Neutral Cues on SO Disclosure
We conducted a 3 (health care office: affirming, nonaffirming, identity-neutral) × 3 (sexual/gender identity: cishet, TGD, LGBQ) between-subjects factorial analysis of variance to investigate whether health care office environments and sexual/gender identities affect willingness to disclose SO.
Our hypothesis that all participants would report a greater willingness to disclose SO in affirming offices compared to other office types was supported, with a significant main effect of health care office, F(2, 390) = 8.30, p < .001, η2p = .041. Post hoc analyses revealed that participants in affirming health care offices (M = 5.52, SD = 1.11) reported significantly higher willingness to disclose SO compared to those in nonaffirming offices (M = 4.99, SD = 1.36, p < .001, d = 0.42). However, no significant difference was found when comparing affirming and identity-neutral health care offices (M = 5.32, SD = 1.19, p = .172). There was significantly greater willingness to disclose SO in identity-neutral health care offices compared to nonaffirming offices (p = .011, d = 0.25).
Our hypothesis that LGBQ participants would report the lowest willingness to disclose SO compared to TGD and cishet participants was partially supported, with a significant main effect of sexual/gender identity, F(2, 390)= 20.94, p < .001, η2p = .097. Post hoc analyses indicated that both LGBQ participants (M = 5.07, SD = 1.31) and TGD participants (M = 4.47, SD = 1.45) reported significantly lower willingness to disclose SO compared to cishet participants (M = 5.59, SD = 1.02; cishet vs. LGBQ, p = .004, d = 0.44; cishet vs. TGD, p < .001, d = 0.89). Additionally, TGD participants reported significantly lower willingness to disclose SO compared to LGBQ participants (p = .004, d = 0.43).
We expected that these main effects would be qualified by a significant interaction between health care office and sexual/gender identity, with the greatest impact on increasing LGBQ participants’ willingness to disclose SO in the affirming offices. As anticipated, there was a significant health care office by sexual/gender identity interaction, F(4, 390) = 3.70, p = .006, η2p = .037. Post hoc analyses revealed that LGBQ participants reported significantly more willingness to disclose SO in the affirming health care office (M = 5.68, SD = 1.00) compared to the identity-neutral office (M = 5.01, SD = 1.32, p = .004, d = 0.57) and nonaffirming office (M = 4.48, SD= 1.32, p < .001, d = 1.03). Additionally, LGBQ participants reported significantly less willingness to disclose their SO in the nonaffirming office compared to the identity-neutral office (p = .018, d = 0.42). Among TGD participants and cishet, no significant differences were observed in willingness to disclose SO based on health care office (see Figure 2).
Impact of Affirming, Nonaffirming, and Identity-Neutral Cues on GI Disclosure
Similar to SO disclosure, we conducted a 3 (health care office: affirming, nonaffirming, identity-neutral) × 3 (sexual/gender identity: cishet, TGD, LGBQ) between-subjects factorial analysis of variance to examine the effects of health care office environments and sexual/gender identities on willingness to disclose GI.
Our hypothesis that all participants would be more willing to disclose GI in affirming offices compared to other office types was supported as there was a significant main effect of health care office, F(2, 390) = 6.16, p = .002, η2p = .031. Post hoc analyses revealed that participants in affirming health care offices (M = 5.65, SD = to disclose GI compared to those in nonaffirming offices (M = 5.37, SD = 1.23, p = .002, d = 0.24). However, no significant differences were observed between those assigned to the affirming and identity-neutral health care offices (M = 5.70, SD = 1.00, p = .885). Participants assigned to the identity-neutral health care offices also showed significantly higher willingness to disclose GI compared to those assigned to the nonaffirming offices (p = .004, d = 0.30).
Our hypothesis that TGD participants would report the lowest willingness to disclose GI compared to LGBQ and cishet participants was supported, as there was a significant main effect of sexual/gender identity, F(2, 390) = 26.52, p < .001, η2p = .12. TGD participants (M = 4.50, SD = 1.49) reported significantly lower willingness to disclose GI compared to cishet participants (M = 5.70, SD = 0.95, p <. 001, d = 0.96) and LGBQ participants (M = 5.70, SD = 1.02, p <.001, d = 0.94). No significant differences were found between cishet and LGBQ participants (p = .994).
We anticipated that these main effects would be qualified by a significant interaction between health care office and sexual/gender identity, with affirming offices having the greatest impact on TGD participants. However, the interaction was not significant, F(4, 390) = 1.50, p = .202, η2p = .015. This suggests that the effect of health care office on willingness to disclose GI did not significantly differ across different sexual/gender identity groups (see Figure 3).
LGBTQ+-Related Stressors as Moderators of Health Care Office Effects on SO and GI Disclosure
Giventhe significance of internalized LGBTQ+ stigma, previous medical discrimination, and outness as predictors of SOGI disclosure, (see the online supplemental materials for information about the analyses conducted for predictors of SOGI disclosure) we conducted a series of exploratory moderation analyses.
We hypothesized that those with more minority stress, such as higher levels of internalized LGBTQ+ stigma, would benefit the most from an affirming office space, leading to higher levels of willingness to disclose SO and GI. We conducted moderation analyses using the Hayes PROCESS macro in SPSS (Hayes & Rockwood, 2017). We regressed willingness to disclose SO or GI on each of the stressors of interest, health care office space (treated as a categorical indicator variable), and the interaction term of the minority stressor by health care office space, controlling for the other LGBTQ+-related stressors, and centering all continuous variables.
The interaction of internalized LGBTQ+ stigma and health careo ffice on SO disclosure (controlling for outness and previous medical discrimination) was significant. The overall model, F(7, 187) = 11.74, p < .001, explained 31% of the variance in willingness to disclose SO. While controlling for covariates and interactions, neither affirming office space (β = .17, p = .13) nor internalized LGBTQ+ stigma (β = .10, p = .50) were significant predictors of willingness to disclose SO. Nonaffirming (β = −.84, p < .001) and identity-neutral (β = −.47, p = .005) office space as well as outness (β = .36, p < .001) and previous medical discrimination (β = −.24, p = .001) were significant predictors of willingness to disclose SO.
These effects, however, were qualified by the significant office space by internalized LGBTQ+ stigma interaction F(2, 187) = 3.33, p = .038, such that in the affirming condition there was a nonsignificant, positive relationship between internalized LGBTQ+ stigma and SO disclosure (β = .10, p = .501). In the nonaffirming condition, there was a significant negative relationship between internalized LGBTQ+ stigma and SO disclosure (β = −.28, p = .015) and a similar significant negative relationship in the identity-neutral condition (β =−.34, p = .002). Thus, it appears that in the nonaffirming and identity-neutral office conditions, more internalized LGBTQ+ stigma predicted less willingness to disclose SO while there was no relationship between internalized LGBTQ+ stigma and willingness to disclose SO in the affirming office condition (see Figure 4 for model-implied values adjusting for covariates).2
Discussion
This study aimed to investigate how affirming, nonaffirming, and identity-neutral cues in health care environments affect the willingness of LGBTQ+ and cishet individuals to disclose their SOGI status. Through experimental manipulation of health care office settings and the examination of individual-level LGBTQ+-related stressors, we aimed to examine the impacts of environmental cues and personal characteristics on willingness to disclose SO and GI. Overall, findings showed that within affirming health care environments, there is an increased willingness to disclose one’s SOGI status, especially among LGBQ individuals. In contrast, TGD individuals exhibit a lower propensity to disclose their status when compared to both LGBQ and cishet individuals. Factors at the individual level, such as one’s level of outness and the extent of internalized LGBTQ+ stigma, significantly influence one’s willingness to disclose SOGI status. Findings showed that the presence of affirming health care environments mitigated the adverse impact of internalized LGBTQ+ stigma on SO status disclosure.
Our study revealed that affirming cues in health care settings increased the willingness of LGBTQ+ individuals to disclose their SOGI status when compared to nonaffirming settings. The lack of significant differences between affirming and identity-neutral environments may suggest that the absence of nonaffirming cues may be enough for patients to perceive safety in at least their initial willingness to disclose. Yet, identity-neutral cues are not enough for all patients to feel safe in disclosing their identity. For example, when environmental and individual factors were considered, such as internalized LGBTQ+ stigma, both nonaffirming and identity-neutral environments negatively impacted LGBTQ+ participants’ willingness to disclose SO. This highlights the complexity of identity-neutral cues and the need for nuanced interventions that consider the diverse range of environmental and individual factors influencing disclosure decisions.
LGBQ individuals were particularly responsive to affirming cues when considering their willingness to disclose SO, highlighting the importance of creating inclusive health care spaces that foster trust and openness (Cipollina & Sanchez, 2019, 2022). However, the lack of a similar pattern for GI disclosure among TGD participants raises questions about additional barriers or complexities unique to TGD people’s experiences within affirming health care settings. This suggests that even in supportive contexts, TGD individuals may encounter distinct obstacles that hinder their willingness or ability to share their GI. These findings suggest that perhaps hospitals and health care settings in general may not be viewed as safe for TGD individuals to disclose such information. Given the rise of antigender-affirming care policies in the U.S. along with our results, there is an urgent need for ensuring affirming and inclusive health care quality improvement across structural and interpersonal factors for TGD patients going forward (Elliott et al., 2015; Kattari & Hasche, 2016). We found that cishet individuals were comfortable disclosing their SOGI status across various health care contexts including offices with LGBTQ+ affirming cues. These results support the notion that inclusive environments support everyone, debunk concerns around patient discomfort (Hettinger & Vandello, 2014), and emphasize the importance of equitable access to quality health care for LGBTQ+ patients (Kline et al., 2022).
Our study deepened the understanding of individual factors influencing the willingness to disclose one’s SOGI status in health care. Outness was a positive predictor of the willingness to disclose, echoing previous findings (Mohr & Fassinger, 2000). Importantly, the presence of affirming cues in health care environments seemed to mitigate the negative influence of internalized LGBTQ+ stigma on disclosure, highlighting the potential of affirming spaces to counteract internalized LGBTQ+ stigma. These findings contribute to the growing body of research on LGBTQ+-related stressors, emphasizing the need for comprehensive approaches addressing both external discrimination and internalized negative beliefs.
This work highlights the significance of creating affirming environments where individuals feel safe and supported in disclosing their identities, which is needed for optimal health care outcomes. Identity disclosure is related to higher patient satisfaction, more adherence, more health service use, and better self-reported health and psychological well-being (Ruben & Fullerton, 2018). To foster affirming environments for disclosing SOGI, health care providers and clinicians alike can adapt and enhance existing facilities with tailored physical affirming cues for LGBTQ+ individuals. Interdisciplinary teams could advocate for policy changes prioritizing LGBTQ+ health care equity, such as mandating the inclusion of SOGI data collection in electronic health records to ensure comprehensive and culturally competent care. This comprehensive approach could include implementing gender-affirming signage, revising institutional policies for nondiscrimination and confidentiality, and fostering community partnerships to expand access to affirming health care services. Such collaboration with physicians, surgeons, endocrinologists, psychologists, speech therapists, policymakers, and patient advocacy groups would help foster inclusive care delivery.
It is crucial to acknowledge the assumption that health care providers are uniformly equipped to respond effectively when patients disclose their SOGI status. While affirming cues in health care environments are undeniably significant for LGBTQ+ individuals, not all health care providers may possess the necessary training or cultural competence to deliver patient-centered LGBTQ+ affirming care. To address these concerns, comprehensive approaches are required, encompassing not only affirming environments but also integrating robust provider training programs, focusing on LGBTQ+ health knowledge, empathy, and communication skills (e.g., Banerjee et al., 2021), in tandem with implementing affirming environments. We strongly caution against health care systems merely adopting affirming cues without a systematic approach to training staff. Tangible plans, such as revising paperwork to be more inclusive, ensuring inclusive bedside manner and front desk staff, creating feedback mechanisms for patients to share their experiences, and establishing visible support resources (such as LGBTQ+ patient health navigators) could complement the physical affirmations present in health care settings.
Several limitations merit consideration when interpreting our study’s findings. Despite our efforts to diversify participant groups, the sample may not fully represent the broader population, especially individuals without access to online research platforms. The collapse of identities within groups, such as LGBQ and TGD individuals, may mask nuances within these communities. This could be addressed in future work by employing more varied recruitment strategies and conducting finer grained analyses of identity groups to enhance the applicability of our findings. Future research should purposefully sample racially and ethnically diverse participants to continue examining intersectionality as multiple marginalized identities compound barriers to care, stigma, and discrimination, impacting health care access and well-being. While broad inclusive cues have the potential to promote positive outcomes across diverse identities (Kraus & Crul, 2022), future research should examine TGD-specific affirming cues (e.g., resources on hormone treatment) versus LGBQ-specific affirming cues (e.g., resources on sexual health for LGBQ people) alongside broader LGBTQ+ affirming cues (e.g., a pride flag), as we did not differentiate between them in this study. Given the ambiguity of health care spaces with combinations of affirming, nonaffirming, and identityneutral cues, further studies should investigate how these cues collectively impact the health care experiences and willingness to disclose SOGI status among LGBTQ+ individuals. This research could provide insights into the dynamics within health care environments and inform the development of customized strategies to enhance inclusivity.
A strength of this research is the examination of LGBQ and TGD individuals separately, as well as comparing LGBQ and TGD participants to cishet participants, instead of collapsing them into a single LGBTQ+ category, highlighting the unique experiences of different identity groups. Our study incorporated a theoretical framework that considers social safety alongside LGBTQ+-related stressors, deepening our understanding of LGBTQ+ health care experiences, highlighting the critical role of affirming health care environments in promoting SOGI disclosure and the barriers LGBTQ+ individuals face in accessing basic health care when social safety is lacking (Diamond & Alley, 2022). Our results, combined with prior literature and theory, emphasize the necessity of a comprehensive approach in creating affirming health care spaces, focused on physical cues, interpersonal interactions, and policy reforms to unequivocally support LGBTQ+ individuals (Diamond & Alley, 2022; Hatzenbuehler, 2014). Our study emphasizes the pivotal role of affirming cues in health care settings, suggesting the importance of being “cue-full” rather than “cueless” in our efforts to promote inclusivity, equity, and ultimately health among sexual and gender minorities.

What is the significance of this article for the general public?
This research reveals health care settings’ impact on lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer individuals’ willingness to disclose their sexual and gender identity. Affirming health care spaces facilitate sexual minorities who identify as cisgender individuals’ disclosure, while transgender and gender diverse individuals may need more than affirming spaces to comfortably disclose. How open someone is about one’s identity and internalized negative feelings about one’s identity are key to how willing lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer individuals are to share their identity in these spaces, emphasizing inclusive spaces to create more equitable health care.

This approach is informed by the need for inclusive measures that ensure all participants can meaningfully contribute, as well as research highlighting the importance of gender identity-specific measures in understanding diverse experiences (Testa et al., 2015). Inclusive measures help capture the broad spectrum of participants’ experiences, while gender-specific metrics provide insights into the unique experiences of TGD individuals.
We were not powered to incorporate race and ethnicity into our initial models; however, at a later stage, we did test whether a binary race/ethnicity variable (comparing White people to people of color) significantly impacted any of our outcomes or interacted with office condition or sexual/gender identity in any of our ANOVA, regression, or moderation models. There was no significant main effects or interactions with race/ethnicity; however, future research should purposefully sample racially and ethnically diverse participants in order to continue examining intersectionality in this work.
