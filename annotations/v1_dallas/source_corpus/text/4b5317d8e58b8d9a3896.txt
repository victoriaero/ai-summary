Since the conclusion of the US COVID-19 Public Health Emergency (PHE) and the unwinding of Medicaid continuous enrollment, an alarming number of individuals have been disenrolled from Medicaid because states were unable to verify eligibility, an issue known as procedural disenrollment. As of January 30, 2024, 70% of the approximately 16 million individuals disenrolled from Medicaid experienced procedural disenrollment—much higher than initial projections—despite many enrollees reporting unchanged finances during the PHE and thus likely remaining Medicaid-eligible.1
Much of the current focus on Medicaid disenrollments has been on children and working-age adults. However, adults older than 65 and those with disabilities face unique challenges renewing Medicaid coverage due to different Medicaid eligibility rules that apply to these populations, and they may face unique consequences of disenrollment. We discuss the substantial risk and impact of Medicaid disenrollment amongst enrollees older than 65 or with disabilities and recommend reforms to help these individuals maintain continuous Medicaid coverage.
Non-MAGI Enrollees Face Substantial Risk and Consequences of Medicaid Disenrollment
Medicaid eligibility criteria vary across subpopulations of enrollees. Working-age adults who qualify for Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act’s Medicaid expansion need only report income when applying for Medicaid, and states use a simplified modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) test to determine eligibility. However, other populations are subject to more complex non-MAGI eligibility criteria; this includes adults older than 65 and people with disabilities, many of whom are enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid (dual enrollees). For dual enrollees, Medicaid is a vital source of supplemental health insurance, covering substantial cost sharing and out-of-pocket costs not covered by Medicare.
Pre-pandemic procedural disenrollment rates were historically lower for non-MAGI and dual enrollees compared to MAGI enrollees, likely due to relatively stable income amongst older adults and people with disabilities. Nevertheless, this population experienced appreciable Medicaid churn—disenrollment and reenrollment—before the pandemic. One analysis found that between 16% and 23% of dual eligible individuals were disenrolled from Medicaid at least once within a 3-year period, and that 51% to 58% of those who disenrolled from Medicaid subsequently regained this coverage.2 Interruptions in Medicaid coverage can be disruptive for individuals who rely on Medicaid to pay for Medicare’s out-of-pocket costs and long-term care.
Further, non-MAGI criteria require individuals to provide detailed documentation of income (itemizing earned and unearned income) and assets, and potentially undergo more frequent redeterminations. This can be burdensome for non-MAGI enrollees and Medicaid agencies alike. Post-PHE circumstances exacerbate the issue. Compared to younger populations, adults older than 65 are less aware of the Medicaid unwinding process, less likely to have updated contact information with state agencies, and more likely to be experiencing their first ever Medicaid redetermination.3 People with disabilities may face even greater difficulties completing frequent redeterminations due to physical and cognitive impairments.4
In short, non-MAGI older adult enrollees are less likely than younger adults to have changes in income and financial circumstances from year to year, but have more burdensome application requirements. Therefore, non-MAGI enrollees are at significant risk of Medicaid disenrollment even while potentially remaining fully eligible during the Medicaid unwinding, and the consequences of Medicaid loss may be greater due to their complex care needs.
States should consider the following approaches to prevent procedural disenrollment among non-MAGI enrollees.
1. Improve Accessibility of State Medicaid Redetermination Processes
Many individuals who meet disability criteria for Medicaid eligibility have significant vision and hearing impairments. Despite this, at least 6 state Medicaid agencies do not have large-print or braille applications available.5 Even when agencies offer visual accessibility features, they may not be equally available to users across digital and physical application formats; for instance, only 2 states make large-print applications available online.5 Individuals with hearing impairment may need speech-to-text translation, but 9 states do not reference this service on their online homepage, and 24 states do not describe how to request a sign-language interpreter.5 Enhanced accessibility of application materials and of call centers for assistance would improve the Medicaid redetermination process for people with vision and hearing disabilities.
2. Target Patient Navigators to Older Adults and People with Disabilities
Beyond accessibility concerns, the Medicaid application itself is complex and confusing. This can be particularly challenging for older adults and people with disabilities. Prior studies of Medicare enrollment find that older adults with cognitive impairments were less likely to enroll in any Medicare Advantage plan when given complex choices on the enrollment site.6 Similarly, asset tests and other required documentation may overwhelm many individuals, resulting in incomplete submissions.7
Patient navigators can help enrollees complete the redetermination process, but poor staffing and budgetary constraints limit the availability of real-time human assistance. Federal and state investment in navigator programs should be prioritized for older adults and individuals with disabilities who are more likely to require assistance.
3. Increase Streamlined or Automated Renewals for non-MAGI Enrollees
A 2022 proposal by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services aims to align non-MAGI and MAGI eligibility criteria, streamlining redeterminations for state Medicaid agencies by consolidating the two eligibility criteria.8 Currently it is anticipated that CMS will publish the final rule to be implemented in 2024.
Another approach that states have used to mitigate procedural disenrollments is increasing rates of automatic (ex parte) Medicaid renewals based on existing income and eligibility data.9 For example, some states use eligibility data in applications for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) to conduct ex parte Medicaid renewals. This can streamline the redetermination process for individuals who have been screened for other public assistance programs. Leveraging this for non-MAGI enrollees would preempt the need for older adults and people with disabilities to complete separate Medicaid redetermination forms. However, state agencies must ensure they utilize current financial data for enrollees, or they may inadvertently disenroll eligible participants, based on out-of-date data.10
Several states have applied for Medicaid waivers to allow greater flexibility in ex parte renewals during the unwinding period. For example, one waiver option broadens data sources states may use to verify income, including SNAP, Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), or the Social Security Administration. Twenty states have already applied for waivers to use SNAP data and 5 to use TANF data in ex parte renewals for MAGI enrollees; however, for non-MAGI enrollees, only 8 states use SNAP data and only 2 use TANF data.11 Expanding the range of data sources would increase the number of ex parte renewals and reduce procedural disenrollments.
4. Ease Asset Testing for Non-MAGI Enrollees During the Medicaid Unwinding Period
Most older adults who meet income eligibility criteria for Medicaid have few assets, suggesting that steps to streamline asset tests would still focus Medicaid on low-income groups while reducing administrative burden to states and enrollees.12 Several states have experimented with Medicaid waivers that ease asset testing for older adults and people with disabilities. California, Maryland, Nevada, and Rhode Island have entirely waived asset testing during renewals for non-MAGI enrollees. New Hampshire simplified asset verification by assuming no change in assets for non-MAGI enrollees with assets less than $1000, which is lower than typical asset limits for Medicaid supplemental coverage. These types of simplifications could be particularly beneficial for state Medicaid agencies processing an unprecedented volume of redeterminations in the first 12 months of unwinding.
CONCLUSIONS
Recent data on Medicaid unwinding reveals alarming rates of procedural disenrollments, for which older adults and people with disabilities (non-MAGI enrollees) are particularly at-risk. Coverage loss for these vulnerable individuals may be mitigated by state policies to enhance accessibility of Medicaid platforms, prioritize access to patient navigators, and simplify non-MAGI redetermination protocols.
