Anti-DEI policies a threat to colleges’ recruitment of male applicants?
The Trump administration’s ban against diversity, equity, and inclusion in college admissions and student support programming may negatively impact another group underrepresented in college enrollment: men, especially white men,
The Washington Post
reports.
For over four decades,
women have outnumbered men on college campuses
, with nearly 40% more women now enrolled in higher education than men, according to
federal data
. Over 800,000 more women applied to college than men in 2023, the most recent year this data is available, according to the
Post
. Women apply to college at higher rates than men, and males on average have
lower mean scores on the SAT
in reading and writing, have
lower overall ACT scores
, and
lower GPA averages
in high school.
Gender-balancing admissions practices
Highly selective colleges and universities, worried about a gender imbalance on campus, have been working to even out the number of men and women they admit, supported by a loophole under Title IX, a federal law that otherwise bans discrimination by higher education institutions receiving federal funding.
The law, passed in 1971, when men outnumbered women in college by nearly three to two, was amended to allow private colleges and universities to continue considering gender in admissions. Overall, the proportion of male undergraduates at the nation’s top 50 private higher education institutions is two percentage points higher than that for the top 50 flagship public institutions, which do not consider gender in admissions, the
Post
reports. Experts say this data suggests that at least some private institutions consider gender for male applicants in their admissions process.
For instance, Brown University, which had twice as many female applicants as male applicants last year, admitted a higher share of male applicants (7%) than female applicants (4.4%) to arrive at nearly equal numbers of male and female students, 2024-25
university data
shows.
To appeal to male applicants, institutions have also
tailored their outreach and programming
, even adding more sports programs, promoting hunting and foresting programs, and launching entrepreneurship competitions.
Eliminating gender preferences for male applicants?
Trump’s anti-DEI stance encompasses admissions practices that the administration perceives as considering applicants’ gender identity, along with their race, ethnicity, nationality, political views, sexual orientation, or religious associations, the
Post
reports. This year, Brown, Columbia, and Northwestern universities signed agreements with the Trump administration to settle allegations of antisemitism. In a statement announcing Brown’s agreement, Secretary of Education Linda McMahon
said
that the agreement also ensured “aspiring students will be judged solely on their merits, not their race or sex.” The administration has also said all higher education institutions should submit details about the students they admit, including their gender, to determine if colleges are “discriminating against hard working American” prospective students, McMahon
said in a statement
.
As institutions seek to avoid scrutiny from the administration, “I think we will see people dropping gender preferences, even though it is still within the law,” Ted Mitchell, president of the American Council on Education, the nation’s largest association of universities and colleges, told the
Post
.
Although some experts say that ending consideration of gender in admissions will have a minimal impact on the makeup of the college student population, others say it could dramatically change admissions at highly competitive private colleges that accept higher percentages of men than women, including Columbia University, the University of Chicago, the University of Miami, and Vassar College.
“If we were going to eliminate preferences for men, the undergraduate population would skew to 65 percent female overnight,” Mitchell told the
Post
.
