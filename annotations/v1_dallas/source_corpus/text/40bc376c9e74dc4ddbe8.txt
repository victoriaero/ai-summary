Abstract.
BACKGROUND: Employment barriers experienced by people with disabilities are well-documented by researchers. A
closer look at the nature of these barriers at different stages of the job acquisition process may help to understand and address
current challenges.
OBJECTIVE: To understand how employment barriers differ for job seekers with and without disabilities; the impact of
disability type; and are job acquisition barriers most salient, when looking for, applying to, or accepting a position.
METHODS: 3,021 working-age adults (including 1,491 adults with disabilities) from a Qualtrics opt-in panel completed
an online survey about employment status, job search barriers, and demographic factors (including disability). Logistic
regression predicted odds of experiencing a job search barrier based on disability and other sociodemographic factors.
RESULTS: Most job seekers reported barriers to employment, with disability being the strongest predictor. While the types
of barriers experienced at all stages of the job search were similar for people with and without disabilities, people with
disabilities experienced barriers signiﬁcantly more often.
CONCLUSION: Employment barriers among people with and without disabilities are similar in nature but vary by degree.
Improvements in transportation and better alignment between education, training, and the job market may beneﬁt all job
seekers.
Keywords: Employment, people with disabilities, job application, sociodemographic factors

persists between people with and without disabili-

Hampshire, 2023b). For example, in July of 2023, the
labor participation rate differential was similar to the
employment rate pattern, with 40.4% of people with
disabilities aged 16–64 participating in the work-
force, as compared to 78.4% of their contemporaries
without disabilities. Historically, lower employment
rates among people with disabilities were common,
and times of economic upset and recession affected
this part of the population longer and more severely
than people without disabilities (Kessler Foundation
& the University of New Hampshire, 2023a; Liver-
more & Honeycutt, 2015; McAlpine & Alang, 2021).
Prior research has shown that these effects do not
reﬂect a lack of desire or effort to be employed
on the part of people with disabilities. In fact, the
majority (68.4%) have demonstrated that they are
striving to work, meaning that they are either cur-
rently employed, held jobs in the past, or had been
actively seeking a position (Sundar et al., 2018).
Despite the desire and efforts to obtain and main-
tain work, people with disabilities experience myriad
barriers to employment (Khayatzadeh-Mahani et al.,
2020; McKinney & Swartz, 2021; Schur et al., 2017).
Employment barriers faced by people with dis-
abilities was a special topic of the ﬁrst disability
supplement to the monthly Current Population Sur-
vey ([CPS], Bureau of the Census, 2012). Findings
from the supplemental data collection showed that,
among people with disabilities who were unem-
ployed or not in the labor force, about 50% reported
at least one barrier to employment (Bureau of Labor
Statistics, 2013). The most commonly faced chal-
lenge was their own disability, followed by lack of
education or training, lack of transportation, and the
need for special features at the job (Bureau of Labor
Statistics, 2013). Informed largely by these ﬁndings,
the purpose of this article is to update and expand the
current understanding of employment obstacles faced
by people with disabilities during the job acquisition
process.
We replicated the CPS disability supplement ques-
tions about barriers that job seekers commonly face.
We also compared the nature and frequency of
barriers experienced by people with and without dis-
abilities, and we dove deeper to inquire about the
different key stages of job acquisition. Based on
prior research and the fact that different subpopu-
lations have varying success with distinct aspects of
obtaining employment (Barbulescu, 2015; Dannals
et al., 2021; Hernandez et al., 2019), we conceptual-
ized three stages of job acquisition: 1) looking for
a job means identifying desirable and potentially-
attainable positions; 2) applying for a job includes
the cover letter, application, and interview process;
and 3) accepting a job includes negotiating pay and
work schedule, and requesting any needed accommo-
dations. Speciﬁcally, this study sought to answer the
following research questions (RQ):
RQ1: What types of barriers are job seekers with
disabilities most likely to face, compared to job
seekers without disabilities?
RQ2: How do job search barriers differ by type
of disability?
RQ3: To what extent are job search barriers more
salient when looking for, applying to, or accepting
a position?
Barriers to employment can be categorized at the
levels of the individual employee, the employing
organization, and the larger societal level, as reﬂected
in Table 1 (Sundar et al., 2018).

1.2. Organizational-level barriers
Organizational-level factors that serve as barriers
to employment for people with disabilities include
employer perceptions and misconceptions. For exam-
ple, in an environmental scan of publications on
employer policies and practices conducted by Baker
and his colleagues (2018) several studies identiﬁed
instances where employers expressed concerns about
the sufﬁciency of educational and training credentials
or qualiﬁcations of applicants with disabilities. Peo-
ple with disabilities also report employer beliefs that
they were not qualiﬁed (Houtenville et al., 2015; Sun-
dar et al., 2018). While this was found to be the case
among employing organizations as well, employer
reports did not directly speak to non-inclusive work-
place cultures that people with disabilities reported
as a barrier to employment (Chen, 2015; Meltzer,
Robinson, & Fisher, 2020). Employers did acknowl-
edge having greater hiring concerns for people with
intellectual disabilities or mental disorders than for
those with physical disabilities (Kocman, Fischer,
& Weber, 2018), consistent with the experiences of
stigma and discrimination described by employees
with intellectual disabilities in competitive work-
places (Meltzer, Robinson, & Fisher, 2020), a group
that has reported discrimination as their greatest bar-
rier to employment (Jacob et al., 2022).
Doubt or questions about applicant capacity also
emerged as a barrier in a qualitative study of South
Africans engaged in the job search process (McKin-
ney & Swartz, 2021), even when those capabilities
were not clearly associated with the responsibilities
of a position. In Austria, 30 hiring managers empha-
sized a lack of appropriate jobs, frequently relating
this to high organizational job requirements (Koc-
man, Fischer, & Weber, 2018). The purely structural
argument in that study seemed undermined how-
ever by the association found between perceived
employment barriers and manager attitudes. While
perceptions about people with intellectual disabilities
were not said to inﬂuence beliefs about their employ-
ability, managers who saw them as more different
from people without disabilities, or who preferred
segregated solutions for them, also perceived them
as having greater employment barriers. In the US,
negative employer assumptions were also reported as
frequent barriers to employment (36%) (Houtenville
et al., 2015).
Negative presumptions about people with dis-
abilities, as well as lack of consideration for job
applicants with disabilities can play out as employ-
ment barriers at various stages of the application
process and beyond. Evidence of discriminatory

in which job applicants’ cover letters disclosed either

Even though disability type was matched to job

included inaccessible forms or tests, and interviews

by Jacob et al. (2022), it was found that both employ-
ers and coworkers expressed prejudiced attitudes as
well as discrimination against employees with intel-
lectual disabilities.
1.3. Societal-level barriers
Societal-level prejudices can manifest not only in
laws but also in policies and practices that inhibit
the ability of people with disabilities to participate
equally in employment opportunities. For recent col-
lege graduates in Taiwan, perception of acceptance
by society and the job market were viewed as the key
factors explaining employment status, while neither
disability type nor disability level were signiﬁcant
(Chen, 2015). Limited protections for prospective
employees with disabilities (outside of those hired by
large employers) may also reﬂect societal prejudices.
Even when protections are in place, they can have
mixed results, such as employer concerns about the
ramiﬁcations of hiring a person with disabilities given
regulations concerning their dismissal (Bonaccio et
al., 2019; Kocman, Fischer, & Weber, 2018).
Friedman (2020) found that lower disability
employment rates were signiﬁcantly correlated with
state disability prejudice scores. Misperceptions feed
these types of bias. For example, the unmet need
for workplace accommodations reported in the US
(Maestas, Mullen, & Rennane, 2019) is likely exac-
erbated by employers failing to recognize that most
employees with disabilities do not require workplace
accommodations and that, when they do, their costs
tend to be low (Baker et al., 2018).
Other societal-level barriers to employment for
some people with disabilities include fear of loss or
reduction in governmental supports (e.g., Veterans
Affairs disability payments or caregiver stipends in
the US, or disability pensions in Australia) if they
attain a paid position (Gong & He, 2019; Shepherd-
Banigan et al., 2021). Transportation is also a major
concern. In a nationally representative survey of
Americans with disabilities, respondents indicated
that lack of transportation was the third most common
job search barrier that they encountered, following
insufﬁcient training, and employer misconceptions
about their capabilities (Houtenville et al., 2015).
Although prejudice and discrimination may originate
in the larger national (Shaw et al., 2014) and state
(Friedman, 2020) contexts within which employers
are situated, they can have deleterious effects on the
employment of individuals with disabilities at differ-
ent stages of the employment process.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Participant recruitment
Participants for this study were recruited from
a Qualtrics opt-in Internet panel. Qualtrics and its
partners use various recruitment techniques includ-
ing member referrals, targeted email lists, gaming
sites, customer loyalty web portals, permission-based
networks, and social media. Qualtrics utilizes third
party partners to design samples meant to meet
recruitment quotas. Because of our interest in peo-
ple with disabilities, we recruited from the general
adult and the chronic conditions panels to oversample
respondents with disabilities (about half of the total
sample). While this generated a non-probability sam-
ple design, respondents were successfully recruited
from each of the 50 states, and the resulting data were
weighted to be more representative of the population
(described below).
In all, n = 6,350 people accessed the online sur-
vey; n = 2,252 screened out of the survey for being
under 18 or because their demographic quota was
already met. The online study consent form that
preceded access to the survey also speciﬁed the
requirement that participants be younger than 65.
This was done to limit our sample to traditionally
working aged adults. Of the remaining n = 4,098, an
additional n = 1,062 were screened out for failing
to provide informed consent or for failing built-in
survey quality checks used to detect inattentive or
rushed responses. More speciﬁcally, to avoid exclu-
sion, respondents were required to correctly answer
one attention check, and responses completed in less
than 2 minutes were rejected as rushed. These proce-
dures resulted in a ﬁnal analytic sample of n = 3,021,

2.2. Data collection procedures and instrument
The survey was conducted over the course of
ﬁve days at the end of March and beginning of
April 2019 and took an average of seven minutes
to complete. Both Qualtrics and their third-party
data hosts employ double opt-in veriﬁcation as well
as encryptions for all transmitted data, that is later
destroyed. The data available to researchers con-
tained no personally identiﬁable information but
prospective participants were informed that there
would be restricted researcher access to electronic
copies,whichwouldbesecurelystored,andpassword
protected. Although the employment-related barrier
questionnaire was not expected to generate any harm
to prospective respondents, they were nonetheless
informed that their participation was completely vol-
untary, that they could refuse to answer any question,
and that they could withdraw their consent at any
time. The primary ethical concern with the involve-
ment of human subjects was the risk of identifying
subjects based upon their responses. Stored data did
not include email addresses or location (ISP address),
and only summarized data is presented here. While
there were no explicit beneﬁts to participation, any
small incentives or compensation for survey comple-
tion (e.g., cash, free downloads, or reward points)
were provided at the discretion of Qualtrics and its
partners. This study was approved by the Univer-
sity of New Hampshire Institutional Review Board
(# 8054) after review of its data collection protocol.
The data collection instrument was based primar-
ily on the CPS disability supplement, with additional
questions drafted by the investigators and vetted by a
disability and employment expert panel. The survey
included a variety of demographic measures, with a
focus on disability status and type, employment status
and experiences, job search, and job quality. Because
prior research has shown that the six disability iden-
tiﬁers used in many federal surveys, such as the
American Community Survey, fail to identify many
people with disabilities (Burkhauser, Houtenville, &
Tennant, 2014), additional questions were included to
capture additional types of disabilities that individu-
als might experience (e.g., psychiatric and learning
disabilities). Speciﬁcs regarding the main dependent
and independent variables are provided below.

ability status, was assessed by asking up to nine

The key dependent measure in this study was
whether someone reported a barrier to employment.
This was measured by responses to the following
question asked of those who reported they were not
working at the time of survey, Do you consider any of
the following to be a barrier to employment for you:
• Lack of education or training
• Lack of transportation
• Loss of beneﬁts or government assistance
• Needforspecialjobfeaturesoraccommodations
• Employer or coworker attitudes
• Disability or health condition
• Some other problem (if yes, please specify)
Respondents could select all that apply. Those who
reported any of these barriers (including “other”)
were counted as having experienced a barrier to
employment. Among those who reported any type of
disability, a follow-up question asked whether each
reported employment barrier was “due to a health
condition or disability.”
The survey instrument also captured standard
demographic measures, including education (for
which eight categories were presented and were
collapsed into four: High school graduate or less,
some college, 4-year degree, and advanced degree);
total family income (seven categories were pre-
sented, with a low of under $15,000 and a high
of over $100,000, which were collapsed into
four categories: under $30,000, between $30,000
and under $60,000, $60,000 to under $75,000,
$75,000 to under $100,000, and everything higher);
race/ethnicity, derived from asking about Hispanic
ethnicity (yes/no), and race (multiple responses per-
mitted); age (coded from year of birth), and region
(Northeast, Midwest, South and West), constructed
based on the state of residence identiﬁed.
2.3. Description of analytic approach
To address this study’s research questions, descrip-
tive statistics and logistic regression models were
conducted for each barrier type as well as for the
presence of any barrier. Prior to multivariate analy-
ses, survey data were weighted by gender, age group,
household income, race, and region of the coun-
try using 2017 5-year American Community Survey
estimates. The median weight was 0.72 (range 0.4
to 6.0). The unweighted study sample was dispro-
portionately female and slightly younger than the
working-age population. Hispanic or Latinx partic-
ipants were also underrepresented in the unweighted
sample. The unweighted sample was similar to the
overall population regarding educational attainment,
region, and receipt of Social Security beneﬁts (SSI
or SSDI), though it was slightly lower on average
income distribution. This may be due to our oversam-
pling of people with disabilities, who are less likely
to be employed. The weighted sample is presented
throughout the rest of the ﬁndings, unless otherwise
speciﬁed. The demographics of the unweighted and
weighted sample are presented in Table 2.
3. Results
3.1. Demographics
As displayed in Table 2, 47 percent of our weighted
sample reported having one or more disabilities. The
most frequently reported disabilities by sample par-
ticipants included those pertaining to upper body
mobility (18%), cognition (17%), psychiatric state
(16%), walking upstairs (13%), vision (11%), and
hearing (10%). Each of the remaining disability types
were reported by fewer than 10% of respondents.
3.2. Employment participation
In terms of employment status, 59.2% of people
with disabilities were currently working, compared
to 68.0% of those without a disability. Of those who
were unemployed at the time of the survey, 76.0%
had been employed previously and 24.0% had never
been employed. Among those previously employed,
32.6% of people with disabilities were looking for
work, compared to 36.8% of those without a disabil-
ity. See Table 3.

3.5. Demographic and socioeconomic predictors
of employment barriers
Based on logistic regressions conducted on demo-
graphic variables for the presence of each individual
employment barrier, as well as the presence of any
barrier at all, it was observed that only the presence
of a disability signiﬁcantly increased the likelihood
of an unemployed person reporting an employment
barrier (analysis not shown). Given these results,
logistic regressions were rerun replacing the presence
of any disability variable with dichotomous dummy
variables for each disability type. For this analysis
cognitive, learning, and intellectual or developmen-
tal disabilities were combined, as were the physical
challenges of climbing stairs, walking a quarter mile
or upper body mobility difﬁculties.
Results of this analysis showed that experiencing
any employment barrier was signiﬁcantly associated
with disability status, family income, age, and edu-
cational attainment (see Table 6). Again, disability
was the predictor most signiﬁcantly associated with
increased risk of experiencing an employment bar-
rier at all. Of these, having a cognitive, learning,
or intellectual disability most signiﬁcantly increased
the likelihood of reporting any employment barrier.
The only unique employment barrier signiﬁcantly
associated with this likelihood was one’s disabil-
ity or health condition. For people with physical
disabilities, the risk of experiencing some employ-
ment barriers increased signiﬁcantly in terms of the
need for accommodations, loss of beneﬁts, or bar-
riers because of their disability or health condition,
relative to people without physical disabilities. They
were signiﬁcantly less at risk however for lack of
transportation, or other barriers. People with psy-
chiatric disabilities experienced signiﬁcantly greater
employment barrier risks regarding their disability or
ahealthcondition,butsigniﬁcantlylessriskregarding
need for accommodation or loss of beneﬁts than those
without psychiatric disabilities. Notably, people with
vision disabilities were signiﬁcantly less likely than
those without a vision disability to report coworker
attitudes as an employment barrier than those without

3.6. Predicting attribution of other barriers to
disability or health
In this study, survey respondents were not only
asked to identify direct employment barriers, but also
to indicate whether a barrier may be attributable to a
disability or health condition that they have. Because
attribution for each non-disability or health condition
was at least partially attributed to one’s disability or
health condition (see Table 4), logistic regressions
were run to identify the demographic and socioe-
conomic predictors of employment barriers for the
subset of respondents who identiﬁed themselves as
having a disability. Because of an insufﬁcient num-
ber of cases, loss of beneﬁts (n = 53) and “other”
employment barriers (n = 33), were excluded from
this analysis. (See Table 7).
Results for this group of respondents with dis-
abilities revealed sex, education, and family income
were signiﬁcantly associated with the likelihood
of reporting any employment barrier attributed to
one’s disability or health condition. Looking across
employment barrier types in our analysis, however,
of these only the educational attainment barrier was
signiﬁcantly attributed to one’s disability or health
condition. In terms of speciﬁc barriers, coworker atti-
tudes as a barrier were signiﬁcantly more likely to
be attributed to a disability or health condition only
among people holding advanced degrees, compared
to those with a high school diploma or less. Similarly,
the need for accommodation was attributed to a dis-
ability or health condition more often among people
with a bachelor’s degree, compared to a high school
diploma or less.
While the need for accommodations attributed
to a disability was signiﬁcantly associated with the
majority of demographic variables in this analysis,
the nature of the relation was not uniform. While
age was conversely associated with the likelihood
of attributing the need for accommodation to a dis-
ability or health condition, people with disabilities
who were Black and non-Hispanic were signiﬁcantly
less likely to attribute the need for accommodation
to their disability or health condition, than White,
non-Hispanic people with disabilities. People with
disabilities residing in the Midwest or Northeast were
also signiﬁcantly less likely to attribute the need for
accommodation to their disability or health condition
than people with disabilities residing in the West.
3.7. Employment barriers by educational
attainment
Because of the signiﬁcance of educational attain-
ment in this study, further analysis was conducted to
look for associations between employment barriers
of unemployed study participants and their levels of
educational attainment. Results displayed in Table 8
indicate that there were signiﬁcantly inverse relations
betweeneducationalattainmentandemploymentbar-
riers relating to lack of education or training, lack of
transportation, and disability or health condition.

abilities reported disability or health condition most
often, and people without disabilities reported orga-

In general, people with physical mobility, learning-
related, or psychological limitations experienced
signiﬁcantly more employment barriers than people
with vision or hearing limitations, compared to those
with no disabilities. Statistically signiﬁcant differ-
ences were found between people with and without
disabilities across barrier types, except regarding a
lack of education or training, at the job acceptance
stage of the job acquisition process.
While previous research led us to expect particu-
lar employment barriers among survey respondents
with self-reported disabilities, such as lack of qual-
iﬁcations or inaccessible transport (Devine et al.,
2021), one surprise was the similarly high prevalence
of job seekers without disabilities who experienced
any job search barriers (90%). Even though peo-
ple with and without disabilities report encountering
some of the same types of barriers to employment
(Phillips et al., 2020), our ﬁnding that people with
disabilities experienced barriers signiﬁcantly more
frequently when searching for work was also some-
what expected (Meltzer, Robinson, & Fisher, 2020;
McKinney & Swartz, 2021).
All respondents, regardless of disability status,
cited a lack of education or training and lack of
transportation as among the most common barri-
ers. As in past studies, people with disabilities were
most likely to identify their disability or health
condition as the major barrier to employment. An
unexpected ﬁnding was that respondents without dis-

abilities ranked employer or coworker attitudes as a
more frequent employment barrier than people with
disabilities. This may reﬂect other social identities
among participants without disabilities that were not
explored in depth in our study. For example, L’Horty
et al. (2022) have found that racial and ethnic dis-
crimination can occur to a similar or even greater
extent than disability-related discrimination in some
occupations. Although we did not ﬁnd racial iden-
tity to be signiﬁcantly associated with employer or
coworker attitudes directly, our ﬁnding that Black,
non-Hispanic people with disabilities were signiﬁ-
cantly less likely to attribute accommodation barriers
to their disability implies a need for further investiga-
tion of racial disparities in disability employment by
occupation. Moreover, because of the lack of a sta-
tistically signiﬁcant association between employer or
coworker attitudes and gender, education, income, or
age, as well as race or ethnicity for those without
disabilities, these attitudinal barriers to employment
may instead be based on other marginalized identi-
ties.Inaddition,itmaybetheco-existenceofmultiple
prejudicial attitudes that, as was reported in a scop-
ing review on employment barriers for individuals
with disabilities, be particularly problematic, as was
observed for veterans with disabilities (Shaw et al.,
2014). Considering these unobserved possibilities,
it is therefore suggested that future research efforts
should incorporate other identities, such as immigra-
tion status (Keita & Valette, 2019), sexual orientation
(Fric, 2021), and veteran status in examinations of
employment barriers for individuals with disabilities
that have been found to be meaningful in the past but
that were not included in our survey.
Another potentially surprising ﬁnding, that runs
counter to some of the economic literature (Barlev-
Kotler, Rivkin, & Sandler-Loeff, 2014; Brew &
Gleason, 2014), was the low ranking of loss of bene-
ﬁts as a barrier among people with disabilities. Such
concerns may be reﬂected in the extreme hesitancy
(only 1%) of working aged Israelis with disabilities to
increase their income beyond a prior disability ben-
eﬁt income cut-off point even three years after the
allowance amount was lifted (Barlev-Kotler, Rivkin,
& Sandler-Loeff, 2014).
Meanwhile in the U.S., Brew and Gleason cal-
culated that only 0.2% of people returned to work
once they started receiving SSI and SSDI beneﬁts.
Providing greater context to these reports however,
Yosef, Soffer, and Malul (2019) demonstrated that
individuals with disabilities were actually more likely
to prefer to maintain whatever their current income
source was regardless of whether it was employment
or welfare, than to change it.
In our study, loss of beneﬁts was infrequently men-
tioned (10.7%), ranking sixth of the seven barriers
listed. Anand and Sevak (2017) observed a similarly
low ranking of beneﬁts loss as an employment bar-
rier among nonworking individuals with disabilities,
although at a higher percentage (23%). This does not
mean that loss of beneﬁts is not a legitimate con-
cern, but only that the other employment barriers
listed were considered more problematic for survey
respondents. Its rank below disability, education and
training, lack of transportation, need for accommoda-
tions, and employer or coworker attitudes however is
consistent with previous U.S. Bureau of Labor Statis-
tics ﬁndings in 2019 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics,
2021). Moreover, loss of beneﬁts was reported as a
barrier by about 7% of people without disabilities,
who may have been receiving beneﬁts for income, as
opposed to disability-related reasons. Interestingly,
the loss of beneﬁts employment barrier became less
frequent across the different job acquisition stages
for those with and without disabilities. This is consis-
tent with prior research indicating that fear of beneﬁt
loss can even discourage individuals from seeking
employment-related services (Shepherd-Banigan et
al., 2021). Loss of beneﬁts was also signiﬁcantly
more problematic for people with physical disabil-
ities and least signiﬁcant for people with psychiatric
disabilities. Taken together our ﬁndings regarding
loss of beneﬁts argues for future qualitative research
to help explain the greater reports of this concern
amongthosewithphysicaldisabilitiesthanthosewith
psychiatric disabilities as well as policy interventions
such as New York state’s six-month, 100% income
disregard for residents receiving public assistance,
based on a successful New York City pilot study
(Duffy & Seeley, 2023).
Because this study was conducted prior to the
COVID-19 pandemic we expect that the need for
accommodations as an employment barrier may have
changed for prospective employees both with and
without disabilities. This supposition is supported
by a 2022 survey of workplace supervisors, 78%
of whom stated that their organization established
or changed their accommodation process due to
COVID-19-related issues (Houtenville et al., 2022).
More speciﬁcally, among participating supervisors,
47% indicated a moderate or large increase in
the percentage of paid employees working from
home. Sixty-nine percent of those reporting this
increase also indicated that they anticipated the per-

centage of those engaged in telework to continue
growing.
Given the historically low unemployment rate in
the US (Butera, 2023), and because the need for
accommodations employment barrier was most prob-
lematic at the job acceptance stage, it is likely that it
would be perceived as less of a barrier now than prior
to the pandemic. Telework, in response to America’s
current labor shortage (Ferguson, 2023), is also likely
to reduce the lack of transportation that people with
and without disabilities ranked as one of their top
three employment barriers.
Nevertheless, teleworking may not be a uni-
versal solution. As a recent pre-post difference-
in-difference analysis revealed, even when rural
community members were provided access to a wire-
less broadband network, it did not appear to alter
their educational or employment-related outcomes
(Valentin-Sivico et al., 2023). Therefore, as was sug-
gested by a recent scoping review (Fortune et al.,
2022), in order to uncover the differential use and
beneﬁts of telework interventions, it may ﬁrst be
necessary to pursue qualitative research to more com-
prehensively appreciate how and for whom they are
more or less effective.
Given that telework will not remediate all
transportation-related employment barriers for indi-
viduals with disabilities, other strategies must be
enlisted, such as transportation accommodations,
which have been positively correlated with current
employment for people with either multiple dis-
abilities or exclusively physical disabilities in the
past (Anand & Sevak, 2017). Because transportation
barriers vary and may include lack of availabil-
ity, challenges commuting when transportation is
available, and other issues (Inge et al., 2018), trans-
portationsolutionswillneedtoaccountforcontextual
factors. For instance, interventions to improve trans-
portation options for employment-seeking people
with disabilities in rural areas, such as the “Auto-
mated Driving Systems for Rural America” which
was positively received by its older adult participants
(Mason, Carney, & Gaspar, 2022), may be sensitive
to population density differences.
Because e-learning and distance learning has how-
ever been shown to be beneﬁcial for some individuals
with learning disabilities (Petretto et al., 2021), its
expanded use since the onset of the pandemic may
similarly have reduced some of their educational or
training barriers. The signiﬁcance of education on
employment continually emerged across study ﬁnd-
ings but showed variation by job acquisition phase
and barrier type. Similar to prior research, we found
that insufﬁcient education or training was consid-
ered a primary employment barrier for people with
disabilities (Sundar et al., 2018). This is not surpris-
ing, given that people with disabilities with college
degrees are more likely to have acquired a two-year
or community college degree, which is less likely
to be associated with post-collegiate employment
(Phillips et al., 2022). Regardless of disability status,
lower levels of education were signiﬁcantly associ-
ated with educational, transportation, disability, or
health condition-related employment barriers. Future
interventions and research might seek to differenti-
ate between the beneﬁts of academic and vocational
training programs as a recent longitudinal study in
Australia has shown that graduates of apprentice-
ship and training programs achieved and sustained
positive employment outcomes (Thoresen, Cocks, &
Parsons, 2021).
Although we found signiﬁcant differences in the
extent to which insufﬁcient education or training was
anemploymentbarrierbetweenpeoplewithandwith-
out disabilities during job acquisition stages, it was
notsigniﬁcantatthejobacceptancestage.Insufﬁcient
educationortrainingwasmostsigniﬁcantattheappli-
cation stage, which indicates that training, policies,
and interventions targeting this aspect of job seeking
might be particularly beneﬁcial for people with dis-
abilities across disability types, as well as for people
without disabilities.
Finally, where disability type was a signiﬁcant fac-
tor in the experience of job acquisition challenges,
people with intellectual, cognitive, or psychiatric dis-
abilities experienced barriers most frequently. This
ﬁnding suggests the need for future interventions
directed at improving the employment outcomes for
members of these subpopulations, who, as previously
indicated, may be at greater risk of unemploy-
ment (Kocman, Fisher, & Weber, 2018; McAlpine
& Alang, 2021). Successful efforts such as Mentra’s
online job matching website for neurodivergent indi-
viduals may for example provide a useful model for
individuals with different mental health challenges
(Peluso, 2023).
Other signiﬁcant differences by disability type
were also identiﬁed. People with vision disabilities
were signiﬁcantly less likely than people with other
types of disabilities to report negative attitudes as
a barrier. Although negative employer attitudes had
previously been ranked as a greater barrier by college
graduates with visual impairments seeking employ-
ment (Antonelli, Steverson, & O’Mally, 2018), our

ﬁndings may differ because they controlled for edu-

individuals with advanced degrees were more likely
to perceive accommodation needs as a barrier. Fur-
ther qualitative research may help explain this ﬁnding
as both those with and without disabilities described

Despite the contributions of this study, some limi-
tations must be acknowledged. First, our survey was
based on self-reported perceptions of barriers by non-
working individuals with and without disabilities.
Unfortunately, more objective measures of the range
of impediments considered here would be difﬁcult
to measure in alternative ways. Reviewing reports of
hiring discrimination for instance would likely under-
count discouraged and underemployed job seekers. In
addition, the percentage of the sample with disabili-
ties that is employed is high (59%), which may be a
result of the expanded deﬁnition of disability (captur-
ing 9 disability types instead of the 6 more commonly
used). We chose this approach in hopes of disaggre-
gating cognitive disability and investigating differ-
ences among some of the supplemental types of dis-
abilities. Unfortunately, sample sizes did not always
permit these analyses. Another limitation is that the
disability question skip patterns we used may have
underrepresented people with concentration, mem-
ory, or decision-making disabilities who also have
learning, psychiatric, and developmental disabilities.
The Qualtrics online sample is also biased towards
people who have internet access. People with dis-
abilities are overrepresented among those lacking
internet access, which can be an employment barrier
as well as a barrier to study participation. Although
the Qualtrics Internet panel was derived from a non-
probability sample design, the researchers applied
weights for gender, age group, household income,
race, and region of the country using 2017 5-year
American Community Survey estimates to provide
more representative results.
The survey instrument adapts survey questions
that have been developed by federal statistical agen-
cies, including questions on disability and barriers
to employment present on the Current Popula-
tion Survey-Disability Supplement. This approach
allowed us to beneﬁt from the methodological work
of the designers of such national studies, although
we did not speciﬁcally test for validity of the speciﬁc
survey instrument used in this study.
Lastly, although our conceptualization of employ-
ment barrier levels was based on a prior conceptu-
alization (Sundar et al., 2018), there can be overlap
between categories. Employer and coworker attitudes
may reside within the individual or the corporate
culture of a particular organization, for example. Sit-
uating employer and coworker attitudes within the
workplace, however, may be particularly meaningful
in explaining one’s overall success with job acquisi-
tion and be a useful basis of comparison in regard to
retention.
5. Conclusion
This study assessed individual-, organizational-
, and societal-level employment barriers faced by
people with and without disabilities across different
stages of the job search process. The results indi-
cate that employment barriers faced by people with
and without disabilities vary less in nature than by
degree. Thus, improvements in transportation, and
better alignment between education and training and
the job market may beneﬁt all prospective employees,
aswellasemployers.Becausepeoplewithdisabilities
stillexperiencemoreemploymentbarriers,additional
efforts targeting recruitment, the application pro-
cess, and job negotiations are warranted. In light
of the novel ﬁnding that insufﬁcient training was
seen most frequently as a barrier at the application
stage for people with and without disabilities, this
is a ripe area for more attention by researchers and
employment service professionals, such as vocational
rehabilitation, to address challenges at this particular
stage.
