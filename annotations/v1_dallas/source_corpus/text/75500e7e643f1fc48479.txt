					There's no denying women's prominence in higher education. According to the National Center for Education Statistics, nearly 73 percent of female high school graduates enrolled in college in 2015, compared with nearly 66 percent of their male counterparts. 
It would seem that staffing at colleges and universities would start to resemble the population of students earning those credentials. But this assumption has yet to play out: "The phrase 'the higher the fewer' is used to recognize the fact that even though women have higher education attainment levels than men, this is not reflected in the number of women holding positions with high faculty rank, salary, or prestige. This characterization is apt when it comes to the percentage of full-time instructional faculty with tenure. Women of all races and ethnicities are more likely to hold lower-ranking faculty positions," explains Heather L. Johnson in The American Council on Education's 2017 report Pipelines, Pathways, and Institutional Leadership.
Johnson further explores this discrepancy when it comes to compensation: "Men outearn women by: $13,874 at public institutions [and] $18,201 at private institutions." Johnson also notes that "While the number of women presidents has increased since 1986, as of 2016, women only hold 30 percent of presidencies across all institutions of higher education."
While many female professionals recognize their shared challenges and aid one another's success, female bullying is not uncommon in the workplace. Here's what professionals should know.
Workplace Bullying
The Workplace Bullying Institute (WBI) notes that over 60 million Americans are impacted by workplace abuse, whether they experience it directly or they witness abuse. WBI's 2017 data around gender reveals: "Bullies were more likely to be men (70%) than women (30%). Both men and women perpetrators disproportionately chose women as targets for bullying. Women bullied women in 67% of cases; men [bullied women] in 65% of cases. Women were targets in 66% of cases overall."
WBI finds that healthcare and education are the industries that exhibit the greatest concentration of bullies. Sadly, workplace abuse often leads to job loss. WBI explains: "To stop it, 65% of targets lose their original jobs."
A Loss of Support
Bullies' abuse campaigns are often enabled by peers. Whether co-workers look the other way or they actively contribute, both approaches further victimize targets.
Minority professionals, including women, are "more heavily affected by the effects of workplace bullying and protected less from social support from coworkers" explains Lori Solomon, summarizing a study compiled the Georgia Health Policy Center in the University's Andrew Young School of Policy Studies. According to the GSU researchers, minority professionals, including women, can count less on their coworkers for protection and support, should they experience abuse.
Advancement stands to bring more targeted abuse: "High visibility roles often bring women a distinctive and gendered version of criticism and scrutiny. For women of color, the criticism often had racial overtones as well" explains Higher Education Resource Services' (HERS) report Stories from the Summit Trail: Leadership Journey of Senior Women in Higher Education, authored by Kelly Hannum, Shannon Muhly, Pamela Shockley-Zalabak, and Judith S. White.
Targeting Talent
Sadly, for both the target and the institution, the qualities that make an employee attract the attention of a bully tend to be the attributes of a standout employee. WBI founder, Dr. Gary Namie explains: "[Bullies] do this because they cannot stand the competence, the strength, the emotional intelligence, the likability of the targeted person. They just want to get rid of them."
WBI finds that those in leadership roles are most often the bullies; they're emboldened because "72% of employers deny, discount, encourage, rationalize, or defend it."
Self-Protectionism
Higher education is competitive. This can be a good thing -- a driving force that fuels individual accomplishment. But it can also have a negative side, especially as it plays out in organizational culture: "[W]e need to be very careful about homogenizing all women and all behavior. Context, culture, personalities, and age/stage all matter…We live in a competitive society and women realistically see, sadly, that there are only a limited number of people who can succeed in given situations; this means we tend to be self-focused and eager to self-promote in an effort to succeed in a world of scarce resources," explains Karen Gross, professor, former President of Southern Vermont College, former Senior Policy Advisor to the US Department of Education during the Obama Administration and author of "Trauma Doesn't Stop at the School Door." "If we know that fewer women progress up the proverbial male ladder, then every woman can feel (and they are not wrong) other women can crowd her out. In a sense, it is like the awful children's game, Musical Chairs. By way of example, if there is only one tenure track slot available for a woman, it is understandable that we create a competitive, not cooperative, environment."
Gross adds: "There is a vast difference between what women speak about and share concerning their support of other women in private or outside the workplace and what actually happens in the trenches: it is the difference between law on the books and law in action. Often, our actions don't support our rhetoric because in the midst of 'battle,' we self-protect."
Fortifying Culture
While institutional leaders may give lip service to inclusivity and belonging, the truth behind that rhetoric reveals itself in how it feels to work in the culture they've built. Do employees truly feel comfortable and well-positioned to contribute, advance, and thrive? Do they have access to mentors and sponsors to whom they can relate and who can help them shape their plans and trajectories?
Fortifying culture by supporting female professionals stands to neutralize the emotional climate that invites bullying. Gross explains: "Women moving up the professional ladder often do not have female mentors; that absence makes it hard to see the pathway ahead and the possibility that working together with other women can be beneficial, not harmful, to success."
Gross recommends: "[F]orm a group of women employees to work on institutional culture. Invite women to join and then discuss topics that are related to bullying and lack of support and lack of inclusion… My guess and my experience tell me that many who bully are themselves insecure and unable to control their personal behavior. Some may not know the impact their actions and words are having on others. So, if there can be an environment of support and inclusion created, then those bullying may curb their behavior if they become more comfortable." Fortifying culture to better support staff creates an environment where bullying is less likely to root. It's a worthy investment that stands to benefit staff and bolster retention.
