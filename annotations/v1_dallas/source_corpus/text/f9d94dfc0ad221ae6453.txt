Abstract
This qualitative study explores the lived experiences of undocumented Latina survivors of intimate partner violence (IPV) and their interactions with law enforcement. Existing literature identifies Latina immigrants as disproportionately affected by IPV, but limited research has focused on how undocumented status intersects with survivors’ seeking help and police interactions. Using semi-structured interviews with 17 women from Central and South America, thematic analysis reveals deep mistrust of police and a lack of cultural competency among officers. Findings underscore the need for trauma-informed, culturally responsive policing to ensure the safety and dignity of undocumented IPV survivors.
Keywords: Intimate partner violence, Undocumented Latinas, Law enforcement, Qualitative
Background
While national statistics on the prevalence of Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) among women in the United States vary across studies, there is no denying that IPV affects individuals across all demographics (Esperanza United, 2021). IPV encompasses physical, sexual, and psychological harm inflicted by a current or former partner (CDC, 2024). Physical violence may involve hitting, slapping, knocking down, grabbing, holding, tripping, or pushing. More than 80% of female IPV victims experience some form of physical violence (Catalano, 2013). Survivors are more likely to contact law enforcement once the physical violence reaches a threshold where it becomes frequent and intolerable (Cheng & Lo, 2019). However, despite these statistics, there is a significant gap in data capturing IPV experiences among undocumented individuals. Estimates suggest that immigrant women endure abuse at nearly three times the national average, with rates reaching as high as 49.8% (National Coalition Against Domestic Violence, 2020).
The relationship between law enforcement and IPV survivors is complex. Many survivors experience fear when considering whether to call the police. Research indicates that 80% of individuals who have experienced IPV had never previously contacted law enforcement (Mendoza & Rochford, 2024). Among those who did seek police assistance, 66% reported feeling fearful about making the call. Women with children were particularly hesitant to involve law enforcement due to concerns about potential child removal. This fear was especially pronounced among Black and Latina women, who were more likely to worry about losing custody of their children if they sought police intervention (Mendoza et al., 2024). These concerns are not unfounded, as reaching out to law enforcement does not always lead to increased safety for IPV survivors.
Even when law enforcement responds to an IPV situation, survivors may face greater danger once the officer leaves. Police intervention does not always lead to the arrest of the perpetrator or the confiscation of a firearm. Additionally, survivors themselves may be arrested instead of, or along with, the perpetrator in what is known as a “dual arrest.” The risk of dual arrest is even higher for individuals with limited English proficiency. Research indicates that after law enforcement intervention in a domestic violence situation, only one in five survivors felt safer (Kimberg et al., 2021). In contrast, one-third reported feeling less safe, while half felt that police involvement had no impact on their safety. Furthermore, two in five survivors believed they had experienced discrimination by law enforcement based on gender, race, or other factors (Kimberg et al., 2021).
Cultural-specific barriers impact the experiences IPV survivors have with law enforcement. These barriers can arise from individual’s race, socioeconomic status, immigration history, and language (Espinoza et al., 2022; Garza et al., 2022; Hulley et al., 2023; Monterrosa & Hattery, 2023; Reina & Lohman, 2015). Past research has found that Latina survivors of IPV face great barriers to accessing help from the criminal legal system (Espinoza et al., 2022; Garza et al., 2022; Reina & Lohman, 2015). Mistrust of the law enforcement among undocumented Latina survivors of is deeply rooted in broader historical and sociopolitical experiences of racism and exclusion. Research on BIPOC communities has long documented how discriminatory practices in law enforcement has led to racial profiling, enforcement of illegal immigration, and the over-policing of communities of color. This widespread skepticism and fear of the police—particularly among undocumented individuals—has contributed to negative perceptions of law enforcement (ACLU, 2020). For Latina survivors, this mistrust is compounded by intersecting factors such as fear of their undocumented status and cultural stigma, all of which can discourage reporting abuse and/or seeking protection.
Atouba (2022) studies Latinx young adults' perspectives on national protests over police brutality and racial injustice. The findings reveal that perceptions of systemic racism strongly shape Latinx individuals’ support for protest and their distrust of the criminal justice system. This mistrust is not only a response to individual experiences but also reflects a broader racialized and historical context that informs how Latinx communities, including undocumented survivors of IPV, perceive and interact with systems of authority. By situating survivors’ avoidance of the justice system within this framework, Atouba’s (2022) work deepens our understanding of the structural barriers that prevent many Latina survivors from accessing help and achieving safety.
Furthermore, Latina community members often perceive law enforcement as lacking the necessary knowledge and sensitivity to address the complexities of intimate partner violence (IPV) effectively (Gonzalez-Guarda et al., 2013). While this barrier may be experienced across various communities, Latina survivors face additional culturally specific obstacles that further complicate their interactions with law enforcement. About 1 in 3 Latinas will experience IPV in their lifetime (Esperanza United, 2021). Rather than seeking assistance through formal systems, many Latinas—particularly those who are undocumented—turn to family members, friends, or neighbors for support. For undocumented Latinas, this tendency is often shaped by intersecting forms of discrimination that go beyond gender, race, or ethnicity, creating further barriers to seeking safety and justice. These women also face discrimination due to their immigration status, language barriers, and socioeconomic status (Marrs Fuschel, 2024; Bhuyan & Velagapudi, 2013; Messing et al., 2015). This can lead to fear not only of seeking help from law enforcement but also of avoiding systems of care due to concerns about being reported to authorities.
Cultural and religious influences on help-seeking
Latina survivors of intimate partner violence (IPV), particularly those who are undocumented, face layered barriers to help-seeking that are deeply intertwined with cultural values, religious beliefs, and structural inequalities (Mookerjee et al., 2015). These include fear of deportation, financial stressors, lack of transportation, and negative perceptions of the police and criminal justice system. However, less frequently explored in the literature is the profound influence of religion and family expectations in shaping Latina women's decisions to seek or forgo formal support.
Marriage and family are central pillars of Latino culture, often rooted in religious teachings that emphasize the sanctity and permanence of marriage. According to the Pew Research Center (2023), approximately 67% of Hispanic households in the U.S. are comprised of married couples, and 44% include children under the age of 18. Among Latino couples, religion, children, and communication are consistently cited as components of a healthy marriage (Harris et al., 2008). Importantly, conservatively religious male partners paired with less religious female partners have been associated with increased risk of domestic violence, highlighting the gendered dynamics of religiosity within intimate relationships (Da Silva et al., 2018; Harris et al., 2008). By understanding the connection between cultural norms and religious influences, law enforcement agencies can better address the barriers that undocumented Latina survivors face when seeking safety and justice. This contextual foundation is crucial for enhancing officer response, building trust, and ensuring that interventions are survivor-centered. Hence, the training of police officers in cultural competency and cultural humility is essential. Such training can help officers understand the sociocultural and spiritual dynamics that shape Latina survivors' choices and promote trauma-informed, non-punitive responses rooted in empathy and respect.
Purpose of study
This study addresses notable gaps in the literature on law enforcement responses to intimate partner violence (IPV), with a specific focus on undocumented Latina survivors. While prior research has examined undocumented Latinas’ perceptions of police response, this study offers distinct contributions in several important ways. First, it was conducted during a period when undocumented Latinas were particularly vulnerable due to heightened immigration enforcement and anti-immigrant rhetoric. The study centers the lived experiences of women recruited from healthcare agencies—a community-based setting not commonly accessed in previous IPV research. This recruitment context provides a unique lens into how undocumented survivors, even when engaged with formal care systems, continue to face systemic barriers when interacting with law enforcement in a politically charged climate. Second, this study applies a cultural lens to examine how limited cultural competency among police officers—manifested through language barriers, poor communication, and lack of compassion—shaped survivors’ help-seeking behaviors and perceptions of law enforcement. Few existing studies incorporate this cultural framework when analyzing law enforcement interactions with immigrant survivors of IPV.
Kimberg et al., (2021) found that fear of being reported to police was the most common barrier to seeking care—a theme that strongly emerged in the present study. Participants expressed fear not only for their own deportation but also for the potential deportation of their partners, underscoring the emotional and familial complexities that shape survivors’ decision-making. These concerns were further compounded by limited knowledge of legal rights and the criminal justice system, which, as Messing et al. (2015) argue, can make law enforcement appear more threatening than protective. In the most tragic cases, these failures may contribute to intimate partner homicide.
The findings underscore an urgent need for national attention and effective law enforcement response in addressing IPV among undocumented survivors. By focusing on the act of reporting IPV to law enforcement—rather than solely on the abuse itself—this study contributes actionable knowledge to inform culturally responsive, trauma-informed policing practices and policy reform.
Method
This qualitative research study explores the lived experiences of undocumented Latina survivors of intimate partner violence (IPV), with a particular focus on their perceptions of law enforcement interactions. The participants in this study were drawn from a larger phenomenological investigation examining the experiences of undocumented Latina IPV survivors. While the original study identified multiple themes (Gandarilla-Javier, 2020), this study specifically examines their perspectives on police responses when seeking assistance and expands on a question not addressed in the original study: How do undocumented Latina survivors of IPV perceive and experience interactions with law enforcement when seeking assistance?
In this study, the term “law enforcement” is used to refer to the broader institution, including its policies, practices, and systemic structures that shape responses to intimate partner violence (IPV). In contrast, “police” or “police officers” refers to the individual actors who interact directly with survivors. This distinction is important for understanding how participants’ experiences reflect both personal encounters and broader institutional dynamics.
To explore how these institutional and interpersonal dynamics were experienced, a total of 17 women were interviewed between 2018 and 2019, recruited through convenience sampling. All participants identified as heterosexual women whose primary spoken language was Spanish and originated from Central and South American countries. Data were collected through a semi-structured questionnaire designed to explore their encounters with police when reporting IPV. The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University at [redacted for review], ensuring adherence to ethical research standards.
Recruitment procedure
Recruitment flyers written in Spanish were distributed across multiple agencies in Westchester County, NY, offering outpatient medical and behavioral health services on a sliding scale fee basis. These agencies were selected for their accessibility and relevance to the target population and were familiar to the researcher due to her prior work as a behavioral specialist in similar settings. A convenience sampling method was used, resulting in a sample of 17 participants, all of whom identified as undocumented, Latina, and heterosexual women. At the time of the interview, 15 of the participants were no longer engaged in ongoing medical or behavioral health treatment, underscoring the limited continuity of care often experienced by this population.
It is important to note that although these participants were already accessing medical or behavioral health services, many remained hesitant in reporting IPV to law enforcement due to mistrust and fear of deportation, fear of losing their children, and past negative experiences.
Data collection
Before participating, each woman was informed that all identifiable information would remain confidential, and they consented to participate under these conditions. The participants expressed their strong support for the study’s purpose, emphasizing their commitment to advancing knowledge and fostering a sense of solidarity among women. They highlighted the importance of creating a community that empowers and uplifts one another through informal support networks and active community engagement. The interviews lasted about two hours and were audio recorded and transcribed with permission by participants. The use of pseudonyms and non-identifiable information was used to protect their identities.
Audio recordings were conducted among 17 women as part of the data collection process. Prior to the interviews, the first author read a consent form in Spanish to ensure that each participant fully understood the study's purpose and their rights. Participants were informed that all collected information would remain confidential and be used solely for advancing research, policy, and practice. Each participant voluntarily agreed to participate, and their time and contributions were acknowledged with a $30 cash honorarium.
Analysis
The interviews, originally conducted in Spanish, were translated into English by the first author to ensure the accuracy and authenticity of participants’ narratives. To maintain data credibility and trustworthiness, the first author reviewed the audio recordings and transcripts multiple times, following established best practices for qualitative research (Cope, 2014). Thematic analysis was conducted to identify recurring patterns and insights related to the primary research question: How do undocumented Latina survivors of IPV perceive and experience interactions with law enforcement when seeking assistance? To support the analysis, a manual color-coding technique was employed, with specific colors assigned to themes and subthemes. This approach facilitated the systematic organization of the data and allowed for an in-depth exploration of participants’ lived experiences. Saturation and generalizability were less of a concern due to its focus on capturing the rich, nuanced experiences of the 17 undocumented Latina survivors of IPV. Hence, the purpose of the findings was not to generalize to a larger population but to provide deep insights into the participants lived experiences and perceptions of police interactions. As Creswell et al. (2007) note, qualitative research often prioritizes depth over breadth, aiming to understand complex phenomena within specific contexts rather than seeking statistical representativeness.
Furthermore, to complement the thematic analysis, the first author quantified participants’ responses by systematically coding the transcripts for specific experiences related to law enforcement interactions to provide statistical reporting. These experiences—such as fear of deportation, lack of respect, and communication barriers—were not asked as direct yes/no questions but emerged organically during the semi-structured interviews. The frequency counts presented in Tables 1 and 2 were derived from this coding process, where each transcript was reviewed to identify and tally recurring experiences. This approach allowed for both qualitative depth and descriptive quantification of key themes. The findings contribute to a growing body of literature that emphasizing the importance of cultural competence and trauma-informed practices in law enforcement responses to IPV.

Findings
The findings revealed that despite being recruited from a healthcare or behavioral health setting, most participants (15 out of 17) were no longer engaged in ongoing services. This disengagement underscores why these women felt not only compelled to share their lived experiences but also motivated to advocate for change in how intimate partner violence (IPV) is addressed and criminalized. For many of the participants, their perception of the current support systems was disconnected in providing continuity of care. Nonetheless, recruitment through healthcare settings provided a valuable lens into a marginalized population that, despite initial system contact, remained underserved and vulnerable.
This study explored how undocumented Latina survivors of intimate partner violence (IPV) perceive and experience interactions with law enforcement. The participants predominantly occupied low socioeconomic status (SES) positions, which significantly shaped their experiences with IPV and law enforcement. Educational attainment among the participants was generally limited; most had completed only elementary or middle school, with a few reaching high school. Many participants reported leaving school at an early age due to family responsibilities or disruptions related to migration. Employment was primarily concentrated in low-wage, labor-intensive sectors such as domestic work, childcare, or food service, with several participants reporting unstable or informal employment. Although 76.5% (n = 13) of participants were employed at the time of the study, their financial stability remained uncertain. Several participants reported experiencing exploitation in the workplace, while the majority expressed concerns about their limited access to essential employment benefits such as healthcare, paid leave, and job security that were typically afforded to permanent residents. In addition, many participants were primary caregivers to their children while also working, which made it difficult to secure affordable childcare and access consistent social services. Intersecting factors such as low educational attainment, limited English proficiency, immigration status, and economic insecurity further compounded their vulnerability to IPV and significantly influenced both their willingness and ability to seek help from formal systems, including law enforcement. Two dominant themes emerged from both the quantitative and qualitative data: (1) Police Mistrust and (2) Lack of Cultural Competency. Participants consistently reported deep mistrust of law enforcement, citing fears of deportation, losing custody of their children, or being arrested themselves. Language-communication barriers and lack of cultural competency among officers exacerbated these fears and contributed to harmful or dismissive interactions. Additionally, the data revealed notable distinctions in arrest outcomes based on the gender of the responding officers (Sun, 2007 & Decker et al., 2019). Theme 1: Police mistrust
Mistrust of police was a universal theme, reported by all 17 participants. This mistrust was largely rooted in fears related to their undocumented status. Every participant shared a profound fear of deportation, which often overrode their desire or ability to seek help. Six of the participants (35.3%) who feared losing their children to Child Protective Services added another layer of trauma, while all 17 participants (100%) cited concerns about being arrested themselves. Additionally, only 14 (82.4%) reported fear of losing their job as a result of police involvement.
These fears were deeply rooted with many stemming from prior negative experiences with law enforcement and threats made by their abusers. Several participants shared that their abusers weaponized their immigration status as a tactic to prevent them from seeking police assistance. Participant 2 explained that after calling 911 during a violent episode, the responding officer repeatedly questioned her story,
Estaba muy nervioso cuando llamé, pero pensé que me ayudarían. En cambio, el oficial seguía pidiéndome que repitiera lo que estaba diciendo como si no me creyera. Empecé a llorar porque no sabía qué hacer. Estaba asustado antes, pero después de que se fueron, me sentí aún peor.
I was very nervous when I called, but I thought they would help me. Instead, the officer kept asking me to repeat what I was saying as if he didn’t believe me. I started crying because I didn’t know what to do. I was scared before, but after they left, I felt even worse.
Participant 5 shared a similarly traumatic experience, where the officer issued a warning that made her fear calling again,
Él me dijo que si tenían que regresar, arrestarían a los dos. Yo era la que estaba llena de moretones, pero no les importó. Dijo: 'La próxima vez, los llevaremos a los dos.' No entendía. Yo era la víctima, pero me hicieron sentir como si fuera una criminal.
He told me that if they had to come back, they would arrest both of us. I was the one covered in bruises, but they didn’t care. He said, ‘Next time, we’re taking you both in.’ I didn’t understand. I was the victim, but they made me feel like I was a criminal.
Participant 5 continued, “Todo el tiempo estaba pensando, ¿y si me arrestan? ¿Qué pasará con mis hijos? ¿A dónde irían? Sufrí en silencio durante tanto tiempo porque sabía que si llamaba a la policía, podría perderlo todo.” “The whole time I was thinking, what if they arrest me? What happens to my children? Where would they go? I stayed quiet for so long because I knew that if I called the police, I might lose everything.”
The fear of losing their children was a concern for six of the participants. Participant 4 shared, “El oficial dijo que mis hijos no deberían vivir en un hogar violento. Me sentí atrapada. Si presento cargos, mantengo a mis hijos, pero si no lo hago, riesgo perderlos.” “He said that my children should not be living in a violent home. I felt trapped. If I press charges, I kept my children, but if I don’t, I risked losing them.” Participant 6 added, “Tenía miedo de llamar a la policía de nuevo porque no quería que me quitaran a mis hijos.” “I was afraid to call the police because I didn’t want them to take my kids away.”
However, not all encounters were negative. Participant 10 described a supportive response from a female officer who displayed empathy,
Ella fue muy comprensiva y se ofreció a hablar con mi esposo cuando regresara. Me dio un abrazo, lo cual me hizo sentir apoyada. Mi hija estaba molesta, y el oficial también la abrazó, asegurándole que sus padres no serían arrestados.
She was very understanding and offered to speak with my husband when he returned. She gave me a hug, which made me feel supported. My daughter was upset, and the officer hugged her too, reassuring her that her parents would not be arrested.
This participant’s experience highlights the potential of trauma-informed, compassionate policing in reducing fear and promoting trust among survivors.
Theme 2: Lack of cultural competency
All 17 participants described unprofessional or culturally insensitive behavior from law enforcement. Fourteen participants (82.4%) cited a lack of compassion or empathy, nine (52.9%) reported a lack of respect, and 12 (70.6%) experienced lack of communication. For many, these issues overlapped. Eight participants (47.1%) reported experiencing both a lack of compassion, empathy, and respect, 15 (88.2%) cited both communication and respect, and seven participants (41.2%) experienced all three simultaneously. Participant 12 recalled being judged when she declined to press charges: “El oficial me miró y me dijo: ‘Si es tan malo, ¿por qué no quieres presentar cargos?’ Supe entonces que no entendía; no tenía dinero, no tenía familia aquí, y tenía miedo de lo que pasaría si me iba.”
The officer looked at me and told me, ‘If it’s so bad, why don’t you want to press charges?’ I knew then that he didn’t understand—I had no money, no family here, and I was scared of what would happen if I left.
Participant 9 expressed her frustration: “La policía seguía preguntando, ‘¿Por qué soportaste esto tanto tiempo?’ En mi cultura, no simplemente dejas a tu esposo. Es un pecado. Pero la policía no entendía eso.” “He kept asking, ‘Why did you put up with this for so long?’ In my culture, you don’t just leave your husband. It’s a sin. But the police don’t understand that.”
Several participants shared that male police officers were more likely to dismissed or overlooked their religious or cultural beliefs regarding marriage. Participant 12 recounted calling the police to intervene and stop her partner’s violence, only to be misunderstood: “Llamé a la policía y lo arrestaron. Solo quería que hablaran con él.” “I called the police and they arrested him. I just wanted them to talk to him.” Participant 11 explained that her decision-making was shaped by love and cultural values: “La policía no tenía sentido de la humildad cultural cuando el oficial masculino respondió. Irse no era una opción inmediata para mí. Amo a mi esposo. Simplemente no lo amaba cuando se emborrachaba y me golpeaba.” “There was no sense of cultural humility. Leaving was not an immediate option for me. I love my husband. I just didn’t love him when he got drunk and would get aggressive.”
This pattern reflects how law enforcement responses often failed to account for marianismo—a traditional Latina belief system that emphasizes emotional strength, nurturing,
and spiritual obligation within marriage. For some participants, separation or divorce was viewed as socially unacceptable, and police intervention that disregarded these beliefs resulted in unintended consequences, such as family separation and deeper isolation.
Communication-language barriers significantly impacted the participants’ ability to communicate with law enforcement. Several survivors described being ignored, misunderstood, or even blamed during their interactions due to limited English proficiency. Participant 12 recalled, “Intenté decirles a los oficiales lo que había sucedido en español, pero ellos simplemente continuaron hablando en inglés. Me sentí despreciada y malentendida. Como si no les importara.” “I tried to tell them what happened in Spanish, but they just kept talking over me in English. I didn’t understand, and they didn’t care.” Participant 9 said, “Mi esposo hablaba mejor inglés que yo. Así que, cuando llegó la policía, lo escucharon a él. El oficial me miró y dijo que no había ningún informe que tomar y se fue sin hablarme.” “My husband spoke better English than me. So, when the police came, they listened to him. The officer looked at me and said that there is no report to take and left without talking to me.”
Several of the women described moments of frustration and humiliation. Participant 7 said, “Le dije al oficial de policía que no entendía lo que decía y que ambos hablaban demasiado rápido. El oficial parecía frustrado.” “I told them I didn’t understand what they were saying and that they were speaking too fast. They just ignored me.” Participant 8 added, “Un oficial se frustró y dijo: ‘Si quieres mi ayuda, aprende a hablar inglés.’ Me sentí avergonzado por no entender.” “One officer got frustrated and said, ‘If you want my help, learn to speak English.’ I felt like I was being shamed for not understanding.” Participant 13 shared that the officer who came was bilingual Spanish and was able to speak to both her and her husband to avoid it escalating to an arrest. Participant 13 explained,
Él hablaba suavemente pero era asertivo. Le dijo a mi esposo que si lo arrestaban, perdería su trabajo y luego, ¿qué? Mi esposo estaba apenado y le dijo al oficial que la próxima vez se alejaría. Sentí que el oficial era cariñoso y respetuoso.

Discussion
Law enforcement agencies have struggled to provide consistent and culturally responsive training for effectively addressing intimate partner violence (IPV), especially when working with undocumented Latina survivors who face intersecting barriers such as immigration status, language limitations, cultural norms, and legal challenges. This study highlights how these fears contribute to mistrust of police and reluctance to seek help. Participants described instances where officers escalated situations, failed to communicate effectively, or dismissed their concerns. Survivors with limited English proficiency often felt unheard or blamed, particularly due to the lack of compassion among officers who did not understand their language. Some feared that miscommunication could lead to family separation, compounding their hesitation to seek help from law enforcement.
Notably, participants reported that female officers were generally more empathetic, less judgmental, and more solution-focused than their male counterparts. Although this dynamic was not explored in depth, these accounts suggest a shift in twenty-first-century policing, where female officers may be better positioned to respond to IPV calls with care and sensitivity. These findings underscore the urgent need for culturally competent training that fosters survivor-centered responses and addresses negative perceptions of law enforcement.
Moreover, undocumented women should not feel trapped by the double-edged sword of fearing both continued abuse and the consequences of calling the police (Garza et al., 2022). Officers must be trained to respond compassionately and effectively, offering referrals to safe housing, legal advocacy, culturally competent counseling, and information about survivors' rights—regardless of immigration status. These accounts further reinforce the need for a cultural shift in policing that centers the safety and dignity of undocumented IPV survivors.
Limitations and strengths
This study is not without limitations. First, participants were recruited through healthcare and behavioral health settings, which may have introduced a selection bias favoring individuals who had already accessed some form of formal support. Consequently, the findings may not fully represent undocumented Latina survivors who remain disconnected from service systems. Recruitment through alternative community-based sources—such as churches, healthcare clinics, or shelters—might have revealed even greater reluctance to disclose IPV or engage with formal institutions. Notably, the fact that participants had prior contact with healthcare settings yet still expressed pervasive mistrust of law enforcement underscores the depth of these fears and highlights the need for systemic reform.
Additionally, the use of a convenience sample limits the generalizability of the findings. The small sample size (n = 17) reflects the study’s emphasis on depth rather than breadth, consistent with qualitative research methodology. Language barriers, immigration concerns, and cultural stigma may have influenced participants’ willingness to fully disclose their experiences, despite efforts to build trust and ensure confidentiality.
Despite these limitations, the study offers several strengths. It centers the voices of an underrepresented and often understudied population—undocumented Latina survivors of IPV—through qualitative inquiry that captures the complexity of their lived experiences. The bilingual, culturally attuned approach to data collection, combined with prolonged engagement and trust-building, yielded rich narratives that provide critical insights into the intersection of IPV, immigration status, and law enforcement. These findings can inform future researchers interested in working with vulnerable populations, guide practitioners in developing culturally responsive and trauma-informed interventions, and support community-based services in tailoring outreach and support strategies to better meet the needs of immigrant survivors.
Practice implications
The findings of this study reveal that undocumented Latina survivors of IPV often experience fear, mistrust, and secondary victimization when calling the police for help. These experiences demonstrate a disconnect between law enforcement and the survivors. First, participants consistently reported fear of deportation, language barriers, and cultural insensitivity as reasons for avoiding police contact. To address these issues, law enforcement agencies should implement mandatory, ongoing training that includes trauma-informed care and cultural competency practices (Ramos et al., 2010). This training must be embedded in police academy training and reinforced throughout their law enforcement career to better equip them with the skills needed to effectively respond to the complexities of IPV. Participants also described situations where police escalated the conflict, which in turn increased the risk of further abuse from their partners. In contrast, participants who encountered female officers often reported receiving empathy, respect, and solutions that centered their safety and dignity. Although this researcher did not further explore this dynamic, participants’ accounts suggest a meaningful shift in twenty-first-century policing, with female officers perceived as more effective in de-escalating IPV calls (Sun, 2007). Survivors described female officers as empathetic, nonjudgmental, and solution-focused, in contrast to male officers who were often viewed as dismissive or escalatory. These experiences highlight the need to strengthen training that builds on these effective practices and promotes respectful, trauma-informed responses. Building partnerships between law enforcement and community-based service providers, including police social workers, can help close this gap and ensure survivors receive comprehensive support.
Research implications
The future of research on intimate partner violence (IPV) should prioritize participatory action research (PAR) and ethnographic methodologies to deepen our understanding of survivors lived experiences. These approaches allow researchers to engage directly with undocumented Latina survivors in ways that honor their voices and contextual realities. Ethnographic research can reveal subtle cultural, emotional, and structural influences on how survivors view and engage with law enforcement. Similarly, participatory action research empowers communities to co-create knowledge and solutions, fostering trust and relevance. By using these qualitative methods, future studies can explore how survivors navigate fear of deportation, language barriers, and historical mistrust of police—insights that are critical for shaping more responsive and culturally competent interventions with law enforcement. These methodologies not only validate survivor experiences but also ensure that their perspectives are the forefront of informed research and policy.
Conclusion
Respecting a woman's choice to leave her abuser is essential for promoting self-efficacy and empowerment, leading to better outcomes when women have agency and support from police (Marrs Fuchsel, 2014). This support should include culturally informed police response. Thus, to address these challenges, a paradigm shift in law enforcement training and response is necessary. Police officers should be trained not only in cultural competency but also from a community-oriented policing approach. This would enable police to better understand and respond to the IPV calls (Garza et al., 2022). By fostering compassion, cultural understanding, and language skills among law enforcement personnel, we can create a more supportive and effective system for all IPV survivors-one that prioritizes the well-being of survivors and respecting their autonomy. Officers should be equipped to identify and mitigate secondary victimization by refraining from victim-blaming language, understanding the role of cultural and religious beliefs in survivors' decisions to stay, and improving communication strategies, including access to trained interpreters for non-English-speaking survivors. Without this shift in policing attitudes and policies, undocumented women will continue to fear law enforcement, reinforcing cycles of abuse and deterring survivors from seeking help.
