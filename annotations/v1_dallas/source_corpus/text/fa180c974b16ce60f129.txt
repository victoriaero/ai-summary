Abstract 
Article info 
Purpose: Despite the proliferation of microfinance institutions, many women remain 
financially excluded due to entrenched socio-cultural and institutional barriers. This study 
investigates the factors influencing women’s access to microfinance services in Kinondoni 
Municipality, Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. 
 
Methods: A cross-sectional research design was employed, collecting data from a 
sample of 100 women. The data were analyzed using a probit regression model to assess 
the influence of key variables including marital status, income, loan procedures, and 
perceptions of interest rates on women’s access to microfinance. 
 
Findings: The results show that married women are 28.4 percentage less likely to access 
microfinance, reflecting the influence of patriarchal norms and spousal consent 
requirements. Surprisingly, simplified loan procedures are associated with a 15.7 
percentage point decrease in access, possibly due to hidden complexities or inconsistent 
implementation. Women who perceive interest rates as moderate or high are more likely 
to access microfinance, indicating a preference for institutional reliability over affordability. 
While income has a statistically significant effect, its practical impact on access is limited. 
 
Originality: This study uniquely studies urban women’s access to microfinance in 
Kinondoni. The study reveals unexpected barriers which are linked to simplified loan 
procedures and spousal consent. The study challenges assumptions about affordability 
by highlighting trust-driven perceptions of interest rates 
 
Practical Implications: These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted policy 
reforms, including the removal of spousal consent requirements, standardization of loan 
procedures, introduction of collateral-free lending options, and expansion of community-
based financial literacy programs. Such measures are critical to fostering inclusive 
financial systems and supporting national and international efforts toward gender equality 
and women’s economic empowerment.

1.0 INTRODUCTION 
Microfinance has emerged as a powerful instrument for addressing financial exclusion, 
particularly among women in low-income communities (Lesya and Mwakalobo, 2023; Dimoso and 
Andrew, 2021). It provides access to small loans, savings, insurance, and other essential financial 
services to individuals who are traditionally excluded from the formal banking sector (Feanyi et 
al., 2019; Kitole and Genda, 2024). Women face systemic barriers to credit access due to limited 
ownership of assets, lower literacy levels, and cultural norms that restrict their financial autonomy 
(Ambler et al., 2021). Microfinance addresses these gaps by offering financial solutions tailored 
to the needs of the poor and underserved (Kitole and Genda, 2024). Access to such services 
enables women to invest in small businesses, manage household consumption, and build 
resilience against economic shocks (Bismark, 2017). Importantly, microfinance is more than a 
financial intervention; it is also a social and developmental tool that contributes to women’s 
economic empowerment (Abebe and Kegne, 2023).  It helps shift women from a state of 
dependency to one of active economic participation and decision-making, thereby improving their 
status within the household and community (NMP, 2017). As such, microfinance plays a dual role: 
alleviating poverty and advancing gender equality. 
At the global level, empowering women through improved access to financial services is a critical 
pillar of major development frameworks. The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals 
(SDGs), particularly Goal 5, emphasize the need to achieve gender equality and empower all 
women and girls by 2030 (UNDP, 2015). One of the key strategies toward this goal is the 
expansion of women’s access to credit and financial resources. Evidence from countries such as 
Brazil and India show that gender-focused financial programs have led to a rise in women-owned 
enterprises and increased participation in economic activities (Pinchot and Reilman, 2007). These 
initiatives not only improve individual outcomes but also contribute to broader economic 
development by leveraging the productive potential of women (Njau, 2023). However, access to 
credit alone is not sufficient. The effectiveness of microfinance in empowering women depends 
on the broader social and institutional context (Mia, 2022). Without addressing underlying 
inequalities in education, property rights, and decision-making power, financial access may not 
translate into meaningful empowerment (Lesya and Mwakalobo, 2023).  
Across Africa, many women run microenterprises not by choice but out of necessity (Ambler et 
al., 2021). Despite their vital contribution to local economies, they face several challenges, 
particularly limited access to finance. The International Labour Organization (ILO) highlights 
financial constraints as a major barrier, especially in low-income countries like Malawi and 
Madagascar (Porter & Nagarajan, 2023). In Zimbabwe, a study by Quinn and Zein (2017) found 
that education levels affected women's ability to secure funding, with less educated women often 
unaware of available financing options. 
In Tanzania, microfinance has expanded to reach underserved communities, particularly women. 
It provides an alternative to informal moneylenders who often charge high interest rates 
(Khandker, 2000). Access to microfinance has been linked to improved living standards and

reduced poverty among women (Mayoux, 2001; World Bank, 2014; Ambler et al., 2021). 
However, deep-rooted social norms, gender inequality, and limited control over productive 
resources continue to hinder women’s economic participation (Paul, 2021; Kitole et al., 2025). 
Women are often less educated and concentrated in unskilled jobs, with limited access to land or 
capital (Rwebangira, 1996). In patriarchal systems—especially in areas like Dar es Salaam—land 
ownership and financial decision-making remain male-dominated (Lusasi & Mwaseba, 2020; 
Kamwela, 2020). This study explores the specific institutional, socioeconomic, and demographic 
factors influencing women’s access to microfinance services in Kinondoni Municipality, Dar es 
Salaam. 
2.0. 
LITERATURE REVIEW 
2.1. 
Theoretical Review 
The theory of women’s economic empowerment draws from the broader empowerment 
framework introduced by Rappaport (1981), which emphasizes the importance of gaining access 
to resources, developing personal agency, and exerting control over decisions affecting one’s life. 
Malhotra, Schuler, and Boender (2002) later expanded this theory to include multiple dimensions 
of empowerment—economic, social, familial, legal, and psychological—emphasizing that 
empowerment is both an individual and structural process. This holistic approach is particularly 
relevant for analyzing women’s access to microfinance in Kinondoni Municipality, Dar es Salaam, 
as it allows for the examination of how institutional arrangements, economic status, and 
demographic characteristics jointly shape women's financial inclusion and autonomy. 
Institutional factors play a critical role in shaping women's ability to access and benefit from 
microfinance services. Within the empowerment framework, institutional environments can either 
facilitate or hinder women’s agency by creating enabling or restrictive structures. In this study, 
two institutional variables—loan procedures and perception of interest rates—capture key 
structural dimensions. Loan procedures reflect the accessibility and transparency of financial 
systems. Complex or opaque procedures can act as gatekeepers that reduce women’s agency, 
while simplified processes foster participation and autonomy. Similarly, women’s perceptions of 
interest rates serve as proxies for institutional trust and the perceived fairness of microfinance 
institutions. A woman’s willingness to engage with certain institutions despite high interest rates 
may indicate a strategic exercise of agency, where informed decision-making outweighs cost 
concerns. 
Socioeconomic factors, including education and income, are directly linked to the empowerment 
theory’s emphasis on resource access and cognitive agency. Education enhances women’s 
capacity to interpret financial information, understand procedures, and interact confidently with 
institutional actors. This aligns with the theory’s assertion that empowerment requires not just 
material access but also the ability to make informed choices. Income level, meanwhile, 
represents both a resource and a potential outcome of empowerment. While higher income 
provides the material means to support financial independence, empowerment theory cautions 
that income alone is insufficient; it must be accompanied by the capacity and freedom to manage 
resources autonomously (Naz et al., 2024). These socioeconomic factors thus interact with 
institutional dynamics to shape a woman’s trajectory toward economic empowerment. 
Demographic factors such as marital status, work experience, age, and family size are crucial for 
understanding how personal and household dynamics influence empowerment. Marital status, for 
instance, often reflects intra-household power relations, particularly in patriarchal societies. 
Married women may face spousal control over financial decisions, limiting their autonomy—a

structural barrier recognized in the empowerment literature (Sierra et al., 2024). Work experience 
can enhance agency by building confidence and familiarity with economic activities, while larger 
family size increases financial obligations, influencing the perceived need for credit and financial 
planning (Hadi et al., 2015). Age, though not elaborated in the previous studies, may serve as a 
proxy for life stage, maturity, or social standing, all of which affect access to resources and 
agency. Together, these demographic variables help contextualize women’s microfinance access 
within broader social norms and life circumstances, reinforcing the multidimensional nature of 
empowerment. 
2.2. 
Empirical Review 
Empirical evidence across various contexts has underscored the critical role of microfinance in 
fostering women's economic empowerment, poverty alleviation, and entrepreneurial success. In 
Ethiopia, Abebe and Kegne (2023) demonstrated that microfinance institutions significantly 
contribute to women's entrepreneurship development through credit, savings, and training 
services. Similarly, Bhuiya et al. (2016), using quasi-experimental survey methods in Bangladesh, 
found that access to microfinance led to improved household income and consumption, 
confirming its role in poverty reduction. These results are echoed by Zoynul and Fahmida (2013), 
who showed that microcredit enabled poor women in Bangladesh to improve their living conditions 
and access better housing, particularly through BRAC programs. However, despite such positive 
impacts, several studies also emphasize that microfinance outcomes are not uniformly positive 
and often depend on broader structural factors. 
In Tanzania, studies continue to highlight the complex dynamics influencing women’s access to 
financial services. Kamwela (2020) and Kipesha and Zhang (2018) found that gender gaps in 
microfinance access remain persistent due to sociocultural barriers, limited financial literacy, and 
discriminatory lending practices. Their findings suggest that while microfinance institutions (MFIs) 
are expanding in urban centers like Dar es Salaam, women still face unequal access due to 
factors such as spousal consent requirements, patriarchal household norms, and institutional 
biases. Similarly, Njau (2023), in a study conducted in Kilimanjaro Region, revealed that socio-
demographic factors—particularly marital status and educational attainment—play a significant 
role in shaping access to microfinance services. However, the study also concluded that 
microfinance alone has not been sufficient to dismantle the deeply rooted patriarchal system. 
These findings are consistent with Lusasi and Mwaseba (2020), who explored gender inequality 
and symbolic violence in women’s access to land, arguing that access to resources is often 
constrained by social norms that prioritize male authority. 
Regional and international studies have also highlighted how institutional inefficiencies can 
undermine the intended impacts of microfinance. For instance, Mishi and Kapingula (2012), 
examining the microfinance sector in Zimbabwe, identified high interest rates, lack of collateral, 
and short repayment periods as major obstacles to financial access for women entrepreneurs. 
These constraints reflect a broader pattern found in microfinance institutions across Sub-Saharan 
Africa, where informal screening processes and profit-driven models often exclude the poorest or 
most vulnerable women (Mia, 2022; Naz et al., 2024). Moreover, Daniel (2020) cautioned against 
the polarizing effects of microcredit, noting that in Zambia’s Copperbelt region, unequal access to 
credit exacerbated income disparities rather than reducing them. These findings are supported 
by Hansen et al. (2021), who emphasized the ethical dilemmas faced by commercialized MFIs, 
particularly when empowerment goals clash with profitability. 
In Nigeria, empirical research by Usman (2023) confirmed that education level, age, and 
household size significantly influenced women's ability to access microfinance loans, while

challenges such as inadequate loan amounts, high interest rates, and short loan tenures were 
key constraints. Feanyi et al. (2019) added a psychological perspective to the issue, noting that 
poverty among women in Southwest Nigeria was deeply intertwined with systemic 
disempowerment, and that access to financial services—while important—was insufficient without 
broader social reforms. Similarly, Mayoux (2001) warned that microfinance could inadvertently 
reinforce existing gender roles if not accompanied by targeted empowerment strategies. This 
perspective aligns with the work of Rappaport (1981), who argued for a social policy approach 
rooted in empowerment rather than prevention—one that emphasizes capacity-building, voice, 
and control over economic decisions. 
Recent Tanzanian literature also confirms the critical need for more inclusive and context-
sensitive approaches to microfinance. Mussa and Mwakajinga (2019) found that despite Dar es 
Salaam’s rapid urbanization, financial access remains unevenly distributed, especially among 
informal women entrepreneurs. Lwesya and Mwakalobo (2023) further pointed out that 
microfinance institutions in Tanzania often lack sufficient outreach strategies to engage female-
led SMEs effectively. Meanwhile, Kitole and Genda (2024) explored the resilience of rural women 
entrepreneurs, emphasizing that despite limited resources and institutional support, women 
continue to innovate and drive local economies. This resilience, however, is constantly tested by 
structural inequalities and weak policy implementation. To address these gaps, researchers such 
as Utouh and Kitole (2024) and Kitole (2025) advocate for comprehensive policy reforms, 
including gender-sensitive lending policies, community-based financial education, and stronger 
regulatory oversight. These reforms are critical to advancing Sustainable Development Goal 5 on 
gender equality (UNDP, 2015) and achieving Tanzania’s Vision 2025 objectives (URT, 2021).

3.0. 
METHODS 
This study adopted a cross-sectional research design to examine the factors influencing women’s 
access to microfinance services in Kinondoni Municipality, Dar es Salaam. The design enabled 
the collection of data at a single point in time, making it suitable for identifying existing 
relationships between key variables such as age, education, marital status, income level, and 
access to loans. A stratified sampling technique was employed to ensure the representation of 
women from diverse socio-economic backgrounds, allowing the study to capture the complex 
ways in which institutional, socio-economic, and demographic barriers affect financial access. The 
use of stratification also strengthened the reliability of findings by ensuring inclusivity across 
different subpopulations. This approach was guided by prior research, which emphasizes the 
impact of structural issues—such as lack of collateral, high interest rates, and limited financial 
literacy—on women’s access to credit (Kamwela, 2020; Kipesha and Zhang, 2018). The 
methodology was further aligned with the study’s theoretical framework, which highlights the 
importance of resource access, agency, and institutional conditions in shaping women’s 
empowerment (Dimoso and Andrew, 2021). 
Kinondoni Municipality was selected as the study site due to its status as a rapidly urbanizing and 
economically vibrant district within Dar es Salaam. Despite the presence of multiple microfinance 
institutions (MFIs), women in Kinondoni continue to face significant obstacles in accessing formal 
financial services. These include restrictive lending practices, gender-based norms, and 
institutional limitations that disproportionately affect women entrepreneurs (Mussa and 
Mwakajinga, 2019). The municipality represents a microcosm of broader urban financial 
challenges, where high demand for credit coexists with persistent gender inequalities. Research 
by the World Bank (2020) and other scholars underline that financial exclusion among women in 
urban Tanzania stems not only from economic disadvantage but also from socio-cultural norms 
and regulatory barriers, such as spousal consent requirements and asset ownership constraints. 
By focusing on Kinondoni, this study provides valuable insights into the urban dynamics of 
financial inclusion and contributes to evidence-based strategies aimed at promoting gender-
responsive microfinance policies and inclusive economic growth.

4.2 Determinants of women access to microfinance in Kinondoni municipal 
The analysis shows that married women are 28.4 percentage points less likely to access financial 
services compared to their unmarried counterparts, and this result is statistically significant. This 
negative association may be due to social factors that limit married women’s financial autonomy, 
including household decision-making norms and loan requirements such as spousal consent. 
These constraints may discourage or prevent married women from engaging with financial 
institutions, contributing to their exclusion from formal financial systems. An increase in monthly 
income has a positive but very small effect on the likelihood of accessing finance, with a one TZS 
increase raising the probability by only 0.00000238 percentage points. Although statistically 
significant, this minimal change implies that income alone has limited practical influence on 
financial access. This suggests that non-income-related factors play a stronger role in determining 
whether women can engage with financial services.

Contrary to expectations, simplified loan procedures are associated with a 15.7 percentage point 
reduction in the likelihood of accessing financial services. This unexpected finding may reflect 
underlying issues such as hidden conditions, confusing processes labeled as simple or 
exclusionary practices during implementation. It is also possible that such procedures are not well 
communicated or understood by the target population, thereby limiting their effectiveness. 
Women who perceive interest rates as moderate or high are more likely to access finance, by 
16.4 and 24.6 percentage points respectively. This may indicate a preference for formal lending 
institutions, which are often associated with higher rates but also greater reliability and legitimacy. 
It could also suggest that women with urgent financial needs are willing to accept higher costs in 
exchange for immediate access to funds. These patterns reflect the complexity of financial 
decision making among women and highlight the importance of both affordability and trust in 
financial services. 
5.0. 
DISCUSSION  
The findings of this study both support and challenge aspects of the women’s economic 
empowerment theory, particularly its emphasis on access to resources, agency, and structural 
transformation. The significant negative effect of marital status on women’s access to 
microfinance—where married women were 28.4% less likely to access services compared to their 


Jamaldin et al.                                                                                         Eminent Journal of Social Sciences 
 
 
26 
 
unmarried counterparts—strongly supports the theoretical prediction that structural and relational 
barriers limit women's autonomy. This is consistent with the empowerment framework’s critique 
of patriarchal household dynamics that restrict women’s agency in decision-making (Malhotra et 
al., 2002). Empirically, this aligns with studies from Hai District (Njau, 2023) and Nigeria (Usman, 
2023), affirming that institutional requirements like spousal consent reinforce gendered power 
imbalances. Thus, the findings confirm that marriage can serve as a constraint rather than an 
enabler of women’s financial empowerment in contexts where male dominance in financial affairs 
is normalized. 
 
Income showed a statistically significant yet practically modest positive association with 
microfinance access—a 100,000 TZS increase raised the likelihood of access by only 0.24 
percentage points. This outcome is theoretically consistent with the notion that while income is a 
necessary resource, it is not a sufficient condition for empowerment when structural barriers 
persist. The result echoes findings from Zimbabwe (Mishi and Kapingula, 2012), where income 
increases did not yield substantial gains in financial access due to collateral demands and 
discriminatory lending practices. This nuanced result affirms the empowerment theory’s view that 
economic empowerment depends not only on material wealth but also on the structural 
environment in which financial decisions are made. Without institutional reforms, increased 
income alone cannot ensure women’s equitable participation in financial systems. 
Interestingly, the finding that simplified loan procedures reduced access to microfinance by 15.7 
percentage points contradicts theoretical expectations. The empowerment framework suggests 
that simplified, transparent processes should enhance women's agency and promote inclusion. 
However, this contradiction aligns with critiques from earlier research in Zimbabwe (Mishi and 
Kapingula, 2012), where "simplified" procedures often concealed hidden costs, rigid eligibility 
criteria, or inconsistent application. This mismatch between policy intent and lived experience 
reveals a gap in the practical realization of empowerment. The result highlights that empowerment 
requires more than procedural reform; it demands effective implementation and institutional 
accountability to ensure that women genuinely benefit from intended reforms. 
Women’s willingness to engage with microfinance despite moderate or high perceived interest 
rates also aligns with theoretical expectations, particularly the empowerment framework’s 
emphasis on informed choice and agency. Contrary to traditional assumptions that higher costs 
deter participation, this study found that women who perceived rates as moderate or high were 
16.4 and 24.6 percentage points more likely, respectively, to access microfinance. This pattern 
reflects a strategic choice, where women prioritize trust and institutional reliability over 
affordability, a trend supported by similar findings in Bangladesh (Zoynul and Fahmida, 2013). It 
suggests that agency is exercised not only in pursuit of the lowest cost but also in favor of 
dependable service delivery, especially in environments where informal lenders dominate but lack 
regulation and security. 
Finally, the lack of a statistically significant relationship between education level and microfinance 
access diverges from the empowerment theory’s expectations, which posit education as a vital 
enabler of agency and decision-making. Unlike findings from Hai District and Bauchi, Nigeria, 
where education influenced access (Njau, 2023; Usman, 2023), this study found no meaningful 
effect in Kinondoni. This divergence may be attributed to the relatively homogenous educational 
attainment among respondents—over half had completed secondary education—or to the

overpowering influence of marital and institutional factors. In urban settings like Kinondoni, where 
institutional constraints and cultural norms remain entrenched, education alone may not suffice 
to overcome access barriers. The broader context of structural inequality, including land 
ownership laws and male-dominated resource control (Lusasi and Mwaseba, 2020; Hansen et 
al., 2021), remains a major impediment. Nonetheless, the observed association between 
microfinance access and higher income levels (as shown in Figure 3) affirms the potential of 
financial inclusion to improve women’s economic outcomes—if paired with deliberate structural 
and institutional change. 
Overall, the findings both align with and diverge from the women’s economic empowerment 
theory, offering nuanced insights into how structural, institutional, and agency-related factors 
influence financial access. Consistent with the theory, the study affirms that marital status, 
institutional trust, and income-based disparities are significant barriers to women’s access to 
microfinance, reinforcing the idea that unequal power relations and limited control over resources 
restrict women’s autonomy. The positive association between perceived interest rates and access 
further supports the theory’s emphasis on informed agency, where women make rational choices 
based on institutional reliability rather than cost alone. However, the non-significant effect of 
education and the unexpected negative influence of simplified procedures challenge the 
assumption that knowledge and formal process improvements automatically lead to 
empowerment. These divergences suggest that in contexts like Kinondoni, structural norms and 
implementation gaps can dilute or even reverse the intended outcomes of empowerment policies. 
Therefore, while the theory provides a valuable framework, its practical application must account 
for local sociocultural dynamics and institutional weaknesses that mediate how empowerment is 
experienced on the ground. 
6.0. 
 CONCLUSION 
This study reveals the multifaceted and deeply entrenched barriers that limit women’s access to 
microfinance services in Kinondoni Municipality, Tanzania. The analysis demonstrates how socio-
cultural norms, institutional limitations, and economic vulnerabilities converge to create persistent 
exclusion for women, particularly married women, who often face additional hurdles due to 
spousal consent requirements. The paradox of simplified loan procedures that are still 
inaccessible in practice, and the prioritization of institutional trust over affordability, further 
illustrates the disconnect between policy intent and women’s lived realities. These dynamics are 
embedded in broader patriarchal structures and regulatory gaps, which restrict women’s agency 
and undermine the transformative potential of microfinance as a tool for economic empowerment. 
To align with the goals of Sustainable Development Goal 5 and Tanzania’s National Microfinance 
Policy, there is a critical need for multidimensional strategies that combine legal reform, 
institutional transformation, and community engagement. Gender-sensitive lending practices, 
transparent and truly accessible procedures, and grassroots advocacy must form the backbone 
of a more inclusive financial system. Moreover, continuous monitoring, robust enforcement of 
gender equity laws, and expanded qualitative research into women’s financial experiences are 
essential to ensure that interventions are grounded in context and responsive to actual needs. If 
these systemic reforms are pursued, microfinance can evolve from a nominal tool into a genuine 
catalyst for women’s empowerment and inclusive economic growth in Kinondoni and similar 
contexts across Tanzania.

6.1 Recommendations 
The study offers the following recommendations which have been divided into three groups: 
Policy Recommendations 
To address the legal and regulatory barriers restricting women’s access to microfinance, policy 
reforms must prioritize the elimination of spousal consent requirements, which continue to 
undermine women’s financial autonomy. Married women in particular face significant challenges 
in accessing credit independently due to these constraints, reinforcing patriarchal control over 
household financial decisions. The government should revise legal frameworks to ensure that 
women are recognized as independent financial agents, in line with Tanzania’s commitments to 
gender equality and the objectives of SDG 5. In addition, legal protections must be strengthened 
to guarantee women’s equal rights to own land, register businesses, and use property as 
collateral—key criteria for loan eligibility. These protections should be actively enforced through 
public legal education and streamlined procedures that reduce bureaucratic obstacles. Lastly, it 
is essential to mainstream gender-sensitive policies within national financial inclusion strategies 
by setting measurable targets for female participation, investing in gender-disaggregated financial 
data, and ensuring that all economic development policies consider the unique barriers women 
face. 
Institutional Recommendations 
At the institutional level, microfinance providers must adopt lending models that expand access 
for women, particularly those without traditional forms of collateral. One effective approach is to 
promote group lending schemes that rely on social guarantees rather than physical assets, 
enabling women—especially in low-income and informal sectors—to access credit without 
needing land or property. Financial institutions should also enhance transparency in their 
operations by clearly communicating loan conditions such as interest rates, repayment terms, and 
documentation requirements in simplified formats and local languages. This is crucial to ensure 
that simplified procedures are not only accessible in name but in practice, particularly for women 
with limited education. In addition, financial institutions must invest in training their loan officers 
on gender sensitivity, equipping them with the skills to understand and respond to women’s 
specific needs and challenges. Gender-sensitive service delivery can help build trust, reduce 
discrimination, and encourage more women to engage confidently with microfinance systems. 
Community and Advocacy-Based Solutions 
To complement legal and institutional reforms, community-based initiatives must play a central 
role in advancing women’s financial inclusion. Expanding financial literacy through grassroots 
methods such as mobile learning, radio broadcasts, and peer-led workshops can help women 
make informed decisions about budgeting, managing loans, and identifying fair lending practices. 
Programs should be delivered in local languages and adapted to various literacy levels to ensure 
broad participation. Local women’s networks, religious leaders, and community organizations 
should be engaged as trusted partners to promote awareness about microfinance opportunities 
and address misinformation. These networks are instrumental in breaking social stigmas and 
building confidence among women hesitant to seek formal financial services. Furthermore, peer 
support groups or financial self-help groups can provide women with platforms to share

financial reforms at the local level. Together, these grassroots strategies can help shift social 
norms and enable a more equitable financial environment for women in Kinondoni and beyond.
