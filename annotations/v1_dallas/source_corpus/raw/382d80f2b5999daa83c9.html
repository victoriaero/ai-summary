Abstract

Purpose:

This study examines adolescents' self-reported school-based developmental assets and four intersecting social positions as they relate to prevalence of bullying involvement.

Methods:

Participants were 80,456 ninth and 11th grade students who participated in the 2019 Minnesota Student Survey (30.2% youth of color; 11% lesbian/gay/bisexual/pansexual/queer/questioning; 2.9% transgender/gender diverse [TGD] or gender questioning). Exhaustive Chi-square Automatic Interaction Detection analysis was used to identify school-based developmental assets (i.e., school safety, school adult support) and intersecting social positions (i.e., sexual identity; gender identity/modality; racial/ethnic identity; physical disabilities/chronic illness; and/or mental health/behavioral/emotional problems) associated with the highest prevalence of involvement as physical and relational bullies, victims, and bully-victims.

Results:

Adolescents with 2+ marginalized social positions who often lacked school-based developmental assets were part of nearly all the highest prevalence bullying involvement groups. TGD and gender questioning adolescents, Native American youth, and youth living with both physical disabilities/chronic illness and mental health/emotional/behavioral problems—most of whom had additional marginalized social positions and lacked school-based assets—were particularly overrepresented in high prevalence groups. For example, 31.1% of TGD or gender questioning youth of color living with both types of disabilities/health problems who did not feel strongly that school was safe reported involvement as physical bully-victims—nearly six times the sample average rate.

Conclusion:

Adolescents with multiple marginalized social positions and those lacking certain school-based assets—often overlapping categories—were involved in bullying at higher-than-average rates. Findings underscore the need for schools to address intersecting experiences of stigma and structural oppression that may perpetuate bullying involvement disparities.

Keywords: adolescence, bullying, intersectionality, minority stress, school safety, teacher caring

Introduction

Disparities in bullying involvement

Bullying has myriad adverse health and well-being correlates for adolescents involved as victims, bullies, or bully-victims (i.e., those who both bully and are bullied by others). Among the short- and long-term consequences of bullying involvement are mental health problems, physical health problems, substance use, and academic problems.1,2 Differences in prevalence of bullying involvement by social positions such as gender identity,3,4 sexual identity,4,5 racial/ethnic identity,6–8 and disability status9–12 are well documented, and highlight disproportionate rates of bullying involvement among socially marginalized youth—perhaps especially among multiply marginalized youth.8,13 These disparities in bullying involvement can be tied to proximal (e.g., internalized stigma) and distal (e.g., systemic oppression) minority stressors.8,13–19

Findings from studies of adolescents in North America underscore these bullying involvement disparities. Ample evidence suggests that lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer adolescents or those questioning their sexual identity (LGBQ) and transgender/gender diverse (TGD) adolescents are disproportionately victimized relative to their cisgender and heterosexual peers—often explicitly due to their LGBQ and/or TGD identities or identity expression.3,13,17,20–23 For example, about one-third of lesbian, gay, and bisexual high school students in Quebec reported school-based victimization, compared with 24.5% of heterosexual youth.20 Victimization disparities are even more stark among TGD youth compared with cisgender youth. For instance, rates of physical (25.1%), relational (52.2%), and cyberbullying (27.6%) victimization among TGD students were as much as double those among cisgender students in Minnesota.23

Youth with disabilities/health problems (e.g., psychological, physical, and psychosocial disabilities) are also disproportionately likely to experience general- and disability-based bullying relative to their peers without disabilities.7,10,12 For example, one study using data from >90,000 public schools in the United States found that students with disabilities were 32% more likely to be victims of bullying, and 282% more likely to be victims of disability-based bullying, than students without disabilities.7 Racial and/or ethnic differences in bullying involvement are less clear; some findings suggest that Black youth are less likely to be victimized than White or Hispanic youth,6 while others find that Black and Hispanic youth are more likely to be victimized than White youth.7

Notably, many youth who are victimized also victimize others with proactive or reactive actions,24 often in response to being bullied and/or to cope with social marginalization or rejection.19,25 Indeed, elevated rates of bullying among some marginalized youth (e.g., youth with disabilities/health problems, LGBQ youth, TGD youth), and bullying-victimization in particular, have likewise been documented.12,22,26,27

For instance, nearly twice as many LGBQ adolescents in a large statewide sample were bully-victims (23.5%–24.1%) compared with their heterosexual peers (13.5%).22 These disparities are troubling, given that bully-victims may fare even worse than victims or bullies with respect to mental health, engagement in risk behaviors, peer relationships, and other well-being indices.28–30

School-based developmental assets

External assets—contextual and relational features of young people's environments (e.g., supportive adults, a caring school climate)—have been linked to positive youth development,31 and may be protective against bullying involvement.32 For instance, findings from a systematic review revealed that positive school climate and school safety were two school-based factors consistently associated with lower likelihood of bullying involvement (as a victim, bully, or bully-victim).32 However, youth with certain marginalized social positions may have access to fewer developmental assets than their peers due to social stigma and systemic oppression.33

In the context of schools, this social stigma and systemic oppression may manifest as unsupportive school adults, exclusion from school activities, and disaffirming, potentially unsafe, environments. In fact, for some marginalized youth, the adults intended to protect them from school-based bullying may fail to intervene, or themselves act as bullies.34

The present study

A growing body of research uses an intersectional framework35 to examine nuances in bullying involvement across adolescents' mutually constitutive social positions.13,36,37 However, most quantitative intersectional studies focus only on victimization experiences and use methods (e.g., regression) that are limited in their ability to simultaneously consider multiple intersecting social positions.38

Further, few studies consider more than two (e.g., racial/ethnic identity and sexual identity36) or three (e.g., racial/ethnic identity, gender, sexual identity37) social positions, with disability/health problem status especially under-represented in extant intersectional research. Better understanding bullying involvement disparities at the intersection of multiple social positions, and the role of school-based developmental assets, can have important implications for tailored intervention and prevention programming. Using a quantitative approach recommended for intersectional research39,40 and a large, population-based sample, this study aims to identify characteristics of adolescents with the highest prevalence of bullying involvement.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Participants in the present analysis were 80,456 ninth and 11th grade students who participated in the 2019 Minnesota Student Survey (MSS). The MSS—an anonymous, online, school-based survey—is conducted every 3 years with students in grades 5, 8, 9, and 11.41 All Minnesota school districts are invited to participate in the MSS, and most districts (81%) had at least one school provide data in 2019. The present analysis focused on students in grades 9 and 11 (56% ninth graders) because items related to sexual identity were only included in surveys for students in these grades. Due to its use of existing anonymous data, this study was deemed exempt from review by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Social position variables and school-based developmental assets

Participants reported on four social positions included in this analysis: gender identity/modality,42,* sexual identity, racial/ethnic identity, and disability/health problem. Due to shared experiences of colonialism, and based on prior research highlighting elevated rates of health risk behaviors among youth with Native/indigenous identities,43,44 we grouped adolescents with any Native/indigenous identities (i.e., American Indian, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, or Other Pacific Islander), even if they reported one or more other racial and/or ethnic identities (i.e., Native+).

Participants also responded to items assessing the extent to which they perceived (1) adults at school care for students45 and (2) school to be safe. See Table 1 for complete information on social position and school-based developmental asset variables.

School-based peer bullying involvement

Participants responded to eight items related to their involvement in physical and relational bullying as victims and bullies at school in the last 30 days, on a 5-point scale (0 = Never-4 = Every day; see Supplementary Table S1 for detailed frequencies). Based on evidence that even infrequent involvement in bullying can have negative consequences for well-being,46 responses were dichotomized (“ever” vs. “never” involved with each form of bullying) and used to create six indicators of bullying involvement.

Based on their responses to the physical bullying items, students were categorized as physical victims (e.g., “How many times have other students at school pushed, shoved, slapped, hit or kicked you when they weren't kidding around?”), physical bullies (e.g., “How many times at school have YOU threatened to beat someone up?”), or physical bully-victims. Three similar indicators were also created for relational bullying involvement (e.g., “How many times at school have YOU spread mean rumors or lies about someone else?” “How often have other students at school excluded you from friends, other students or activities?”). Rates of missingness were low (≤4.5%) across these bullying involvement variables.

Statistical analysis

Exhaustive Chi-square Automatic Interaction Detection (CHAID) analyses with a Bonferroni correction and ten-fold cross-validation were conducted for each of the six indicators of bullying involvement. Exhaustive CHAID is a quantitative approach to intersectionality research used to identify which intersecting categorical independent variables have the strongest associations with dependent variables.40,47 This data-driven, nonparametric, decision-tree approach systematically cycles through predictors, splitting between categories where significant differences are detected in the prevalence of dependent variables until a “terminal node” is reached (i.e., no further statistically significant splits can be made given the established p value [p < 0.05] and minimum node size [40 in the present analysis to avoid overfitting]).

A strength of this approach is that missing data for categorical independent variables are retained and included in estimated models. Further, unlike statistical approaches like regression, exhaustive CHAID is not limited to a single reference group, and it does not reduce intersectional analyses to main effects and two- or three-way interaction effects. Rather, exhaustive CHAID tests every interaction between independent and dependent variables.40,47

Exhaustive CHAID was used to identify school-based developmental assets and intersecting social positions associated with the highest prevalence of involvement in bullying. The five nodes with the highest prevalence are reported here, for each of six models of bullying involvement. Index scores were calculated for each node to demonstrate the proportion of adolescents with those social positions and developmental assets involved in bullying compared with the overall sample same prevalence. Analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS v28.48

Results

Characteristics of the sample

Most participants were non-Hispanic/Latina/x/o (NH) White (69.8%), straight (78.1%), and reported no disabilities (63.3%). About half of participants were cisgender boys (46.6%) or cisgender girls (46.8%), and 2.9% were TGD or questioning their gender identity. Just over one-quarter of students had a strong sense that adults at school cared about students, and 37.1% had a strong sense that school was safe (see Supplementary Table S2 for prevalence of school-based developmental assets across social positions).

Finally, rates of involvement as a victim (physical: 12.1%; relational: 11.9%), bully (physical: 4.9%; relational: 3.4%), and bully-victim (physical: 5.4%; relational: 5.5%) were low. Similarly, adolescents who reported the highest prevalence of involvement as a victim of relational bullying were youth of color (especially Native+ and bi/multiracial youth) living with a mental health, emotional, or behavioral problem or both types of disability/health problem who did not have a strong sense that school was safe and generally did not have a strong sense that school adults cared about students. For instance, 44.0% of Native+ youth living with both types of disability/health problem, who identified as cisgender girls, TGD, or gender questioning (or were missing for gender), and who neither felt strongly that school was safe nor that school adults cared for students, reported involvement as a victim of relational bullying—nearly four times the sample average rate of 11.9%.

Bullies

Multiply marginalized youth were part of eight of the 10 high prevalence bullying nodes (Table 4). Across gender identities, youth of color (especially NH Black and Native+ youth), LGBQ youth, and youth living with one or more disabilities were common in the high-risk physical bullying nodes, often concurrently. For example, 14% of NH Black, cisgender girls who identified as lesbian, gay, or bisexual reported engaging in physical bullying—a rate nearly three times the sample average rate of 4.9%. For relational bullying, LGBQ youth across gender identities were common in the high-risk nodes. For instance, 5% of cisgender girls or TGD adolescents who identified as pansexual, queer, bisexual, or questioning their sexual identity engaged in relational bullying—a rate nearly one-and-a-half times the sample average rate of 3.4%.

Discussion

Ample evidence highlights disparities in bullying involvement and poor health sequelae among adolescents with marginalized social positions, yet research on bullying involvement from an intersectional perspective—especially research that considers three or more social positions and different forms of bullying involvement—is limited. This study examined the role of school-based developmental assets and four intersecting social positions as they relate to prevalence of involvement in bullying as a victim, bully, and bully-victim. Findings provide insight into intersecting experiences of stigma and structural oppression that may perpetuate disparities in bullying involvement and implications for school-based intervention.

Across types of bullying involvement, TGD and gender questioning adolescents, Native+ youth, and youth living with both physical and mental health, emotional, or behavioral disabilities—most of whom had additional marginalized social positions and lacked school-based assets—were over-represented in high prevalence nodes. Consistent with prior studies of victimization prevalence,13,37 multiply marginalized adolescents were part of 28 of the 30 highest prevalence bullying involvement groups in this study.

Indeed, when a social position that confers privilege (e.g., cisgender boy) did emerge in a high prevalence bullying involvement group, it was in conjunction with at least one (and usually more than one) marginalized social position. Myriad forms of interpersonal stigma and structural inequities (e.g., identity-based stereotypes about aggression;49 elevated rates of bullying involvement among youth with disabilities in segregated, vs. more inclusive, school settings;50 disparities in school-based developmental assets; and social safety by social positions such as race or sexual identity)51,52 may converge in complex ways that exacerbate bullying involvement risk for multiply marginalized youth and can, in turn, leave youth at greater risk of compromised health and well-being.1,2

Although troublingly few participants endorsed a strong sense of school adult caring and school safety, rates were especially low among youth with marginalized social positions; nearly two-thirds of youth in high prevalence nodes lacked at least one school-based asset. In their proposed expansion of the minority stress model, Diamond and Alley suggest that what is absent from marginalized individuals' lives (e.g., reliable social protection) is as salient and consequential as what is present (minority stressors).52 In the absence of social safety, marginalized individuals must be chronically vigilant of threats.

Hypervigilance can tax coping resources and health, and result in behavioral adaptations, such as isolation and defensive aggression, that may account, in part, for bullying involvement disparities.52 Indeed, in this study, multiply marginalized adolescents who did not strongly believe that school was safe were part of most of the high prevalence physical bully-victim nodes. At the same time, it is possible that students involved in bullying, especially as a victim or bully-victim, feel less safe at school because of these experiences.

These findings suggest that individual-level bullying interventions (e.g., social skills building programs53) alone may be inadequate to combat bullying involvement disparities. Rather, structural changes are also needed to combat cisgenderism, heterosexism, racism, ableism, and intersecting experiences of marginalization in schools, and to promote school safety and connectedness for all youth—especially youth with minoritized social positions who may have fewer such school-based developmental assets.33,34,51,54

For example, schools should enumerate clear policies and procedures related to bullying and provide teachers and school staff members with training on how to sensitively intervene in instances of bullying—including more covert bullying scenarios (e.g., rumor spreading and other forms of relational bullying)—without ostracizing students or contributing to feelings that school is unsafe.55 Structural changes such as these are needed to promote youth experiences of social safety and connection in the day to day, and mitigate bullying involvement and/or downstream correlates of bullying involvement, such as emotional distress and other indices of poor health and well-being.1,2

Limitations and future directions

Using a very large, population-based sample of adolescents and an intersectional quantitative approach, this study provides insight into bullying involvement disparities at the intersection of four social positions, while considering the role of school-based developmental assets. Despite this study's strengths, findings should be interpreted bearing in mind several limitations. First, these data are cross-sectional, which precludes inferences about causality or directionality of effects. For instance, students in many of the high prevalence bullying involvement nodes reported lacking school-based developmental assets related to safety and connection to school adults.

It is unclear whether (1) a dearth of school-based developmental assets contributes to bullying involvement, (2) bullying involvement leads to fewer school-based developmental assets (e.g., feeling unsafe at school), (3) these associations are bidirectional, or (4) school-based developmental assets are indirectly related to bullying involvement through mechanisms not measured in this study, among other possibilities. Future longitudinal work is needed to elucidate these associations over time.

Second, self-reported data are subject to response bias. For example, adolescents may under-report behaviors, such as bullying, due to social desirability bias. Third, students who were absent from school when data were collected may differ from those who participated in data collection. Indeed, some social positions, experiences, and school-based developmental assets of interest to this study are associated with school absenteeism (e.g., youth who feel unsafe at school and those who are bullied are more likely to be absent54,56,57). Thus, our findings may understate bullying involvement disparities.

Fourth, while retention of missing data in a “missing” category for independent variables is an advantage of exhaustive CHAID, it can be difficult to interpret “missing” categories. Finally, several measures were limited in their ability to assess constructs of interest. Specifically, the survey items assessing long-term mental health, behavioral, and emotional problems, and physical disabilities or long-term health problems were overly broad; bullying involvement items did not assess why participants felt they were bullied (e.g., gender expression) or bullied others (e.g., social power), and perceived teacher caring and school safety are only two of many school-based developmental assets youth may have.

Future intersectional research with more comprehensive measures of disability is needed to elucidate nuanced differences in vulnerability to bullying involvement (e.g., based on visibility of the disability) across specific disabilities.58,59 Likewise, assessment of perceived motives related to bullying involvement, as well as a wider range of school-based developmental assets, would be informative for prevention and intervention programming.

Conclusion

TGD and gender questioning adolescents, Native+ youth, youth living with both a physical disability and mental health, emotional, or behavioral problem, and multiply marginalized adolescents who lacked school-based developmental assets were consistently among those adolescents with the highest prevalence of bullying involvement. These findings underscore the importance of understanding and addressing school-based stigma and social safety, especially among adolescents with intersecting marginalized social positions, to reduce bullying involvement disparities.

Future intersectional research, especially with adolescents whose social positions were over-represented in the high prevalence bullying involvement nodes, is needed to identify and dismantle structural factors that may underlie these disparities. School adults and school-based bullying prevention programming should attend to adolescents' intersecting social positions and cultivate a school climate that is inclusive of, and safe for, students with diverse, marginalized social positions.
