What Influences Criminal Behavior?

Online Learning

Share Article:

  • facebook
  • twitter
  • linkedin
  • email
Research helps answer the pressing question: Why do people commit crimes?

Key Takeaways

  • Criminologists point to four overlapping factors that contribute to criminal behavior: biological risk factors, adverse childhood experiences, negative social environment (including poverty and high-crime neighborhoods), and substance abuse.
  • The link between substance abuse and crime is especially strong. Much of the U.S. prison population has a history of drug or alcohol abuse, and most people arrested for common crimes test positive for illegal drugs at the time of arrest.
  • Earning a BS in Criminal Justice can help students understand these root causes of crime and pursue careers focused on prevention, corrections, or criminal justice reform.

In 2025, the FBI recorded an estimated 1.1 million violent crimes and 5.2 million property crimes nationwide, the lowest rate recorded in more than five decades.1 That’s genuine progress. But crime, in all its forms, remains part of our society. So, what drives people who engage in criminal activity?

In modern times, researchers studying crime and criminality have taken a scientific approach to finding answers. While people who commit crimes have their own motivations and circumstances, researchers point to four overlapping factors that can help explain what causes crime.
 

1. Biological Risk Factors

Just as we don’t choose our eye color, we don’t choose the chemical wiring of our bodies and brains. Several researchers have looked at low resting heart rates as a possible biological marker for antisocial behavior. A study in Brazil following more than 3,600 people from birth into their late teens found that boys with the lowest resting heart rates in adolescence had close to double the odds of committing a violent crime by age 18, compared with boys with the highest resting heart rates.2

This pattern held up even after accounting for family income and other social factors, and it matches earlier findings from the U.K. and Sweden.2 The researchers behind the Brazilian study note that biology appears to work together with a person’s environment and does not determine behavior or criminal activity on its own.2
 

2. Adverse Childhood Experiences

The environment we’re raised in isn’t something we choose either. Some people have stable, supportive childhoods; others don’t. Researchers recognize multiple types of adverse childhood experiences, or ACEs, ranging from abuse and neglect to growing up with a parent who has a substance-use disorder or spends time incarcerated. 

A study of more than 64,000 juvenile offenders in Florida found that 97% had experienced at least one ACE, and half had experienced four or more. The three most common ACEs were family violence, parental separation or divorce, and having an incarcerated household member.3
 

3. Negative Social Environment

The people and places around us can help shape who we become. An experiment in Denmark offers evidence of this. A government housing policy placed newly arrived families into neighborhoods based on availability rather than choice, which meant families ended up in higher- or lower-crime areas essentially at random. Tracking children in those families over time, researchers found that growing up in a higher-crime neighborhood raised the odds of a later criminal conviction. This suggests that neighborhood crime spreads partly through social interaction, not just circumstances.4
 

4. Substance Abuse

Few relationships in criminology are as well documented as the one between substance abuse and crime. According to the National Institute on Drug Abuse, an estimated “85% of the U.S. prison population has an active substance-use disorder or were incarcerated for a crime that involved drugs or drug use.”5 Drug use at the time of arrest is common, too: a 2025 study of first-time arrestees across 25 U.S. county jails found that nearly 75% of those who were drug-tested had a positive result.6

Some substances, such as alcohol, lower inhibitions; others, such as cocaine, overstimulate the nervous system. In either instance, researchers agree that the physiological and psychological effects can impair judgment and self-control. And an addiction that requires money to sustain may push someone toward criminal activity.

What causes crime and why do people commit crimes? There’s no single or simple answer. But a criminal justice degree can help you investigate the causes and build knowledge and skills you can apply to your career goals. For some, that’s helping to create safer communities. For others, it’s supporting people who suffer when a crime is committed. What kind of changemaker do you want to be?
 

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main factors that influence criminal behavior?
Criminologists point to four overlapping factors: biological risk factors, adverse childhood experiences, negative social environment (including poverty and high-crime neighborhoods), and substance abuse.

Can biology really predispose someone to criminal behavior?
A large birth-cohort study in Brazil found that boys with the lowest resting heart rates in adolescence had close to double the odds of committing a violent crime by age 18 compared with boys with the highest resting heart rates, though researchers stress that this biological factor works together with environmental and social factors rather than determining behavior on its own. 2

How much do childhood experiences affect criminal behavior?
A study of more than 64,000 juvenile offenders in Florida found that 97% had experienced at least one ACE and half had experienced four or more.3

What is the connection between substance abuse and crime?
According to the National Institute on Drug Abuse, an estimated 85% of the U.S. prison population has an active substance-use disorder or was incarcerated for a crime involving drugs.5 A 2025 study of 25 U.S. county jails also found that nearly 75% of first-time arrestees who were drug-tested had a positive result.6

What can I do with a BS in Criminal Justice?
A BS in Criminal Justice can help prepare graduates to develop knowledge and skills they can apply to understanding and then addressing the root causes of criminal activity.
