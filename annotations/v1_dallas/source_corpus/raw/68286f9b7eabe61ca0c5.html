Patient Safety and Satisfaction in Healthcare

Errors in healthcare can carry serious consequences. When errors occur, patients are dissatisfied and lose their trust in the system. In the landmark study, To Err Is Human, preventable errors within healthcare systems were noted to be one of the top causes of death in the United States.

Patient safety is defined by the World Health Organization as the delivery of safe, timely, equitable, efficient, and effective health services. Patient satisfaction is the act of fully meeting the patient's expectations during a healthcare encounter. Patient safety and satisfaction are integrated goals. When placing patient safety components as top priority, such as improving communication as well as other patient safety goals, patients feel they are at the center of their care. Providing patient-centered care increases patient satisfaction while reducing errors and improving patient safety.

Patient Safety in Healthcare: Factors

Healthcare is a high-risk environment dealing with the complexities of the human body. Without an integrated system of communication, transparency, and proper identification of patients, medical staff is at high risk of errors which then negatively impact patient satisfaction.

Factors that affect patient safety in healthcare include:

  • Lack of communication
  • Unsafe staffing ratios
  • Lack of organizational culture
  • Certain healthcare environments
  • Lack of ongoing safety education

How Do Finances Affect Patient Safety?

The financial picture of a healthcare facility in fact does directly affect patient safety. Research has found that hospitals that are financially secure have more resources for quality assurance and quality improvement initiatives. This in turn improves patient safety by reducing errors and improving patient satisfaction. Healthcare systems that are financially secure also have a larger budget for staffing and additional resources for staff retention initiatives. Healthcare systems that are not financially secure are forced to cut costs, which increases the risk of an adverse patient event or death.

How Do Nurse-Patient Ratio Regulations Affect Patient Safety?

Due to lower insurance reimbursement rates, increasing cost of supplies and staff, and a greater focus on profit, many healthcare systems are forced to reorganize budgets which include reducing staff. Ensuring adequate staffing to care for patients is imperative to patient safety, patient satisfaction, and medical staff satisfaction in healthcare. Staffing ratios are based on a number of factors, including:

  • Staff experience
  • Patient acuity
  • Healthcare environment (hospital, nursing home, clinic, etc.)
  • Staff support

Safe staffing ratios are so important to patient safety and satisfaction that sixteen states now have laws and regulations mandating safe nurse-patient ratios. The first state to implement legislation on nurse-patient ratios was California. The Minimum Nurse-Patient Ratio Law, known as AB 394, was passed in 1999 and implemented in 2004. This law ensures that in acute care, acute psychiatric, and specialty care areas, the registered nurse will not be assigned more than a maximum number of patients.

Patient Safety Goals

Patient safety goals are established to improve overall patient safety and to reduce healthcare-associated adverse events and deaths. The current Joint Commission patient safety goals are:

1. Improve the accuracy of patient identification

  • Always use two patient identifiers, such as name and date of birth

2. Improve the effectiveness of communication among caregivers

  • Timely reporting of critical results of any diagnostic test or procedure must be called to the appropriate personnel

3. Improve the safety of using medications

  • All medications prepared for administration must be labeled
  • Anticoagulant therapy is a high-risk drug requiring more attention to reduce risk
  • Ensure patient data systems are updated to reflect accurate patient medication information

4. Reduce patient harm associated with clinical alarm systems

  • Improving the alarm system of any medical device to perform properly and avoid staff alarm fatigue

5. Reduce the risk of healthcare-associated infections

  • Implementing the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) or World Health Organization (WHO) hand hygiene guidelines with all staff in all areas of healthcare

6. Reduce the risk of patient harm resulting from falls

  • Utilizing patient fall assessment tools and interventions to reduce falls

7. Prevent healthcare-associated pressure ulcers (decubitus ulcers)

  • Utilizing pressure ulcer risk and assessment tools with all patients and interventions with those who are at risk

8. The organization identifies safety risks inherent in its patient population

  • Assess for suicide risk by asking about self-harm
  • Assess for factors that would increase the risk of harm from the utilization of home oxygen therapy

9. Improve healthcare equity

  • Evaluate for and implement strategies that will ensure all patients are receiving equitable healthcare

10. Universal protocol for preventing wrong site, wrong procedure, wrong person surgery

  • Confirm the procedure with the medical staff and patient in a pre-procedure verification process
  • Mark the procedure site and confirm with the patient to avoid wrong site surgery
  • Perform a final time-out before the procedure in the presence of the medical staff and the patient

The Role of Communication in Medical Safety

Poor communication between medical staff is a recipe for disaster. If communication is not open and clear, mistakes will happen. Medical malpractice lawsuits often occur due to this communication failure or lack of rapport between the provider and the patient. It is important that healthcare systems cultivate a culture of open communication. This will ensure ample opportunity to hear and address patient concerns.

This culture of open communication will also allow debriefing for medical staff to learn from mistakes and work to improve processes to reduce the risk of error in the future. Communication between medical staff and patients is easily facilitated by bedside report. This process is an end-of-shift report occurring at the bedside between the oncoming nurse and the off-going nurse. Bedside report assists in keeping the patient updated on the plan of care and allows the patient opportunity to ask questions and express concerns or needs. SBAR is a communication method that is used between medical staff, including during bedside report. AWARE is a communication method used between medical staff and patients to increase communication and trust.

SBAR Communication Method

Communication between medical staff can be tricky. Following the SBAR method of communication will ensure that all the pertinent details of a patient problem are communicated effectively to all members of the patient care team. Here is an example of the nurse communication with the physician about a change in the patient's condition.

Situation

Define the problem: "Dr. Jones, this is Cathy, R.N. calling from the I.C.U. Mr. White is complaining of stabbing, substernal chest pain that started ten minutes ago. He is pale and diaphoretic."

Background

State the background of the situation: "Mr. White was admitted 2 days ago for atrial fibrillation and has a past medical history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. His blood pressure is 98/52 mmHg, heart rate is 122 bpm, and respiratory rate is 28."

Assessment

What can be concluded from the situation and background? "Dr. Jones, I am concerned Mr. White is having an acute coronary syndrome."

Recommendation

Based on the relevant data, what professional recommendation can be made? "Dr. Jones, would you like me to obtain an EKG and cardiac labs including a troponin? Are there any other interventions you would like to order?"

AWARE Communication Method

Keeping open and clear communication between medical staff and patients is imperative to patient safety and satisfaction. Using the AWARE approach as a communication strategy can keep a clear pathway of information and expectations.

  • A - Announce - When you enter a patient room, always state your name and title.
  • W - Welcome - At every patient encounter, wear a smile and greet the patient warmly.
  • A - Ask- At every interaction with the patient, ask if there are any needs such as toileting or fresh water. Also, always make sure the call light and all belongings are within reach.
  • R - Review - At the end of the encounter always review the important points and when the patient can expect you to come back.
  • E - Exit - Smile when leaving a patient's room as this will convey care and concern for the patient.

Lesson Summary

Healthcare is a complex system that can have devastating consequences if mistakes occur. There are many factors that contribute to patient safety and satisfaction. Inadequate staffing has a significant impact on patient safety, which is why 16 states now have laws or rules mandating the maximum number of patients nurses can care for. California's Minimum Nurse-Patient Ratio Law specifically mandates how many patients a nurse can be assigned in acute care, psychiatric, and specialty care areas.

Poor communication between medical staff and between patients and staff is a common factor in medical malpractice lawsuits. A culture of open communication is essential for addressing patient concerns and allowing staff to learn and reduce future errors. Communication strategies such as SBAR and AWARE improve patient safety. SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation) improves communication among medical staff, and AWARE improves communication between staff and patients. SBAR is often used in bedside report in order to improve staff and patient communication, keep the patient updated on the plan of care, and address any patient questions. AWARE keeps patients up to date on changes in their care as well as aims to improve patient satisfaction.

I Wouldn't Send My Dog To That Hospital!

MABEL: ''Glad you could come to lunch, Delores, it's good to see you. How have you been doing since the knee surgery?''

DELORES: ''Oh Mabel, it's lucky I made it out of that place alive. I wouldn't send my dog to that horrible hospital!''

MABEL: ''Oh dear! Why is that?''

DELORES: ''After my surgery, I was in pain all the time and it took them all day to bring my pain medication! Nobody ever told me when the doctor would come in or when I could go home! There never seemed to be enough nurses!''

This conversation between two women about a healthcare experience reflects some of the things that commonly go wrong in healthcare settings. Sometimes, consideration of just a few simple factors that affect a patient's perception of his care can make all the difference between a bad experience and a good one. Let's take a look at these.

Simple factors can prevent a bad patient experience

An Industry With High Stakes

The healthcare industry is an evolving and dynamic industry. It's an endeavor that does not involve the sale of widgets, but instead, it deals with the healing of illness and the alleviating of human suffering. It involves cutting-edge science as well as empathy, insight and compassion.

Good Staffing Is Basic To Safe Healthcare Delivery

Adequate staffing in healthcare facilities contributes to both a better nursing work environment and to patient safety and satisfaction. The job simply cannot get done right if there are not enough people to do it.

Implementation of Minimum Nurse-Patient Ratios

During recent decades, the corporate, profit-driven management of healthcare has resulted in many restructurings, with cuts in both budgets and staffing, and the quality of healthcare suffered. As a result, California became the first state in the U.S. to make minimum nurse-patient ratios a law in 1999. This means that one nurse can have only a certain maximum number of patients to care for, depending upon the area worked. The law, AB 394, was not fully implemented until January of 2004. Since that time, the positive results of this legislation have been documented by many studies. One of these studies showed that ''every one additional patient added to a hospital staff nurse's workload is associated with a seven percent increase in hospital mortality''.

Another study done in 2010 at the University of Pennsylvania also reported that the new California nurse-patient ratios ''accompanied a lower likelihood of inpatient death within 30 days of hospital admission than in New Jersey or Pennsylvania.''

Unfortunately, even though hospitals usually have a target number of nurses to staff a given number of patients on a unit, this can vary from shift to shift, and hospitals that want to cut corners don't always plan well for it. But those variations can also mean trouble. A study published in the New England Journal of Medicine found that a ''patient...exposed to three nursing shifts with below target staffing'' resulted in a ''six percent higher risk of mortality''.

Culture of Communication

In the healthcare setting, there is no place for secrets or dishonesty. If the staff members are afraid to report a concern about a patient or a mistake that was made in care because of hostile management, then these problems cannot be learned from and corrected. It is crucial that a healthcare facility have a culture of open communication.

Communication With Patients

Communication failures in healthcare are much more than just an inconvenience. They can be matters of life and death. A communication failure or a general lack of rapport between the provider and patient are nearly always central to any medical malpractice lawsuit. According to the research organization CRICO Strategies, such failures were a major cause in 30 percent of the malpractice cases they examined, involving 1,744 deaths. So what can be done to improve communication between staff members themselves, and between staff and patients?

SBAR Communication And Bedside Report

The change-of-shift nursing report can be given at the bedside with the patient's participation to ensure that he is kept updated on the plan of care. Any questions that the patient has about his care can also be addressed at that time.

SBAR communication is a method that can be used between staff members. It's a way of making sure that all bases are covered when addressing a patient problem. The acronym SBAR stands for:

  • Situation - Define the situation. For example, ''Mr. Smith was taking antibiotics for a bladder infection before he broke his leg and came to the ER. Should these be continued?''
  • Background - Give background details about the situation. In the above example, what antibiotic was Mr. Smith taking, and what dose? How long was he taking it?
  • Assessment - Ask what can be concluded from the situation and background information.
  • Recommendation - Ask how the situation is to be handled. For example, ''Which doctor will prescribe the rest of the antibiotics for Mr. Smith and when should they be started?''

Be AWARE

The acronym, AWARE, is sometimes used in healthcare to provide a guide for providers like nurses and doctors to communicate clearly and effectively with patients. The individual letters in the word ''aware'' spell out the guide:

  • A - Announce your presence - Always knock on the patient's door before entering his room
  • W - Welcome the patient - Always say a few words of welcome to a new patient on the unit. When the patient is being brought from the emergency room to his room on the floor, say, 'Welcome to 4-West!'
  • A - Ask if the patient needs anything - This can also be done during hourly rounding, in which the basic patient needs, such as pain, bathroom, positioning, or location of possessions, are addressed.
  • R - Review what was done and explain what will be done next - keep the patient updated on what is being done to treat him and on what he can expect
  • E - Exit with a kind word - Let the patient know that even though you are leaving the room, you'll be back, and that you care.

Lesson Summary

Healthcare is an evolving, dynamic industry. To deliver safe care, a facility needs adequate staffing, which is basic to patient safety. Many studies done after the passing of the California minimum nurse-patient ratio law have shown that this was a positive change resulting in safer patient care.

A culture of open and honest communication is also central to safe and effective patient care. Malpractice cases often occur simply because of a lack of communication. Measures such as SBAR communication and using the AWARE acronym as a guide can aid in the delivery of safe care and in increased patient satisfaction.

FAQ

What are the key elements of patient safety?

Key elements of patient safety are clear and concise communication, safe staffing ratios, and a culture that supports patient safety. Without all three elements, healthcare errors increase, and patient satisfaction decreases.

What are examples of patient safety?

Examples of factors that affect patient safety include communication, staffing ratios, organizational culture, the healthcare environment, and ongoing education and process improvement on patient safety. Improving these patient safety factors will not only reduce errors but will allow staff to learn from mistakes and reduce future errors. Improving communication between staff and patients will enhance patient-centered care and patient satisfaction.

What is the definition of patient safety in healthcare?

The WHO defines patient safety as the delivery of timely, equitable, efficient, and effective health services. The key is to provide the healthcare services in a safe and effective manner.

How important is safety in healthcare?

Patient safety is of great importance in healthcare. Now 16 states include laws or rules to ensure safe staffing ratios, which has increased patient safety.
