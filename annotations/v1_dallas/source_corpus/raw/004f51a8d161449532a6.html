Abstract

Background: Transgender individuals are known to be at a higher risk for certain chronic conditions compared to cisgender individuals. Similarly, research also shows justice-involved individuals have a higher risk of chronic conditions compared to non-justice-involved individuals.

Aim: This study aims to investigate the differences in chronic disease diagnoses between transgender and cisgender individuals who either utilized healthcare services provided by a US county health system or who were incarcerated in the same county’s jail.

Method: We leveraged electronic health record data from a United States county health system, including data from the local jail, to evaluate the difference in diagnosis of certain chronic conditions among transgender and cisgender individuals. We also compared individuals who experienced incarceration and those who did not.

Results: We found transgender individuals experiencing recent incarceration were more likely to be diagnosed with substance use disorder, HIV/AIDS, and Hepatitis C than cisgender individuals. Transgender individuals not experiencing recent incarceration were more likely to be diagnosed with HIV/AIDS, seizure disorder, major depressive disorder, and bipolar disorder than cisgender individuals.

Conclusion: These results highlight how transgender individuals may be disproportionately impacted by certain diseases within and outside carceral settings.

Keywords: HIV, incarceration, substance use disorder, transgender, United States

Introduction

In the United States (US), transgender individuals have a higher risk of having a chronic disease and psychiatric illness compared to cisgender individuals (Downing & Przedworski, 2018). Individuals with a history of incarceration are similarly at a higher risk for certain chronic diseases than individuals without a history of incarceration (Udo, 2019). Higher rates of substance use disorder, HIV infection, depression, cardiovascular disease, and having multiple medical conditions are seen within transgender populations (Grant et al., 2011; Reisner et al., 2014). One study found that among 254,462,596 inpatient encounters in the US (US), transgender individuals had a significantly higher prevalence of mental health disorders, including substance abuse, (77.0% of encounters) than hospitalizations by cisgender individuals (37.8% of encounters) (Hanna et al., 2019). In an analysis of privately insured patients, transgender individuals were much more likely to be diagnosed with depression, psychoses, drug use disorder, and several other chronic medical conditions compared to insured cisgender individuals (Hughes et al., 2021).

Incarceration has also been linked to negative health outcomes and higher prevalence of certain medical conditions including hypertension, liver diseases, HIV/AIDS, and other sexually transmitted diseases (Udo, 2019). Binswanger et al. (2007) found that individuals being released from a custodial facility had a 3.5 times increased risk of death compared with the general population within two years following release and a 12.7 times increased risk of death within the first two weeks of release. The same study found that drug overdose was the leading cause of death within two years (23.2% of deaths), followed by cardiovascular disease (12.6% of deaths), and homicide (12.2% of deaths). Of individuals in the study population who died within the first two weeks of release, drug overdose constituted 71.1% of deaths (Binswanger et al., 2007). In the US, 20–26% of persons living with HIV and 29–43% of individuals with hepatitis C virus (HCV) have experienced incarceration (Hammett et al., 2002) compared to the US general population which has a lifetime risk of imprisonment of 5.7% (Roehrkasse & Wildeman, 2022). Additionally, individuals with a history of incarceration have significantly higher prevalence of certain psychiatric conditions compared with those who have not been incarcerated (Schnittker et al., 2012) and higher rates of substance use disorders (Fazel et al., 2017). Though formerly incarcerated individuals experience disproportionate rates of certain conditions, they also report barriers to accessing healthcare that perpetuates their inability to manage their health and mental care (Shivani Nishar et al., 2023).

Transgender individuals are at a higher risk of incarceration when compared to their cisgender peers. Studies report 16–19% of transgender individuals ever experience incarceration (Grant et al., 2011; James et al., 2016; Reisner et al., 2014). This is starkly contrasted with the US population as a whole, where estimated lifetime risk of imprisonment is 5.7% (Roehrkasse & Wildeman, 2022). Rates of incarceration are higher in transgender women and in transgender persons of color (Grant et al., 2011; James et al., 2016; Reisner et al., 2014).

Despite the disproportionate negative health outcomes among individuals involved with the criminal justice system, and the increased likelihood of experiencing incarceration among transgender individuals, there is little information about how transgender identity and criminal justice system involvement may affect an individual’s health. In a survey of incarcerated LGBTQ individuals, seven percent reported being HIV positive while 67% reported being diagnosed with a mental illness (Lydon et al., 2015). The disproportionate incarceration of transgender individuals has serious health implications, given that both transgender identity and justice-involvement are individually associated with increased risk of chronic disease. This study aims to investigate the differences in chronic disease diagnoses between transgender and cisgender individuals who either utilized healthcare services provided by a US county health system or who were incarcerated in the same county’s jail.

Methods

Setting

Santa Clara County (SCC) is a large, urban county in northern California with a 2020 population of 1.9 million (United States Census Bureau, 2023). The SCC jail operates two facilities for both pretrial and sentenced individuals that are at least 18 years of age. There are approximately 24,000 individuals booked into custody at the SCC jail annually. Medical services are provided by Adult Custody Health Services (ACHS), a division of the county health and hospital system, Santa Clara Valley Medical Center (SCVMC). Clinics are operated within SCC jail by ACHS providers. An encounter is created within the SCVMC electronic health record (EHR) for each individual booked into the SCC jail. SCVMC also operates community-based outpatient primary care clinics and urgent care clinics, including primary care clinics with specialized care for transgender and gender nonbinary individuals throughout SCC. SCVMC is the primary health system where those who have been incarcerated in SCC access care when in the community, often due to its mission to provide healthcare services, regardless of insurance or ability to pay. All data collected from the SCVMC operated hospitals and clinics are stored in the same EHR as the SCC jail. Additionally, SCVMC uses Epic Systems’ EHR which allows for data exchange through its Care Everywhere platform with other healthcare systems using the Epic EHR if a certain number of unique identifiers match patient records in another healthcare systems’ platform.

Design

This is a retrospective cohort study investigating the differences in disease prevalence between cisgender and transgender individuals who either utilized healthcare services provided by SCVMC or were incarcerated in the SCC jail between 7/1/2018 and 6/30/2021. For inclusion in the study, individuals had at least one completed medical visit in an SCVMC operated outpatient primary care or urgent care clinic or at least one unique admission to the SCC jail recorded in the electronic health record (EHR). Admissions into the SCC jail are recorded within the SCVMC EHR. Individuals under the age of 18 were excluded (the minimum age for admission to jail is 18).

Data source

All data for individuals included in the study is recorded in the Epic EHR (Epic Systems, Verona WI). The EHR for the jail, primary care clinics, and urgent care locations is operated on a single platform by SCVMC. Outpatient medical visits were defined as a completed visit with a medical provider at one of the SCVMC primary care clinics (those with a department specialty of family medicine or internal medicine) or urgent care clinics. Recently experiencing incarceration is defined as any admission record into the SCC Jail within the EHR during the study period (between 7/1/2018 and 6/30/2021).

Variables

Outcome variable(s)

We assessed the prevalence of certain medical conditions between cisgender and transgender individuals who either utilized outpatient medical services offered by SCVMC or were incarcerated in the SCC jail, based on conditions that disproportionately affect transgender individuals or individuals experiencing incarceration. Medical conditions assessed in this study include diabetes, HIV/AIDS, heart disease, seizure, HCV, major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and substance use disorder (SUD). An individual is considered to have any of the aforementioned conditions if the diagnosis is listed on the individual’s “problem list” within the EHR. These are entered by clinical providers and correspond to ICD-10 codes. All jail, hospital, and outpatient clinic facilities utilize the problem list to add or resolve an individual’s diagnoses.

Independent variable of interest

In this study we aimed to determine if transgender individuals are more likely to be diagnosed with certain chronic conditions than their cisgender peers, and if this likelihood differed if an individual’s experience of incarceration during the study period. We used a combination of data points from the EHR to determine gender identity (Chyten-Brennan et al., 2021). SCVMC’s EHR has had fields specific to the individual’s gender identity available since November 2018, in which the individual’s sex assigned at birth and gender identity can be documented. There is a separate field for sex assigned at birth, which can include responses of male, female, or choose not to disclose. Responses for a gender identity field include male, female, transgender female, transgender male, non-binary, or choose not to disclose. Individuals were classified as transgender if meeting at least one of the following three criteria:

  1. They had a documented gender identity of “transgender male to female”, “transgender female to male”, or “nonbinary”.

  2. They had a gender identity that was inconsistent with their sex assigned at birth.

  3. They had a diagnosis on the problem list indicative of being transgender. This included any diagnosis under parent SNOMED concept of “transsexual” (407374003), “gender identity disorder” (87991007), or “transgender identity” (122712410). Also included were specific diagnoses of ICD-10 codes Z78.9, Z79.899, or F64.0.

All other individuals without a qualifying diagnosis code or recorded gender identity were classified as cisgender.

Covariates

Individuals were classified as “not housed” if they had a permanent address of “homeless” or “transient”, had a diagnosis code of homelessness, or had “homeless” marked on the SCVMC health system housing screen (an enterprise health system screening tool). Homelessness is associated with disproportionate disease burden for several diagnoses (Bowen et al., 2019). Age is the age of admission for the first incarceration or the age at the first appointment during the study period. Certain conditions assessed are more prevalent in different age groups. We included race and ethnicity data because of the disproportionate involvement in the criminal justice system by racial and ethnic minorities (The Sentencing Project, 2018). Race and ethnicity are captured as two distinct fields in the EHR, and each allow multiple values to be selected. An entry of “Hispanic or Latino”, “Central American”, “Cuban”, “Dominican”, “Latin American”, “Mexican”, “Puerto Rican”, “South American”, or “Other Hispanic or Latino” in the ethnicity field qualified the individual as having a “Hispanic” ethnicity. Any individual with “Hispanic” entered into race field or a combination of “unknown” entered into the race field and a “Hispanic” ethnicity was grouped into the “Hispanic” category. Any individual with a race other than “Hispanic or Latino” entered in the race field and a race of “Hispanic or Latino” or a “Hispanic” ethnicity was grouped into the “Hispanic Multiracial” category. Any individual with a race other than “Hispanic or Latino” and no recorded “Hispanic” ethnicity was categorized as “Non-Hispanic” and the corresponding race. Any individual with two or more races and no “Hispanic” ethnicity was grouped into the “Non-Hispanic Multiracial” category.

Group stratification

We divided the study population into two subgroups; those experiencing recent incarceration and those not experiencing recent incarceration. An individual who had at least one admission to the SCC jail during the study period was classified as experiencing recent incarceration. Individuals who had both a jail admission and an outpatient medical visit were assigned to the group experiencing recent incarceration. Individuals included in the study as a result of completing an outpatient medical visit and who did not have a jail admission during the study period are considered as not experiencing recent incarceration.

Statistical analysis

We performed chi-square analyses to test for significant differences between the cisgender and transgender populations, within the population experiencing recent incarceration and the population that did not. In cases of small samples Fisher’s exact test was used. We used multivariable logistic regression models to evaluate the likelihood of being diagnosed with any of the diseases of interest while controlling for covariates. All models included the covariates listed above. For the outcomes of HIV/AIDS and HCV diagnosis we added diagnosis of SUD because it is a known risk factor for HIV/AIDS and HCV (Rosenberg et al., 2005; Tsui et al., 2021). Since there were large main effects of transgender identity and SUD diagnosis on HIV/AIDS and HCV, we ran an additional multivariable logistic regression including an interaction term for transgender identity and SUD diagnosis. The interaction term was included in the HCV model because of its significance but not the HIV/AIDS model. Lastly, we performed sensitivity analyses for the multivariable logistic regression models, running all models with only individuals with discrete gender identity data present to account for lack of gender identity documentation for some individuals in the study (44.6% of individuals lacked gender identity documentation in the discrete gender identity fields). All analyses were performed in Stata Version 17.0. The Santa Clara Valley Medical Center Institutional Review Board approved this study.

Results

A total of 176,197 individuals were included in the study. 1,123 individuals (0.6%) were identified as transgender. 46,043 individuals (26.1%) were incarcerated in the SCC jail during the study period. The study population was majority Asian (26%), Hispanic (34%) and non-Hispanic White (21%). The median age of the population was 46. Demographic information for individuals in the study is shown in Table 1. A total of 1.4% of the population had three or more of the outcome diagnoses, 6.0% had two diagnoses, and 19.8% had one diagnosis. Diabetes was the most commonly diagnosed medical condition, affecting 23,034 individuals (13.1%), followed by substance use disorder, affecting 12,970 individuals (7.4%). The least common diagnosis was HIV/AIDS, affecting 637 individuals (0.4%).

Individuals experiencing recent incarceration

Overall, the group experiencing recent incarceration were younger than those not experiencing recent incarceration (median age = 33 vs 53) and disproportionately Hispanic (39.4% vs 32.4%) and non-Hispanic Black (9.2% vs 3.8%). Transgender individuals accounted for 0.6% of the population that were incarcerated during the study period. Age and race/ethnicity were not significantly different between the transgender and cisgender groups. Transgender individuals were more likely than cisgender individuals to be unhoused (26.2% vs. 9.9%).

For individuals who were incarcerated during the study period, SUD was the most commonly identified medical condition for both cisgender and transgender individuals. 17.7% of cisgender and 31.9% of transgender patients had a SUD diagnosis. Proportions of diagnosis of each medical or mental health condition within the group experiencing recent incarceration are shown in Figure 1.

In unadjusted models, transgender patients were more likely to have diagnoses of SUD (odds ratio [OR] = 2.2, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.7–2.8), HIV/AIDS (OR = 17.4, 95% CI = 11.3–26.7), HCV (OR = 1.9, 95% CI = 1.1–3.4), and bipolar disorder (OR = 1.8, 95% CI = 1.1–2.9). After adjustment, transgender individuals remained significantly more likely to be diagnosed with SUD (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 1.6, 95% CI = 1.2–2.2), HIV/AIDS (AOR = 17.4, 95% CI = 10.2–25.2), and HCV (AOR = 3.4, 95% CI = 1.5–7.8); other diagnoses were no longer significantly different. Results of the multivariable logistic regression models assessing the likelihood of diagnosis for transgender individuals compared to cisgender individuals are shown in Table 2.

We performed a sensitivity analysis rerunning the above models with only individuals who had documentation in the discrete gender identity field in the EMR (n = 25,767). The results of the multivariable logistic regression showed that transgender individuals were still significantly more likely to be diagnosed with HIV/AIDS (AOR = 15.3; 95% CI = 9.4–24.8). However, there was no significant relationship found between gender identity and diagnosis of HCV (AOR = 1.3; 95% CI = 0.7–2.4) nor SUD (AOR = 1.3, 95% CI = 1.0–1.7), nor any of the other medical conditions assessed.

Individuals not experiencing recent incarceration

For those who were not incarcerated in the SCC jail during the study period, transgender individuals were younger than cisgender individuals (median age of 29 vs. 53) and more likely to identify as non-Hispanic White (33.1% vs. 21.3%). Transgender individuals were also more likely to be assigned a male sex at birth (48.5% vs. 41.9%) and were more likely to be unhoused (9.1% vs. 2.5%). Demographic information and significant differences within the subgroup are shown in Table 1.

Diabetes was the most commonly diagnosed medical condition among cisgender individuals who were not incarcerated during the study period, affecting 16.6% of the population. MDD was the most common condition affecting transgender individuals in the subgroup, affecting 12.2% of the population. Proportions of diagnosis of each medical condition within the group not experiencing recent incarceration are shown in Figure 2.

In unadjusted analyses, as compared to cisgender individuals, transgender patients were less likely to have diabetes (OR = 0.6, 95% CI = 0.5–0.7) and cardiovascular disease (OR 0.6, 95% CI = 0.4–0.8) and more likely to have a seizure disorder (OR 2.1, 95% CI = 1.4–3.0), SUD (OR = 2.0, 95% CI = 1.5–2.6), MDD (OR = 4.2, 95% CI = 3.4–5.2), bipolar disorder (OR 3.4, 95% CI = 2.5–4.7), and HIV/AIDS (OR = 9.2, 95% CI = 5.7–14.6). After adjustment, transgender individuals remained more likely to be diagnosed with HIV/AIDS (AOR = 9.2, 95% CI = 4.5–12.1), seizure disorder (AOR = 1.5, 95% CI= 1.0–2.2), MDD (AOR = 3.7, 95% CI = 3.0–4.6), and bipolar disorder (AOR = 2.4, 95% CI = 1.7–3.3). Results of the unadjusted and adjusted multivariable logistic regressions assessing the likelihood of diagnosis for transgender individuals compared to cisgender individuals are shown in Table 2.

We performed a sensitivity analysis rerunning the above models with only individuals who had documentation in the discrete gender identity field in the EMR (n = 71,850). The results of the multivariable logistic regression showed that transgender individuals were significantly less likely to be diagnosed with diabetes (AOR = 0.7; 95% CI: 0.6–0.9). The regression also showed transgender individuals were still significantly more likely to be diagnosed with HIV/AIDS (AOR = 8.1; 95% CI: 4.7–14.1), MDD (AOR = 2.9; 95% CI: 2.3–3.6) and bipolar disorder (AOR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.4–2.7). However, there was no significant relationship found between gender identity and diagnosis of SUD (AOR = 0.9; 95% CI: 0.7–1.3).

Discussion

Healthcare and incarceration are uniquely interwoven (Weidner & Schultz, 2019). Transgender individuals disproportionately experience discrimination in the healthcare system (Kcomt, 2019) and abuse in the criminal legal system (Baćak, 2023). Persistent anti-transgender discrimination and internalized transphobia are positively correlated with adverse mental health outcomes (Scandurra et al., 2018). Mental illness is associated with both an increased likelihood of incarceration (Hall et al., 2019) and poor health outcomes (Yehia et al., 2015). This cyclical relationship between discrimination, incarceration, and worsening mental and medical health outcomes is investigated in our study.

Our study demonstrates that transgender and cisgender individuals are affected differently by certain medical conditions, and these health disparities further vary based on recent experiences of incarceration. Among the population experiencing recent incarceration, rates of every condition evaluated in the study were higher in the transgender population (excluding heart disease). Among the population that did not experience recent incarceration, the results differed. Transgender individuals had higher rates of only HIV/AIDS, seizure disorder, major depression, bipolar disorder, and SUD. This highlights the importance of considering recent incarceration history as a potentially influential factor in transgender people’s health.

The results of our analysis showed that transgender individuals were more likely to be diagnosed with HIV infection when compared to their cisgender peers, regardless of recent incarceration experience. This is consistent with existing literature that showed rates of HIV infection are higher among transgender individuals than cisgender individuals (Stutterheim et al., 2021). The proportion of transgender individuals diagnosed with HIV more than quadruples among the group experiencing recent incarceration as compared to those not experiencing recent incarceration (from 2.2% to 9.5%), while the AOR more than doubles (from 7.4 to 16.0). More research is needed to understand the role of recent incarceration in HIV related health disparities among transgender populations. These findings also highlight the need for culturally competent interventions along the HIV care continuum.

Of those individuals with HIV diagnoses in our study, 5.3% were among transgender individuals, which is higher than the California state-level estimate of 1.6% of transgender individuals having a diagnosis of HIV. In our study, 23.4% of transgender individuals experienced recent incarceration. Incarceration is associated with higher rates of HIV (Widra, 2023) and sexual contact with recently incarcerated individuals increases the likelihood of HIV diagnosis (Khan et al., 2011), possibly contributing to this difference. Additionally, these differing results may be influenced by challenges in diagnosing HIV infection and accurately documenting gender identity data, and underscores the need for improved gender identity data collection. This also highlights the importance of providing comprehensive HIV prevention and treatment in carceral settings to stem the spread of HIV among transgender individuals and their partners (United Nations Office on Drugs & Crime, 2020).

Transgender individuals recently experiencing incarceration were significantly more likely than their cisgender peers to be diagnosed with HCV while transgender individuals not recently experiencing incarceration were not more likely than their cisgender peers to be diagnosed with HCV. This could be due to the disproportionate effect of SUD on transgender individuals recently experiencing incarceration as compared to cisgender individuals we found in our study, which would be supported by the significant interaction between transgender identity and SUD on HCV diagnosis.

SUD had the highest prevalence of all conditions included in this analysis for transgender individuals regardless of justice involvement. However, only transgender individuals recently experiencing incarceration were significantly more likely to be diagnosed with SUD as compared to their cisgender counterparts not recently experiencing incarceration. This disparity may in part be due to the combined effects of discrimination due to history of incarceration (Feingold, 2021) and discrimination against transgender identity leading to higher rates of SUD (Connolly & Gilchrist, 2020; Wolfe et al., 2021). Additionally, the rate of homelessness among transgender individuals was disproportionately higher than cisgender individuals among the population recently experiencing incarceration (26.2% vs 9.9%, OR = 3.2) as well as among individuals not recently experiencing incarceration (9.1% vs 2.5%, OR = 3.9). Homelessness places people at a greater risk for SUD (Keuroghlian et al., 2014) and may be influencing the increased likelihood of transgender individuals being diagnosed with SUD. A survey conducted in nearby San Francisco found that among unhoused individuals, transgender individuals were not significantly different in reporting drug or alcohol abuse than cisgender individuals (Flentje et al., 2016).

Given the significantly higher rates of SUD among transgender populations, careful attention should be placed on ensuring screening tools for SUD in correctional settings are accurately capturing gender identity data. Additionally, medication-assisted therapies (MAT) for opiate use disorder and alcohol use disorder should be offered in correctional facilities and should be culturally competent to ensure successful support for transgender individuals experiencing SUD (Oberheim et al., 2017). Experiences of discrimination in SUD treatment programs may hinder recovery (Lombardi, 2007). Cognitive-behavioral based therapies can be tailored to transgender populations and have been shown to result in significant reductions in drug abuse screening scores (Empson et al., 2017); however, a systematic review showed few interventions have been tested in order to show effective reductions in SUD for transgender individuals (Glynn & van den Berg, 2017). Attention should be given to continuing treatment after discharge from jail, and should meet the individual where they are likely to seek care. Clinics serving homeless individuals, for example, may be a primary source of care for individuals leaving correctional settings (Walsh et al., 2023) given the higher rates of homelessness among justice-involved individuals (Couloute, 2018), and the disproportionate homelessness among transgender individuals (Fraser et al., 2019).

Notably, after limiting the dataset to just individuals with discrete gender identity documentation, transgender individuals experiencing recent incarceration were no longer more likely to be diagnosed with SUD. This may be explained by the smaller sample size and underscores the need for improved standardization of gender identity data collection. Because of possible discrepancies in the level of care engagement and systemic training for gender identity data documentation in the ambulatory setting where individuals not recently experiencing incarceration accessed care, this population may be more likely to have discrete gender identity documentation as opposed to the population recently experiencing incarceration.

Among mental health diagnoses, transgender individuals that did not experience recent incarceration were significantly more likely to be diagnosed with MDD and bipolar disorder compared to cisgender peers; although MDD was more likely in transgender individuals recently experiencing incarceration, the finding did not reach statistical significance in this group. The non-significance in this population may be due to differences in care seeking behavior and lack of appropriate diagnosis, as MDD is infrequently and inconsistently identified by jail personnel (Kaba et al., 2015; Teplin, 1990). This lack of diagnosis exists although there are screening tools to detect psychiatric diagnoses in correctional settings (Boothby & Durham, 1999; Martin et al., 2013; Swartz & Lurigio, 2005).

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the retrospective identification of transgender individuals, particularly among individuals who had been incarcerated. Given the disproportionate rates of incarceration for transgender individuals (Grant et al., 2011; James et al., 2016; Reisner et al., 2014), we would expect a higher percent of those incarcerated to identify as transgender than among the general population. However, only 0.57% of individuals incarcerated during the study period were identified as transgender compared with 0.66% of individuals in the outpatient medical visit population. Because of the nature of the jail setting, transgender individuals may not wish to disclose their gender identity due to privacy concerns, medical mistrust, or fear of mistreatment and punitive housing assignment (Brömdal et al., 2023). Further, this study is limited by challenges in incarceration data and does not account for incarcerations in jurisdictions other than SCC or that occurred outside of the study period. Individuals included in the study as a result of an outpatient medical visit may have experienced incarceration in the years prior to the study period or in another jurisdiction that are not accounted for in this dataset.

An additional limitation of this study is the possibility of missed diagnoses of medical conditions, especially among individuals recently experiencing incarceration. While medical screenings are provided to all individuals entering the jail and outside records can be incorporated into the patient’s chart, it is possible that some individuals may be lacking diagnostic information, either due to short length of stay in custody where they may not see a medical provider to update the EHR, due to privacy concerns resulting in the withholding of medical information, or other factors which may cause an individual not to self-report diagnosis information. Individuals included in the study as a result of completing an outpatient medical visit may have higher rates of certain chronic conditions than the community as a whole as those without chronic medical conditions may not routinely seek outpatient care and as a result would not be included in our analysis. Lastly, privately operated health systems in SCC are not included in the study. Still, SCVMC serviced roughly 11.7% of the SCC adult population during the study period (United States Census Bureau, 2023). SCVMC is also a safety net healthcare provider that provides services regardless of ability to pay. Thus, individuals seeking primary care in the study population may be from under-resourced communities who typically have an additional burden of chronic disease (Braveman et al., 2010) compared to individuals accessing care at privately operated facilities. This would potentially imply the differences imposed by recent incarceration in the study results may be greater if incorporating privately insured individuals.

Conclusion

Both transgender individuals and recently incarcerated individuals experience increased burden of disease compared with cisgender individuals and those who have not recently experienced incarceration. However, prior research has not highlighted the impact of rates of chronic disease for transgender individuals based on recent incarceration. Our results show that transgender individuals who recently experienced incarceration are associated with a higher prevalence of disease compared to cisgender individuals who recently experienced incarceration and transgender individuals who did not. In practice, this demonstrates the need for medical providers to provide trans-competent care and to be aware of one’s experience of recent incarceration, as the individual may be at higher risk for some medical conditions, especially SUD, HIV, and HCV. Additionally, proper identification of gender identity in carceral settings will allow for tailored screening for these chronic conditions.
